# Shape synths, voice harmonies, split stems, paint vibes

All five features can be built from open-source code that a GPL-3.0-or-later app may reuse, and none of them needs a cloud service. **The synth** should copy the reface CS's 17-control panel in behaviour (five oscillator types whose two shared knobs, Texture and Mod, change meaning per type; one resonant low-pass; one envelope shared by loudness and brightness; a glide slider whose bottom position means "polyphonic") and then add what the hardware lacks for a beginner: presets in 11 categories, six named macro knobs, velocity, key tracking, a reverb slot and seven ways of playing (sustain, arpeggio, strum, pulse, drone, glide, humanise). Anti-aliased oscillators and a zero-delay filter in one AudioWorklet per track are cheap enough in plain TypeScript: the heaviest test voice cost about 0.24 microseconds per sample in the researchers' Node benchmark, room for about 44 such voices at half the audio budget. **The keys** should be a cut-down Salamander Grand (30 notes, 4 velocity layers, trimmed to 8–10 s, Opus) of about 15–18 MB downloaded once, with electric piano and organ synthesised or small enough to ship inside the app; decoded memory (about 330–390 MB), not download size, is the real limit. **Harmonizing** reduces to named, testable music-theory rules (diatonic thirds and sixths that check the chord on strong beats, species-counterpoint rules for a counter-melody, an avoid-note test for colour tones) plus a best-path search that picks piano voicings that move least, and permissively licensed code from Contrapunk, tonal.js, jazzband and music21 can be ported. **Stems** should come from HT-Demucs v4 (about 9 dB SDR, 81 MB in half precision) run in the Windows app's Rust backend through ONNX Runtime, GPU first and CPU as fallback, downloaded only when the user asks; a 3-minute song should take roughly as long as it plays on a 4-core laptop CPU and well under a minute on a GPU, while cheap non-neural splits paint in the meantime. **The painting** should give every layer its own family of marks in a fixed depth order, let the music set how much of the canvas is covered (about 11% for one quiet part, 67% for six loud ones, never more than 78%), choose among seven immersive layouts, and derive every visual choice from a roughly 74-number "vibe fingerprint" through smooth, seeded functions, so that songs with similar elements look alike and the same file always repaints the same way. A short list of sources must stay out entirely (non-commercial weights and samples, Vital's factory presets, AGPL code), and several more are formula references only. The limits are real: Yamaha's, Hugging Face's and most journal sites were blocked, nothing was listened to or painted, and every speed figure was measured in Node on a cloud server or taken from someone else's hardware, not timed on your PC.

## Four labels separate source facts from sums and choices

Every factual statement below carries a label, because the difference is the difference between something to build on and something to test by ear or eye. **[Read]** means a researcher read it on 18 November 2026 in the linked source file, licence, specification or data file. **[Computed]** means a researcher worked it out: arithmetic on sourced constants, decoding preset files, a script, or a measurement on a cloud machine. **[Design]** means a recommendation or an invented value that no source states; it must be tuned. **[Secondary]** means a search-result summary, a page that could not be opened, or general knowledge. Licence verdicts use five phrases: **Port** (may be copied or translated into GPL-3.0-or-later code, keeping its notice), **Port, pins v3** (GPL-3.0 code whose "or later" wording was not seen; porting it would let the combined app be distributed under version 3 only, so re-implementing from its equations is preferable), **Data + credit** (samples or model weights shipped or downloaded as separate data with attribution), **Formula only**, and **Out**. The licence judgements follow standard free-software practice and are not legal advice. Products and companies are named; no people, bands, songs or record labels are.

## The synth: keep the reface CS's 17 controls and add a beginner layer on top

**In plain words.** The reface CS is easy to love because every sound sits a few slider moves away on one flat panel, with no menus. M-Theory should rebuild that panel and its behaviour faithfully, with the best open algorithms underneath, and then put a thin beginner layer over it: pick a category, pick a preset, turn one of six named macro knobs, choose how it plays (held, arpeggiated, strummed, pulsed, as a drone, gliding), and watch the panel's sliders move so the player learns what each one does. It will not be a clone of the hardware's exact sound: no one had a recording of a reface CS, and Yamaha's manual and data list were unreachable, so every curve below is a choice to tune by ear. Sound quality rests on three decisions: oscillators that do not alias, a filter that stays stable when it screams, and a slow random drift per voice. Everything runs inside one AudioWorklet per synth track, and the same code renders WAV exports, so an export sounds exactly like playback.

### Seventeen values make every reface CS sound

**[Read]** The reface CS's tone-generator block is 22 bytes holding 17 front-panel values plus a volume set only over MIDI: LFO assign, depth and speed; portamento; oscillator type; Texture; Mod; cutoff; resonance; EG balance; attack, decay, sustain and release; effect type, depth and rate, all 0–127 ([reface-cs-live RefaceProtocol.kt, MIT](https://github.com/harpomaxx/reface-cs-live/blob/main/app/src/main/java/com/refacecslive/protocol/RefaceProtocol.kt)). **[Read]** Five-way selectors read in zones 0–25, 26–51, 52–76, 77–102 and 103–127, and a portamento value of 0 means polyphonic while 1–127 means mono with that glide time ([pencilresearch reface CS map](https://github.com/pencilresearch/midi/blob/main/Yamaha/reface%20CS.csv)). **[Read]** The hardware has eight-note polyphony, no patch memory (sounds are stored only through an app or web service) and no key-tracking or envelope-amount control ([cs-control README, GPL-3.0](https://github.com/cfenell/cs-control/blob/master/README.md); [refaceCS help text](https://github.com/sterudo/refaceCS/blob/main/src/help-content.js)); **[Secondary]** reviewers add that the engine ignores velocity, that one effect at a time is limiting, and that the saw-plus-sub bass is strong ([Synthtopia review](https://www.synthtopia.com/content/2015/09/22/yamaha-reface-cs-review-a-powerful-menu-free-minisynth/); [Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/yamaha-reface-series)). **[Read]** A stored patch is a 61-byte .syx file with a 7-bit checksum, so M-Theory can save each sound as the same 17 numbers and export it to a real reface CS for owners of the hardware ([reface-cs-live](https://github.com/harpomaxx/reface-cs-live/blob/main/app/src/main/java/com/refacecslive/protocol/RefaceProtocol.kt)).

### Five oscillators, each made alias-free its own way

**[Read]** The cheap per-voice fix for aliasing is the two-sample PolyBLEP: near each jump the naive saw or pulse is corrected by `−(t/dt − 1)²` just after the wrap and `((t−1)/dt + 1)²` just before it, and a variable-width pulse built this way is free of DC by construction; a matching PolyBLAMP fixes triangle corners, and the reference generator falls back to a pure sine above a quarter of the sample rate ([PolyBLEP.cpp, zlib](https://github.com/martinfinke/PolyBLEP/blob/master/PolyBLEP.cpp)). **[Read]** The stronger fix, a minimum-phase band-limited step table (minBLEP) with 16 zero crossings oversampled 16 times, is inserted into a 32-sample ring buffer and handles hard sync exactly, with a 20 Hz DC blocker on the output ([VCV Fundamental VCO.cpp, GPL-3.0-or-later](https://github.com/VCVRack/Fundamental/blob/v2/src/VCO.cpp); [Rack minblep.cpp](https://github.com/VCVRack/Rack/blob/v2/src/dsp/minblep.cpp)). **[Design]** Use PolyBLEP everywhere except hard sync, which gets the minBLEP buffer. That split rests on the researchers' judgement that a two-sample correction aliases audibly at high slave ratios; **no alias measurement in decibels was made**.

**Multi-saw.** **[Read]** The supersaw in the Deluge firmware (GPL-3.0-or-later) uses seven saws at offsets of 0, −0.11002313, +0.10745242, −0.06288439, +0.06216538, −0.01952356 and +0.01991221 of the note frequency at full spread, a centre gain of 0.99785 − 0.55366·s and a side gain of (1.2841 − 0.73764·s)·s + 0.044372, normalised so the level stays constant, with a maximum spread of 0.6; the file cites a 2010 thesis that measured the original JP-8000 ([supersaw.cpp](https://github.com/SynthstromAudible/DelugeFirmware/blob/HEAD/src/deluge/dsp/oscillators/supersaw.cpp)). **[Read]** An unlicensed SuperCollider version of the same analysis adds free-running random start phases, a high-pass filter at the played fundamental and the thesis's eleventh-order detune curve ([supersaw gist](https://gist.github.com/audionerd/fe50790b7601cba65ddd855caffb05ad)). **[Computed]** That curve is gentle for most of its travel and steep at the end: the outer saws sit about ±18 cents apart at half travel, ±46 cents at three-quarters and −202/+177 cents at full. **[Design]** Mod at 0 gives one saw; above 0 it gives seven saws with spread 0.6·Mod^1.5, which **[Computed]** puts the outer saws about ±114 cents out at full travel and ±38 cents at a spread of 0.2. Texture sets the level of a sub-oscillator one octave down, as the manual describes.

**Pulse, Sync, Ring and FM.** **[Design]** Pulse uses two PolyBLEP pulses: Mod sets the width of both from 50% to 95%, and Texture detunes the second from 0 to +30 cents over its first 40% of travel, then steps through −12, −5, +7, +12, +19 and +24 semitones with a short crossfade. Sync uses a silent master at the note's pitch and a hard-synced slave offset by 36·Texture semitones, with Mod setting how far the shared envelope sweeps the slave (up to +48 semitones). Ring multiplies two oscillators, OSC1 at the note times 2^(2·Texture) and OSC2 at a ratio from Mod that snaps to 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3 and 4 with inharmonic values in between (×2.76 for bells), plus 20% of OSC1 so the pitch stays readable; **[Computed]** the product of two clean saws still contains sum frequencies above the Nyquist limit, so ring-modulate a sine or triangle carrier or render that voice at twice the rate. **[Read]** FM is two-operator phase modulation as in the Apache-2.0 engine inside Dexed, whose self-feedback averages the last two outputs to stay stable ([fm_op_kernel.cc](https://github.com/asb2m10/dexed/blob/master/source/msfa/fm_op_kernel.cc)), with a polynomial sine whose maximum error is 6.5 millionths (total harmonic distortion −103.7 dB) ([VCO.cpp](https://github.com/VCVRack/Fundamental/blob/v2/src/VCO.cpp)). **[Design]** The FM index is 10·Texture² radians and Mod picks the modulator ratio from 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8, with narrow detuned zones between them for metallic tones. **[Computed]** By Carson's rule an index of 5 on a 4 kHz modulator spreads ±24 kHz and folds back at 48 kHz, so oversample by two only when index times modulator frequency exceeds a quarter of the sample rate.

### A zero-delay filter, one shared envelope and analogue drift

**[Read]** The default 12 dB filter in open code is the Cytomic state-variable filter: `g = tan(π·fc/fs)`, `k = 2 − 2·res` with resonance capped at 0.98, about nine multiplies a sample, low-, band- and high-pass from the same core; the file is usable under MIT ([sst-filters CytomicSVF.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/sst-filters/blob/main/include/sst/filters/CytomicSVF.h)). **[Read]** The OB-Xd/OB-Xf 4-pole solves its feedback in closed form with `R24 = 3.5·resonance`, soft-clips only the first stage with one arctangent, and scales the output by 0.6 ([OBXDFilter.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/sst-filters/blob/main/include/sst/filters/OBXDFilter.h)). **[Read]** The nonlinear transistor-ladder model in the same library spends five tanh calls a sample and its comment asks for "an oversampling factor of two (preferably higher)" ([VintageLadders.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/sst-filters/blob/main/include/sst/filters/VintageLadders.h)). **[Design]** Show only Cutoff and Resonance on the panel, as the hardware does, and offer three characters under "More": Classic 24 dB (the OB-Xf-style 4-pole, default), Smooth 12 dB (the Cytomic filter, which also gives band- and high-pass) and Vintage (the ladder at 2× for mono leads). Compute cutoff as base + envelope + LFO + key tracking × (note − 60) in semitones at a control rate of every 8–16 samples; **[Computed]** recalculating the tangent every sample raised a light voice's cost from about 8.3 to 20 ns per sample. Hold the passband steady as resonance rises by subtracting 0.5–0.666 of the input from the feedback, the range the ladder codes use ([VintageLadders.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/sst-filters/blob/main/include/sst/filters/VintageLadders.h)).

**[Read]** The OB-Xf envelope charges toward 1.3 and ends its attack above 0.9, which gives the fast-topped analogue curve, and computes its release coefficient from the level at note-off so release time does not depend on level ([AdsrEnvelope.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/OB-Xf/blob/main/src/engine/AdsrEnvelope.h)); its glide is an RC one-pole on pitch in semitones and its LFO output is smoothed at 250 Hz to stop clicks ([VoiceControlQuad.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/OB-Xf/blob/main/src/engine/VoiceControlQuad.h); [Lfo.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/OB-Xf/blob/main/src/engine/Lfo.h)). **[Design]** Keep one shared envelope with EG Balance as on the hardware, set decay and release so the knob time is the −80 dB point (time constant = time ÷ 9.21), restart a stolen or re-struck voice from its current level, retrigger in mono and keep running in legato, and offer a separate filter decay under "More", because a single envelope cannot make a pluck with a long tail but a short filter "blip". **[Read]** OB-Xf steals the oldest voice and gives stolen notes back their voices on key release, while Tone.js's PolySynth simply drops notes beyond 32 voices with "Max polyphony exceeded. Note dropped." ([VoiceQueue.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/OB-Xf/blob/main/src/engine/VoiceQueue.h); [PolySynth.ts](https://github.com/Tonejs/Tone.js/blob/main/Tone/instrument/PolySynth.ts)), so the synth should not be built on Tone.js. **[Design]** Default to 8 voices (16 for pads) with a 5–15 ms fade on a stolen voice.

**[Read]** Analogue drift in Surge is a per-voice random walk, `last = last·(1 − 10⁻⁵) + uniform(−1, 1)·10⁻⁵`, scaled by 316.2, and OB-Xf adds per-voice "slop" (fixed random offsets of up to ±0.5 units on level, envelope times, cutoff and glide) ([OscillatorDriftUnisonCharacter.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/sst-basic-blocks/blob/main/include/sst/basic-blocks/dsp/OscillatorDriftUnisonCharacter.h); [Voice.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/OB-Xf/blob/main/src/engine/Voice.h)). **[Computed]** The drift's corner is about 0.076 Hz at 48 kHz, a slow wander. **[Design]** Seed every random source (drift, slop, supersaw phases, noise) per voice so that a render is reproducible.

### Effects: the reface slot inside the worklet, plus a reverb send

**[Read]** The Web Audio specification clamps a DelayNode inside a feedback loop to at least one render quantum, and leaves the oversampling filters of the WaveShaperNode unspecified ([Web Audio spec](https://github.com/WebAudio/web-audio-api/blob/main/index.bs)). **[Computed]** At 48 kHz that floor is 2.67 ms, longer than a flanger's shortest delays. **[Design]** So all four reface effects belong inside the synth's worklet, where they behave identically in every browser and in offline export, and the reverb stays a native ConvolverNode on a send. Distortion: Depth is drive from 0 to 36 dB plus mix, Rate is a tone low-pass from 800 Hz to 12 kHz, and the curve is a first-order antiderivative-antialiased tanh (**[Computed]** antiderivative `|x| + log1p(e^(−2|x|)) − ln 2`, falling back to `tanh((x + x_prev)/2)` when consecutive samples are nearly equal), re-derived rather than copied from the GPL-3.0 chowdsp module ([chowdsp ADAA waveshaper](https://github.com/Chowdhury-DSP/chowdsp_utils/blob/main/modules/dsp/chowdsp_waveshapers/Waveshapers/chowdsp_ADAAWaveshaper.h)). Chorus/Flanger: below half Depth a chorus of 7–25 ms delay with up to 5 ms of modulation, above half a crossfade into a flanger of 1–5 ms with feedback up to 0.7, Rate 0.05–8 Hz; the hardware's own switching rule is unknown. Phaser: 4–8 all-pass stages, Rate 0.03–6 Hz, Depth sets sweep and feedback. Delay: 30 ms to 1.2 s or tempo values from a sixteenth to a half note, feedback 0.85 × Depth. **[Read]** Usable starting constants exist in Odin 2 (flanger base 10.5 ms ± 9.5 ms with feedback capped at ±0.98; phaser 0.25 Hz around 4 kHz with feedback × 0.97; an 80 Hz high-pass inside the delay loop) and in the measured Juno-60 chorus already gathered for the app's vocal chain (0.513 Hz and 0.863 Hz sweeping 1.54–5.15 ms) ([Odin 2 Flanger.h](https://github.com/TheWaveWarden/odin2/blob/master/Source/audio/FX/Flanger.h); [Phaser.h](https://github.com/TheWaveWarden/odin2/blob/master/Source/audio/FX/Phaser.h); [Delay.cpp](https://github.com/TheWaveWarden/odin2/blob/master/Source/audio/FX/Delay.cpp); [junox chorus.js](https://github.com/pendragon-andyh/junox/blob/master/src/junox/chorus.js)). **[Read]** Where 2× oversampling is needed (filter drive, ring, high-index FM), the same HIIR half-band coefficients the vocal chain uses reach 80.6 dB of rejection at 48 kHz for about 46 µs of round-trip delay ([HIIR designer](https://github.com/unevens/hiir/blob/master/hiir/PolyphaseIir2Designer.h)).

### Presets, macros, drones and playing modes: the beginner layer

**[Read]** Open synths sort their factory sounds into nearly the same categories: Surge XT's (GPL-3.0) include Basses 59, Leads 131, Pads 65, Plucks 111 and a Templates folder of "Init" sounds per oscillator type; Helm's (presets CC BY 4.0) include Bass 51, Keys 43, Lead 37 and Pad 40; Odin 2 adds Drones 14 and Atmospheres 24 ([Surge patches](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/surge/tree/main/resources/data/patches_factory); [Helm presets and licence](https://github.com/mtytel/helm/tree/master/patches/Factory%20Presets); [Odin 2 presets](https://github.com/TheWaveWarden/odin2/tree/master/assets/Soundbanks/Factory%20Presets)). **[Read]** Vital's presets carry a separate licence that "does not allow redistribution" and must not be used ([Vital README](https://github.com/mtytel/vital/blob/main/README.md)). **[Computed]** The researchers decoded dozens of Surge and Helm factory patches into seconds, hertz and semitones, and the same recipes recur: plucks have zero sustain and a decaying filter envelope; brass has a fast filter "blat", full sustain and velocity to brightness; pads have slow attacks with detuned saws or pulse-width modulation and chorus; bells are inharmonic FM or ring modulation with zero sustain; acid basses have high resonance, a decaying filter envelope and mono glide. **[Design]** Ship 11 categories (Bass, Lead, Pad, Keys, Pluck, Brass, Strings, Bells, Drone, FX, Arp) with two to four presets each plus an "Init" per oscillator type, each preset shown as a name, a one-line description and the 17 panel values. **Macros.** **[Read]** Vital has 4 macros and Surge XT 8 ([Vital synth_constants.h](https://github.com/mtytel/vital/blob/main/src/common/synth_constants.h); [Surge ModulationSource.h](https://github.com/surge-synthesizer/surge/blob/main/src/common/ModulationSource.h)); an unlicensed reface web editor has a "SuperKnob" that drives several parameters through per-parameter curves (idea only) ([refaceCS help text](https://github.com/sterudo/refaceCS/blob/main/src/help-content.js)); **[Secondary]** one commercial preset player names its four macros Brightness, Timbre, Time and Movement ([KVR thread](https://www.kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=8296634)). **[Design]** Six fixed macros, stored per preset as lists of target, start, end and curve: **Brightness** (cutoff +0 to +60 on an exponential curve, resonance −10 to 0 to hold loudness, EG balance toward the filter +0 to +20), **Motion** (LFO depth 0–80, speed 20–90, drift 0–8 cents), **Space** (effect depth 0–80 plus reverb send 0–60%), **Grit** (drive 0–90, Texture +0 to +30, resonance +0 to +20), **Width** (Multi-saw spread +0 to +40, voice stereo spread 0–100%, chorus depth) and **Shape** (attack 0–90 and release 10–110 together). The macros sit above the panel and the panel's sliders visibly move with them.

**Drones.** **[Read]** A GPL-3.0 Web Audio drone machine uses four voices detuned by −1.4, +0.8, −2.1 and +1.6 cents, panned −0.6, −0.2, +0.25 and +0.6, with gains 0.22, 0.18, 0.16 and 0.14, a shared 4 kHz low-pass smoothed with a 0.12 s time constant, and an "Evolve" mode that random-walks its controls so it never repeats ([dronepigreco drone.js](https://github.com/Kalitragus/dronepigreco/blob/HEAD/drone.js); [README](https://github.com/Kalitragus/dronepigreco/blob/HEAD/README.md)). **[Design]** M-Theory's Drone mode holds the key's tonic (or each chord's root) as root, fifth and octave (optional sub-octave), with layer gains falling upward as above, a 300–800 ms glide between roots at chord changes, and a random walk on each layer every 250 ms smoothed over about 2 s.

**Ways of playing.** **[Read]** Odin 2's arpeggiator (GPL-3.0) offers Up, Down, Up-and-Down, Down-and-Up, Random and four "Crawl" patterns, where a window as long as the chord slides one note at a time through the extended chord (C E G, then E G C′, G C′ E′, C′ E′ G′), with two octaves and 50% gate as defaults ([OdinArpeggiator.cpp](https://github.com/TheWaveWarden/odin2/blob/master/Source/gui/OdinArpeggiator.cpp)). **[Design]** Because M-Theory's chords and melodies are already written on a grid, the useful modes are transformations of written notes: **Sustain** (as written); **Arp** (Up, Down, Up-Down, Down-Up, Random, Crawl Up/Down, As Written; quarter to thirty-second notes with triplets; 1–3 octaves; gate 10–100%; restart at each chord; the song's swing); **Strum** (down, up or alternating, 10–80 ms between notes, velocity taper 0–30%); **Pulse** (retrigger the chord on eighths, dotted quarters or off-beats); **Drone**; **Glide** for melodies (mono, glide only when notes overlap, 20–300 ms); and **Humanise** (timing ±0–20 ms, velocity ±0–15%, cutoff ±0–6 semitones and pitch ±0–5 cents per note). Chord memory and latch matter on a live keyboard but are redundant on a grid; a "tie chords" option covers the useful part.

### One worklet per track is fast enough in plain TypeScript

**[Computed]** The researchers benchmarked synth voices in Node 22 (the same V8 engine as Chrome and WebView2) on one 2.3 GHz cloud CPU core, in 128-sample blocks at 48 kHz. A light voice (two PolyBLEP saws, soft clip, Cytomic filter, envelope) cost 8.3–8.6 ns per sample in TypeScript, 8.3 ns in scalar WebAssembly and 5.2 ns in WebAssembly SIMD running four voices at once. A seven-saw supersaw through the filter cost 16.7–17.0 ns in TypeScript and 9.5 ns in SIMD. A deliberately heavy voice (supersaw plus pulse, per-sample vibrato, a 2×-oversampled nonlinear ladder with five tanh calls per sub-sample, two envelopes, stereo) cost 236–238 ns, which is 1.13% of the 2.67 ms block per voice, or about 44 voices at half the budget; moving pitch, pulse width and cutoff to a 16-sample control rate cut it to 102 ns, about 100 voices. **[Design]** Allowing for a laptop two to three times slower under power saving and for the other worklets (vocal chain, drums), plain TypeScript still carries 16 voices with unison and an oversampled filter; WebAssembly is not needed for speed, only perhaps to avoid just-in-time-compiler hiccups on the first notes, which pre-rendering a few silent blocks at start-up also avoids.

**[Read]** An OfflineAudioContext has its own `audioWorklet`, so the same processor module can be loaded into it for WAV export ([Web Audio spec](https://github.com/WebAudio/web-audio-api/blob/main/index.bs)); M-Theory already renders with OfflineAudioContext in `src/audio/synth.ts` and already loads a worklet in `src/listen/live.ts`. **[Read]** Message ports allocate memory and trigger garbage collection while SharedArrayBuffers do not, and many voices in one worklet beat many worklets ([karplus-stress-tester README](https://github.com/jackschaedler/karplus-stress-tester/blob/master/README.md)); SharedArrayBuffer needs cross-origin isolation, which Tauri 2 can switch on with `app.security.headers` ([tauri-utils config.rs](https://github.com/tauri-apps/tauri/blob/dev/crates/tauri-utils/src/config.rs)). **[Design]** One AudioWorkletProcessor per synth track holds the whole voice pool in preallocated typed arrays, allocates nothing inside `process()`, receives timestamped note events over its MessagePort ahead of time, splits each 128-sample block at event boundaries for sample-accurate notes, smooths every parameter over 5–20 ms, and runs the track's effect in the same processor. Shared memory is used only for meters, and only where cross-origin isolation is on. Because the synth lives in the web view, the browser app and the Windows app sound identical.

## Keys: a 15–18 MB sampled grand plus synthesised electric piano and organ, all offline

**In plain words.** A convincing acoustic piano in a free app has to be recorded samples; the open modelled pianos are cheap to run but, by common judgement, sound synthetic next to a multi-sampled grand. The best open grand, the Salamander Grand, is 748 MB as published, but cut to 30 notes (one every three semitones) × 4 loudness layers × at most 8–10 s and encoded as Opus it is about 12–15 MB, plus 2–3 MB of key-release and string-resonance noises. It downloads once, on first use, and then works offline forever. The real constraint is memory once decoded (about 330–390 MB), so notes should be decoded only as they are needed. Electric piano and organ need no download at all: a tiny open sampled electric piano can ship inside the app, and the organ, its rotating speaker and an FM electric piano are all synthesis with published constants.

### Samples beat open modelled pianos, and one sample every minor third is enough

**[Read]** The Salamander Grand V3 was "Recorded @ 48khz24bit, 16 Velocity layers Sampled in minor thirds", with release noises sampled chromatically and string-resonance releases in three layers, under CC BY 3.0 ([Salamander README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sfzinstruments/SalamanderGrandPiano/master/README.md)). **[Computed]** Its 641 files total 748.4 MB of FLAC, holding 6,595 s of stereo sustain audio that would occupy 2.53 GB of memory if all decoded. **[Read]** Other open grands are the Headroom Piano (a Yamaha C3 grand, CC BY 4.0, 156 MB as FLAC), the public-domain Splendid Grand (an Akai Steinway set with 4 layers, used by the smplr library), and the CC0 Versilian VCSL Steinway B (42 pitches, one every two semitones, 3 layers) and Kawai (38 pitches, mostly minor thirds, 4 layers) ([sfzinstruments catalogue](https://github.com/sfzinstruments/sfzinstruments.github.io/blob/master/data/sfz/instruments.yml); [Splendid README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sfzinstruments/SplendidGrandPiano/master/README.md); [VCSL README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sgossner/VCSL/master/README.md)). **[Read]** The open modelled pianos are a commuted-waveguide piano in Faust (STK licence, MIT-style) and a new Faust port of a 2007 waveguide-and-soundboard model that declares no licence ([piano.dsp](https://github.com/grame-cncm/faust/blob/master-dev/examples/physicalModeling/faust-stk/piano.dsp); [Faust waveguide piano port](https://github.com/grame-cncm/faust/blob/master-dev/examples/generator/oteyPianoV2.dsp)); **[Secondary]** such models are generally judged recognisably synthetic. Neither was compiled or heard.

**[Read]** Chromium repitches an AudioBufferSourceNode by linear interpolation ([audio_buffer_source_handler.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/modules/webaudio/audio_buffer_source_handler.cc)). **[Computed]** On the Salamander middle C, that interpolation error sat 54–61 dB below the signal for shifts of 1 to 6 semitones, so the browser's resampler is not the weak point. **[Computed]** Shifting a note also shifts its decay: the real C4 took 9.2 s to fall 40 dB and the real D♯4 5.85 s, while C4 shifted up three semitones took 7.75 s. The natural difference between neighbouring real notes is as large as the error from shifting by a semitone and a half, which is why minor-third sampling (shifts of at most ±1.5 semitones, a 9% change in decay rate) is safe and six-semitone spacing (19%) starts to show. **[Computed]** The Salamander's layers already carry the dynamics: middle C measured −38.9 LUFS at layer 1, −27.4 at layer 8 and −20.5 at layer 16, and grew brighter with each, so **the layers must never be loudness-normalised one by one**; apply one gain to the whole instrument.

## Harmony: named music-theory rules, a best-path voicing search and a clear keyboard

**In plain words.** "Harmonize this track" should never be a black box. It should be two short menus of named choices, each one a small rule from music theory that can be explained in a sentence and tested: for a new melody, a third above, a third below, a sixth below, follow the chord, against the tune, answer in the gaps, octave, hold one note, or a slow guide-tone line; for a new chord track, the same chords higher, smoother, with added colour, open and wide, jazz-piano shapes, shells, and several re-harmonizations. Every option checks the existing melody note on each strong beat and hides choices that would clash, so the user never gets a clash they did not ask for. Piano voicings are chosen by a step-by-step best-path search that keeps the hands moving as little as possible, inside realistic hand spans and registers, and avoids muddy close intervals low on the keyboard. The keyboard view colours the root, the chord tones and the added colours differently and keeps held notes lit as the chords change.

### A melody harmony is a diatonic third that checks the chord on strong beats

**[Secondary]** Hardware "intelligent" harmonizers move each note by a number of scale steps in a chosen key and mode, so a "third above" is three or four semitones depending on the degree ([Eventide H90 pitch algorithms](https://cdn.eventideaudio.com/manuals/h90/1.5.2/content/algorithms/pitch.html); [Lexicon MPX G2 manual](https://www.manualsdir.com/manuals/167187/lexicon-mpx-g2.html?page=101)). **[Read]** Contrapunk (MIT, a Rust engine with Tauri and WebAssembly builds) implements this as `transpose_diatonic(note, +2 degrees)`, and for a note outside the key falls back in three steps: borrow the third from the nearest parallel mode that contains the note (widening from Aeolian and harmonic minor through Dorian, melodic minor, Mixolydian, Phrygian, Lydian and Phrygian dominant to all 11 modes), else try consonant intervals in the order 4, 3, 9, 8, 7, 5 semitones above (or −3, −4, −8, −9, −5, −7 below), else keep the note; it also has contrary-motion, strict-counterpoint and octave-doubling modes ([Contrapunk ENGINE_DEEP_DIVE.md](https://github.com/contrapunk-audio/contrapunk/blob/main/docs/ENGINE_DEEP_DIVE.md)). **[Read]** Second-species counterpoint permits a dissonance on a weak beat only as a stepwise passing tone between consonant strong beats ([Open Music Theory, second species](https://github.com/openmusictheory/openmusictheory.github.io/blob/master/secondSpecies.md)).

**[Design]** The recommended "third that fits the chord" algorithm combines these. Candidates are 2 scale steps up (third above), 2 down, 5 down (sixth below) or 5 up. A note is strong if it falls on beat 1 or 3 in 4/4 (beat 1 in 3/4) or lasts at least a beat; on a strong note the harmony must be a chord tone or a consonance with the melody (3, 4, 8 or 9 semitones; 0, 5 and 7 allowed with a penalty) and must not be an avoid note. If it fails, try the same interval in the other register (third above becomes sixth below), then the other third, then the nearest chord tone on the same side, then hold the previous harmony note. Weak notes shorter than half a beat keep the parallel result even if it clashes, provided the harmony moves by step. Reject any step that makes parallel fifths or octaves while both voices move, never cross the melody, and flip between third-above and sixth-below to stay in range. In C major over a C chord, the melody E–F–G with "third above" gives G–A–B; B on the strong beat is not in C–E–G, so it switches to the third below, E, and the result is G–A–E. "Follow the chord" is simpler still: the nearest chord tone strictly below each melody note (over C: C→G, E→C, G→E), which always gives a third, fourth or sixth and never clashes on a strong beat.

### Counter-melody, answers and pedals come from counterpoint rules

**[Read]** First-species rules give a counter-melody its shape: begin on a unison, fifth or octave; end on the tonic approached by contrary step, so the second-last interval is a minor third or major sixth; never move from a perfect fifth to a perfect fifth or octave to octave, counting compound intervals; never approach a perfect interval by similar motion; use no more than three of the same imperfect consonance in a row; keep the voices within a twelfth, preferably an octave; keep the two climaxes apart ([Open Music Theory, first species](https://github.com/openmusictheory/openmusictheory.github.io/blob/master/firstSpecies.md)). **[Read]** A good line mostly steps, follows any leap of a fourth or more with a step the other way, and takes no more than two leaps in a row ([cantus firmus rules](https://github.com/openmusictheory/openmusictheory.github.io/blob/master/cantusFirmus.md)). **[Design]** "Against the tune" builds a slower line (one note per chord or half bar) from chord tones on strong beats, preferring each chord's third and seventh, joined by stepwise passing tones and chosen by those rules, with Contrapunk's strict-counterpoint scoring as the reference. "Answer in the gaps" fills only rests of a beat or more and phrase ends at 2 or 4 bars, with the last motif moved by a scale step or an arpeggio of the current chord. "Hold one note" holds the tonic or fifth; **[Computed]** a C pedal in C major is a chord tone of C, F, Am and Dm7 and clashes with G, Em and B°, so the fifth is offered when the progression is mostly I–V and either is offered only when at least half the chords contain it. No fetched source states the conventions for pedal dissonance or call-and-response; those two modes are design.

### A second chord layer changes register and colour, filtered by the avoid-note rule

**[Read]** The jazzband library (ISC) computes available colour tones mechanically: on a dominant chord it starts from b9, 9, ♯9, 11, ♯11, b13 and 13 and drops any tension a semitone above the third or the seventh; on other chords a tension is a whole step above a chord tone ([Voicing.ts](https://github.com/felixroos/jazzband/blob/master/src/harmony/Voicing.ts)). **[Design]** Coded as one rule: a tension is unavailable if it sits a minor ninth above any chord tone, and in a diatonic layer it must belong to the key. **[Computed]** Applied to C major's seventh chords:

| Chord in C major | Available colours **[Computed]** | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Cmaj7 (I) | 9, 13 | F (11) |
| Dm7 (ii) | 9, 11, 13 (13 often avoided for its tritone with the third) | none |
| Em7 (iii) | 11 | F (b9), C (b13) |
| Fmaj7 (IV) | 9, ♯11, 13 | none |
| G7 (V) | 9, 13 | C (11; use sus4 instead) |
| Am7 (vi) | 9, 11 | F (b13) |
| Bm7♭5 (vii) | 11, b13 | C (b9) |

**[Computed]** In C major, sus2 chords exist on C, D, F, G and A, and sus4 chords on C, D, E, G and A. **[Read]** Any major or minor triad of the key can be preceded by its own dominant or leading-tone chord, and modal mixture borrows chords from the parallel key ([applied chords](https://github.com/openmusictheory/openmusictheory.github.io/blob/master/appliedChords.md); [modal mixture](https://github.com/openmusictheory/openmusictheory.github.io/blob/master/modalMixture.md)). **[Design]** Each re-harmonization gets a test against the melody note m on the chord's strong beat: a relative swap (I↔vi, I↔iii, IV↔ii, V↔vii°, V↔iii, which share two of three notes) if m is a chord tone or available tension of the new chord; a secondary dominant (in C: A7, B7, C7, D7, E7 before ii, iii, IV, V, vi) taking half of a preceding chord that lasts at least two beats, if m fits it; a passing diminished chord a semitone below the target root (C – C♯°7 – Dm7) where the bass would rise by step anyway; a tritone substitution (G7 and D♭7 share the tritone B–F; melody B becomes the seventh, F the third, G the ♯11, D the b9), plainly when m is the third or seventh and only under a "jazzy" setting otherwise; borrowed chords (iv, ♭VI, ♭VII, ♭III, ii°, v) unless the melody holds the natural form of a note the borrowed chord lowers; and a pedal bass. The app's existing `borrowedChords`, `spiceChords` and `voiceLeadingDistance` in `src/theory/progression.ts` already feed some of these. A second layer used as a pad keeps the chord symbols, sits from C4 to C6, drops the root (the first layer or the bass has it), uses third, seventh and one or two available tensions, holds one note per chord, and plays between the first layer's attacks rather than on them, except on each new chord's downbeat.

### A best-path search picks voicings that move the least

**[Read]** Open code chooses voicings three ways: filter and sort by total movement (tonal.js, jazzband, whose `difference` is the sum of absolute semitone moves), weighted random sampling (Impro-Visor), and hard rules plus a scored search (harmony-ts, Contrapunk) ([jazzband Voicing.ts](https://github.com/felixroos/jazzband/blob/master/src/harmony/Voicing.ts); [Impro-Visor AutomaticVoicingSettings.java](https://github.com/Impro-Visor/Impro-Visor/blob/master/src/imp/voicing/AutomaticVoicingSettings.java); [harmony-ts on npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/harmony-ts)). **[Read]** tonal.js's default only keeps the top note close to the previous top note ([voice-leading/index.ts](https://github.com/tonaljs/tonal/blob/main/packages/voice-leading/index.ts)), and M-Theory's own `voiceLeadingDistance` compares pitch classes with no register at all (`src/theory/progression.ts`). **[Read]** Contrapunk's scored search ships four style presets:

| Setting [Read] | Renaissance-strict | Baroque-chorale | Jazz | Free |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parallel fifths and octaves forbidden | yes | yes | no | no |
| Largest leap (semitones) | 5 | 12 | 127 | 127 |
| Step bonus | 60 | 25 | 3 | 1 |
| Common-tone bonus | 45 | 70 | 2 | 1 |
| Leap penalty per semitone | −15 | −4 | 0 | 0 |
| Crossing penalty | −150 | −80 | −10 | −2 |
| Contrary-motion bonus | +40 | +20 | +3 | 0 |

**[Design]** The recommended chooser is a Viterbi (dynamic-programming) search. For each chord, generate 20–150 candidate voicings from the shape tables below at every transposition inside the layer's range. Each candidate costs 0.1 per semitone away from the register's centre, 5 per low-interval violation and 10 if the third or seventh is missing. Each move between candidates costs the total semitone movement of matched voices, minus 2 per held common tone, plus 0.5 per semitone of top-voice leap beyond 4, plus 10 per parallel fifth or octave between voices that both move (forbidden outright in a classical style), plus 3 if every voice moves the same way. Hard rules forbid crossing, upper neighbours more than an octave apart, spans beyond the hand and a wrong bass note. Ties break by common tones, then total movement, then lowest pitch, so results are deterministic and testable. **[Computed]** For 64 chords with 100 candidates each that is 640,000 move evaluations, estimated at well under 50 ms in JavaScript (not timed). **[Computed]** For two chords with the same number of notes, the smallest total movement is always found among the n rotations of the sorted target chord; checked against brute force over 20,000 random chords of 2–5 notes with no mismatch (C→F triad 3 semitones; G7→Cmaj7 3; Dm7→G7 3). Keep the existing pitch-class score for chord suggestions and add a register-aware `voicingDistance` for the chooser.

### Piano voicings need registers, hand spans and a low-interval rule

**[Read]** tonal.js (MIT) supplies rootless left-hand shapes as data, for example m7 "3m 5P 7m 9M" or "7m 9M 10m 12P", dominant 7 "3M 6M 7m 9M" or "7m 9M 10M 13M", maj7 "3M 5P 7M 9M" or "7M 9M 10M 12P" ([voicing-dictionary data.ts](https://github.com/tonaljs/tonal/blob/main/packages/voicing-dictionary/data.ts)). **[Read]** Impro-Visor's defaults put the left hand at MIDI 46–67 and the right hand at 60–81, each spanning 9 semitones, with 1–2 left-hand notes and 1–4 right-hand notes; Contrapunk's voices are soprano 60–81, alto 55–76, tenor 48–69 and bass 40–64 ([AutomaticVoicingSettings.java](https://github.com/Impro-Visor/Impro-Visor/blob/master/src/imp/voicing/AutomaticVoicingSettings.java); [Contrapunk](https://github.com/contrapunk-audio/contrapunk/blob/main/docs/ENGINE_DEEP_DIVE.md)). **[Read]** In keyboard style the three upper voices, played by one hand, "should never span more than an octave"; root-position chords double the bass, and a seventh or leading tone is never doubled ([basso continuo rules](https://github.com/openmusictheory/openmusictheory.github.io/blob/master/bassoContinuo.md)). **[Secondary]** Low-interval limits from one arranging guide, converted so middle C is C4, put a minor second no lower than E3–F3 and a perfect fifth no lower than C♯2–G♯2; a commonly repeated table differs by 1–3 semitones ([Sweetwater summary](https://www.sweetwater.com/insync/low-interval-limit/)).

**[Design]** Offer close position (Cmaj7 = C4 E4 G4 B4), drop 2 (G3 C4 E4 B4), drop 3 (E3 C4 G4 B4), drop 2+4 (C3 G3 E4 B4), shells 1-3-7 (C3 E3 B3) and 1-7-3 (C3 B3 E4), spread tenths, rootless A/B shapes, and upper-structure triads (only the triad on the second degree of a dominant, adding 9, ♯11 and 13, is "safe"; the others are altered colours). **[Computed]** A rootless ii–V–I in C alternating A and B shapes (Dm7 F3 A3 C4 E4 → G7 F3 A3 B3 E4 → Cmaj7 E3 G3 B3 D4) moves one semitone and then five, six in all. **[Design]** Registers: chord and comping layer C3–C5 (MIDI 48–72), pad C4–C6, bass 40–64. Hand span 9 semitones by default, 12 when the user picks "wide", a tenth only in the left hand of the spread style. A conservative low-interval rule that satisfies both disagreeing tables, applied to every adjacent pair: below E2 (MIDI 40) an octave or more; below B2 (47) a fifth or more; below E3 (52) a minor third or more; above that, anything. The left hand takes the root, root plus seventh or root plus tenth below about C4, and the right hand the rest within 9–12 semitones.

### Showing chords on a keyboard

**[Read]** react-piano (MIT) gives exact key geometry: measured in white-key widths from C, the black keys sit at 0.55 (D♭), 1.8 (E♭), 3.5 (G♭), 4.7 (A♭) and 5.85 (B♭), are 0.65 of a white key wide and 66% as tall, and a white key is three times as tall as it is wide ([Key.js](https://github.com/kevinsqi/react-piano/blob/master/src/Key.js); [Keyboard.js](https://github.com/kevinsqi/react-piano/blob/master/src/Keyboard.js)). **[Design]** The view fits itself from the C at or below the lowest note to the B at or above the highest, at least two octaves and at most four (two on a phone), with a dot on middle C. The root gets the accent fill, other chord tones a second fill, added colours an outline, the melody or top note a ring, and an "original" and a "new" layer the same scheme at two opacities, all from the app's theme tokens. Each key is labelled with its degree (R, 3, 5, b7, 9), with the note name beneath on wider screens, and LH/RH brackets under a split voicing. Stepping through a progression keeps held notes lit and fades moving ones, which shows the voice leading. It sits beside the existing guitar `ChordDiagram.tsx`. Suggested fingerings (left hand 5-3-1 for three-note shells, right hand 1-2-3-5 or 1-2-4-5) are untested and unsourced.

## Stems: HT-Demucs in the Rust backend, downloaded on request, painted around while it works

**In plain words.** The practical choice for separating any song into drums, bass, vocals and "everything else" on the user's own PC is Meta's HT-Demucs v4. It is not the very best model on paper, but it is small (81 MB), its code is MIT-licensed, and it already runs in ONNX Runtime from Rust, which is how the Windows app should run it, on the graphics card where possible and the processor otherwise. On an ordinary 4-core laptop a 3-minute song should take roughly as long as it plays, and on a graphics card perhaps 20–40 seconds, but these are estimates from other people's hardware. The model is downloaded only when the user asks, checked against a known fingerprint, and never bundled into the installer, because its training data includes non-commercial recordings. Until the stems arrive, or if the model is never installed, cheap signal-processing tricks split the song well enough to paint drums, bass and a rough "voice" layer at once. A six-stem option adds a usable guitar stem and a poor piano stem.

### ONNX Runtime in Rust is the fastest portable route

**[Read]** HT-Demucs's internal spectrogram steps cannot be exported to ONNX, so working exports move the STFT (window 4096, hop 1024) and its inverse outside the network; two MIT projects document the recipe, and one publishes half-precision weight files at "~half the download" with identical speed ([demucs.onnx README](https://github.com/sevagh/demucs.onnx/blob/main/README.md); [StemSplit demucs-onnx README](https://github.com/StemSplit/demucs-onnx/blob/main/README.md)). **[Read]** On an 8-core Apple M4 Pro processor with no GPU, the single model took 1.59 s per 7.8 s segment in ONNX Runtime (a real-time factor of 0.20), 1.31 times faster than PyTorch, and the vendor states that the DirectML, CUDA and CoreML providers are "all ≥ 5× faster than the CPU" ([StemSplit README](https://github.com/StemSplit/demucs-onnx/blob/main/README.md)). **[Computed]** The same README's "about 22 s per 3-minute song" cannot be right: 180 s needs at least 23 segments, so at least 37 s, or about 49 s with the default overlap. **[Read]** By contrast the hand-written C++ port took 10 min 23 s for a 4-minute song on a 16-core desktop (4 min 9 s with four threads of four), and its browser WebAssembly build took about 41 minutes single-threaded for a 7-minute track ([demucs.cpp PERFORMANCE.md](https://github.com/sevagh/demucs.cpp/blob/main/.github/PERFORMANCE.md); [free-music-demixer README](https://github.com/sevagh/free-music-demixer/blob/main/README.md)); **[Computed]** real-time factors of 2.6, 1.04 and about 5.9, all too slow.

**[Read]** The Rust `ort` crate (MIT or Apache-2.0) wraps ONNX Runtime 1.31 with DirectML, CUDA, OpenVINO, XNNPACK and WebGPU providers ([ort README](https://github.com/pykeio/ort/blob/main/README.md)). **[Read]** `stem-splitter-core` (MIT or Apache-2.0) is a ready Rust library on `ort` that downloads an HT-Demucs model from a manifest "with SHA-256 verification", auto-detects CUDA, CoreML, DirectML, oneDNN and XNNPACK, reports download and separation progress, and lets the manifest URL be overridden ([stem-splitter-core README](https://github.com/gentij/stem-splitter-core/blob/main/README.md)). **[Read]** `demucs-rs` (Apache-2.0) is a pure-Rust HT-Demucs on the Burn framework with Vulkan and WebGPU back ends, but publishes no timings ([demucs-rs README](https://github.com/nikhilunni/demucs-rs/blob/main/README.md)). **[Read]** The app's Rust side today has Tauri 2, the updater, cpal 0.18 with ASIO and hound, and no machine-learning runtime (`src-tauri/Cargo.toml`). **[Design]** Fastest start: depend on `stem-splitter-core`, point its manifest at M-Theory's own release, and check that its pinned `ort` version (rc.11) builds on Windows next to cpal and ASIO; fall back to `ort` directly with the documented STFT-outside export, or to `demucs-rs` if packaging ONNX Runtime's Windows libraries proves awkward. **[Design]** Speed expectation, extrapolated from 0.27 effective on the M4 Pro and **[Secondary]** 0.49 for PyTorch on a 6-core desktop: a real-time factor of 0.5–1.0 on a 4-core laptop CPU (a 3-minute song in 1.5–3 minutes) and 0.1–0.2 on a DirectML GPU (20–40 s). **This must be measured on Windows hardware.** In the browser app, onnxruntime-web with a specially prepared WebGPU export (stock exports fail on the WebGPU provider **[Secondary]**) or the WebAssembly provider is a best-effort option that needs cross-origin isolation headers ([demucs-web README](https://github.com/timcsy/demucs-web/blob/main/README.md)).

### Seven-point-eight-second segments set the first-output delay

**[Read]** HT-Demucs only accepts segments of up to 7.8 s (343,980 samples at 44.1 kHz), overlaps them by 25% by default ("can probably be reduced to 0.1"), and crossfades them with triangular weights; shorter input is padded to the full 7.8 s, so a short first chunk saves nothing ([Demucs README](https://github.com/facebookresearch/demucs/blob/main/README.md); [apply.py](https://github.com/facebookresearch/demucs/blob/main/demucs/apply.py); [htdemucs.py](https://github.com/facebookresearch/demucs/blob/main/demucs/htdemucs.py)). **[Computed]** A 3-minute song is 31 segments at 25% overlap (26 at 10%), overlap costs ×1.33 or ×1.11, and the first stem arrives one segment's compute after decoding: about 1.6 s on the fast CPU and an estimated 4–8 s on a 4-core laptop. If the effective real-time factor r is below 1, playback can start after the first segment and the lead keeps growing; if r ≥ 1, the song needs a head start of about T × (r − 1) plus one segment, about 54 s for a 3-minute song at r = 1.3. **[Design]** Start separating as soon as a file is loaded, before Play; process segments in time order with a queue that jumps to the playhead on a seek; use overlap 0.1 for painting (17% cheaper; its effect on quality was not measured); resample other files to 44.1 kHz first.

### Cheap splits paint while the model works

**[Read]** Median-filter harmonic/percussive separation (librosa, ISC) filters the spectrogram along time for the harmonic part and along frequency for the percussive part, with a kernel of 31 and soft masks whose margin above 1 leaves a residual ("typical range is between 1 and 10") ([librosa decompose.py](https://github.com/librosa/librosa/blob/main/librosa/decompose.py)); an MIT real-time version uses a 1,024-sample window, 2,048-point FFT and 512 hop ([Real-Time-HPSS](https://github.com/sevagh/Real-Time-HPSS/blob/master/python/hpss_rt/hpss_rt.py)). **[Read]** REPET-SIM separates a repeating accompaniment from a non-repeating foreground by taking, for each frame, the median of its most similar frames at least 2 s away, with margins of 2 and 10 ([librosa vocal-separation example, ISC](https://github.com/librosa/librosa/blob/0.10.2/docs/examples/plot_vocal_separation.py); [nussl repet_sim.py, MIT](https://github.com/nussl/nussl/blob/master/nussl/separation/primitive/repet_sim.py)). **[Secondary]** Even an ideal magnitude mask reaches only about 9.27 dB on vocals, so these splits sit well below the neural model ([arXiv 2010.09453](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.09453v2)). **[Design]** A four-layer stand-in, computed on magnitudes only (no resynthesis is needed for painting): drums = the percussive mask with a margin of 2–3; bass = the harmonic part below 150–250 Hz; "voice-ish" = the harmonic part of the mid channel (L+R)/2 between 200 Hz and 8 kHz, times the REPET-SIM foreground; other = the rest. Percussive and band splits run live; REPET-SIM needs the whole file and takes seconds. It is good for "hit versus held" and "low versus high" and weak for "voice versus lead synth", so the canvas should mark these layers as approximate and crossfade to the real stems as each segment arrives.

### Plumbing, caching and the user's rights

**[Read]** Tauri's events "are not designed for low latency or high throughput", while channels "are designed to be fast and deliver ordered data" ([Tauri calling-frontend docs](https://github.com/tauri-apps/tauri-docs/blob/v2/src/content/docs/develop/calling-frontend.mdx)); GitHub release files may be up to 2 GiB each with no bandwidth limit ([GitHub releases docs](https://github.com/github/docs/blob/main/content/repositories/releasing-projects-on-github/about-releases.md)). **[Design]** Run separation on a blocking background task with a cancel flag checked between segments; stream `{segment, total, speed, time left}` plus per-segment envelopes over a `tauri::ipc::Channel`; ship a manifest (URL, size, SHA-256, licence and credit text) inside the app and refuse a mismatched download; make downloading opt-in with the size shown. **[Computed]** Three minutes of stereo 32-bit audio is 63.5 MB per stem (254 MB for four, 381 MB for six), about half that as 16-bit, and well under 1 MB per stem as band envelopes at 100 frames a second, which is all the canvas needs; key the cache on the SHA-256 of the decoded audio plus the model's name and version, and let the user clear it. **[Read]** Spleeter's README warns users to "get proper authorization from right owners" before separating copyrighted material, and UVR asks apps using its models to credit "UVR and its developers" ([Spleeter README](https://github.com/deezer/spleeter/blob/master/README.md); [UVR README](https://github.com/Anjok07/ultimatevocalremovergui/blob/master/README.md)). **[Design]** Show the model's origin and training data, credit UVR if its 2-stem model is offered, and say that stems are for personal study and painting unless the user holds the rights.

## Painting: each layer its own mark, coverage that follows the music, layouts chosen by a fingerprint

**In plain words.** Three changes turn the canvas from one sweep into a painting of the whole arrangement. First, every layer gets its own kind of paint, recognisable even in black and white: soft round blots for the kick, spatter for the snare, dry stipple for hats, a heavy low band for bass, translucent washes for chords, one continuous line for the melody, a thinner companion line for a harmony, a glowing multi-strand ribbon for vocals and a drifting fog for pads, always stacked in the same order. Second, the music decides how full the canvas gets: one quiet part leaves about a tenth of the ground painted, six loud parts about two-thirds, and at least a fifth always stays bare, so a sparse song looks sparse. Third, a song's measured "vibe" (its timbre, harmony, groove, dynamics and which roles are playing) picks the palette, the composition (a scrolling river, a looping palimpsest, a spiral, blooms, drifting currents, zones per role, a slow zoom, or the current sweep) and the marks, through smooth formulas with a fixed seed, so two songs with similar elements come out looking related while the same song always repaints the same. Science supports only a few of these links; most role-to-mark choices are taste, and should be presented as such.

### What the canvas already does, and what earlier research settled

**[Read]** In the working tree, `src/canvas/layout.ts` runs time left to right on one canvas per section ("the same letter is the same painting"), 4 or 8 bars per canvas, 6% margins, one octave per equal step of height; `src/canvas/cover.ts` averages per-band power over 6 s for only three roles (chords, melody, drums) against a 54 dB range; `src/listen/mood.ts` estimates energy, held versus clipped, brightness and key over the last several seconds. **[Secondary]** The research on matching sound to sight supports pitch to height, higher pitch to lighter and smaller, louder to bigger, harsh timbre to angular shapes (the round-versus-spiky effect held across 917 speakers of 25 languages) and mood to palette, and gives no support to pitch or key as hue ([pitch–lightness study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6407832/); [drawing study](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00789/full); [shape study](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/bitstream/2445/183962/1/719545.pdf)); **[Secondary]** semantic timbre ratings such as "bright" and "warm" predicted the lightness and saturation of colours people matched to instruments ([timbre–colour study](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1520131/full)). **[Computed]** Telling a short sound from a held one takes about 40–100 ms after its start, derived from the filter lengths of a real-time harmonic/percussive splitter ([Real-Time-HPSS](https://github.com/sevagh/Real-Time-HPSS/blob/master/python/hpss_rt/hpss_rt.py)), so each mark should be put down at once and only then lifted or pulled into a stroke. The marks, wet paint and pigment mixing themselves were settled earlier: a dab engine with CC0 brush numbers, bristles, a damped fluid and spectral pigment mixing ([libmypaint](https://github.com/mypaint/libmypaint/blob/d5a88fbe6649d5ec776bc42ec8c1f4bb29d7fd7f/mypaint-brush.c); [spectral.js](https://github.com/rvanwijnen/spectral.js/blob/bb2b05c9d1e65ae824d47e3b1cc17ea32c8ee68f/spectral.js)). What follows decides which marks, how many, where and why.

### A 74-number fingerprint makes similar songs measurably similar

**[Read]** Musly (MPL-2.0) compares songs by the timbre of their central 60 s: 25 mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) summarised as one Gaussian, compared by Jensen–Shannon divergence, then "Mutual Proximity" rescaling, which asks whether two songs are unusually close for both of them and so stops a few average-sounding songs from matching everything ([timbre.cpp](https://github.com/dominikschnitzer/musly/blob/master/libmusly/methods/timbre.cpp); [mutualproximity.cpp](https://github.com/dominikschnitzer/musly/blob/master/libmusly/mutualproximity.cpp)). **[Read]** rp_extract (GPL-3.0) computes rhythm patterns over about 6 s segments on 24 loudness bands, weighting modulation by `1/(f/4 + 4/f)`, which peaks at 4 Hz ([rp_extract.py](https://github.com/tuwien-musicir/rp_extract/blob/master/rp_extract.py)). **[Read]** essentia (AGPL, formulas only) defines danceability by detrended fluctuation analysis over 310 ms to 8.8 s ("usually range from 0 to 3"), dynamic complexity as the mean distance in decibels from a loudness estimate weighted toward loud frames (normalise by 45), and octave-band spectral contrast ([danceability.cpp](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/blob/23ae7ba/src/algorithms/highlevel/danceability.cpp); [dynamiccomplexity.cpp](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/blob/23ae7ba/src/algorithms/highlevel/dynamiccomplexity.cpp); [spectralcontrast.cpp](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/blob/23ae7ba/src/algorithms/spectral/spectralcontrast.cpp)). **[Read]** librosa's CENS chroma (ISC) quantises each chroma vector at 0.4, 0.2, 0.1 and 0.05 and smooths it over 41 frames, making harmony features robust to dynamics and timbre ([spectral.py](https://github.com/librosa/librosa/blob/76f10b8/librosa/feature/spectral.py)).

| Group **[Design]** | Numbers | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Timbre | 35 | MFCC 1–12 means and log-variances over 20–30 s; spectral contrast in 6 bands; flatness in 4 bands; centroid in octaves from 1 kHz |
| Harmony | 16 | Key clarity; major-minus-minor strength; harmonic-change rate; roughness; CENS chroma rotated to the tonic (so transposed songs match) |
| Rhythm | 10 | Tempo folded into one octave; onsets per second; pulse clarity; DFA danceability; rhythm histogram in 6 bands from 0.5 to 10 Hz |
| Dynamics and articulation | 5 | Loudness range; dynamic complexity; crest; share of short versus held events; legato ratio |
| Texture and roles | 8 | Percussive-to-harmonic ratio; number of active layers; presence of drums, bass, chords, melody, vocals; noisiness |

**[Design]** That is about 74 numbers, roughly 300 bytes, computed in a worker on audio first normalised by K-weighted loudness. Each number is turned into a z-score against **fixed reference statistics shipped with the app** (never against the song itself, which would erase the very differences that should show), squashed with tanh(z/2), and compared with a weighted distance in which the five groups weigh the same. For the app's own songs the tempo, key, chord changes, note density and articulation are read straight from the arrangement, and audio supplies only timbre and production; for imported songs, the stems supply the per-role parts. **[Computed]** Ratio features ignore a change of gain but not of equalisation: on a pink-like spectrum a +3 dB shelf above 5 kHz moved the centroid from 3,833 to 4,324 Hz (+12.8%) and full-band flatness from 0.421 to 0.547 (+30%), which is why flatness and contrast are measured per band, the centroid in octaves, and brightness given modest weight. **[Design]** A testable target: the fingerprint of one mix should move less between "−6 dB", "±3 dB shelves" and "MP3 at 128 kb/s" than between two different songs of the same style. **[Read]** Learned embeddings could add a "semantic" axis, but only some licences fit: the CLAP audio model through transformers.js (Apache-2.0 card, with a CC BY 4.0 attribution note **[Secondary]**), OpenL3 (CC BY 4.0 weights) and EfficientAT (MIT; its smallest AudioSet model has 0.98 M parameters and costs 0.11 billion multiply-adds) ([transformers.js README](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers.js/blob/main/README.md); [OpenL3 README](https://github.com/marl/openl3/blob/main/README.md); [EfficientAT README](https://github.com/fschmid56/EfficientAT/blob/main/README.md)); MERT's weights changed to CC BY-NC 4.0 and the Essentia models are non-commercial, so both are out **[Secondary]**. **[Design]** Keep embeddings optional; the hand-built fingerprint alone already makes "similar elements, similar painting" possible and explainable.

### Smooth functions and feature-space noise turn the fingerprint into a style

**[Design]** The mapping has two kinds of function, so that a small change in the music always makes a small change in the picture. First, the fingerprint is projected onto four readable axes, each `tanh` of a fixed weighted sum: **energy** (onset rate, flatness, centroid, percussive share, inverse loudness range), **brightness/valence** (majorness, key clarity, centroid, low roughness), **groove** (danceability, pulse clarity, the 2–4 Hz rhythm bands) and **density** (active layers, inverse contrast, harmonic-change rate). Every continuous visual parameter is a smooth function of those axes; in the OKLCH colour space, for example, ground lightness 0.93 − 0.10 × energy, palette chroma 0.04 + 0.12 × energy, palette centre hue blended from 235° (cool) to 55° (warm) by valence, and hue sweep 30° + 120° × density; stroke speed and field turbulence follow energy, and mean stroke length follows the legato ratio. Second, "arbitrary but stable" choices (secondary hues, the flow field's seed, composition anchors, brush variants) come from value noise over feature space: divide three axes into cells of 0.5 (about four per axis), give each of the eight surrounding lattice corners a hashed random number from its coordinates and the run number, and blend them smoothly. The result is continuous, identical for identical input, nearly identical for neighbours, and different for distant songs. Discrete choices (the layout family, the mark family per role) take the highest of affinity + 0.15 × noise, switch only when another option leads by 0.1 for at least a bar and never mid-phrase, and are fixed for the song after its first 20–30 s (at once for a decoded file). Each mark's own randomness comes from a hash of its layer, onset time rounded to 10 ms, pitch or band, and the visible run number, so similar songs share style and structure while their marks, which follow their own notes, never repeat. **[Read]** The earlier research's rules for this are concrete: all randomness from a seed and never from `Math.random()` or the clock, judged on a contact sheet of at least nine seeds ([algorithmic-art-skill](https://github.com/Pardesco/algorithmic-art-skill)). **[Design]** Three automated checks follow: the same audio twice gives bit-identical parameters; parameter change is bounded by a constant times fingerprint change; and a contact sheet of songs ordered by fingerprint distance looks like a gradual series. No open project was found that maps music features to generative-art parameters with a continuity guarantee, so this scheme is a proposal built from standard noise and hashing tools.

### Nine mark families keep layers readable even in greyscale

**[Read]** The Harmony drawing tool (GPL-3.0-or-later) supplies two ready brushes for the line roles: "ribbon", 50 painters each chasing the pointer as a damped spring (`div` 0.1, `ease` random 0.6–0.8, alpha 0.05), which gives a lagging multi-strand ribbon; and "sketchy", which links each new point to earlier points within about 63 px with a probability falling with distance and draws only the middle 40% of each link at alpha 0.05, giving hatching that thickens where a path revisits itself ([mrdoob/harmony](https://github.com/mrdoob/harmony)). **[Secondary]** Visual texture contrast pairs with sound brightness and coarseness with compactness in one crossmodal project ([QMUL crossmodal page](https://comma.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/projects/crossmodal/)). No study maps instrument roles to mark types, so every row below is **[Design]**.

| Role | Mark family | Scale and opacity | What the sound changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kick and low drums | Round soft "thump" blot at the bottom | Large, 0.15–0.35 | Level → size; attack sharpness → edge hardness; decay → halo |
| Snare, clap | Spatter burst, hard-edged droplets in a cone | Small drops, high opacity | Noisiness → spread; level → count |
| Hats, cymbals, shakers | Dry-brush stipple near the top | Tiny, 0.3–0.6 | Onset rate → density; open versus closed → trail length |
| Bass | Broad flat band in the bottom fifth | Wide, 0.2–0.4 | Legato → continuous band; staccato → stacked short slabs |
| Chords | Translucent washes or blocks spanning the chord's range | Very wide, 0.05–0.15 per layer, 3–6 layers | Held → soft bleeding wash; stabbed → hard-edged block; chord change → new wash; tension → more layers, cooler and greyer |
| Melody | One continuous loaded line on the pitch track | Medium width ∝ loudness, 0.6–0.9 | Legato → connected curve; staccato → dotted dabs along the same path; vibrato → ripple; bends → slope |
| Harmony line | Thinner companion line, or "sketchy" hatching between melody and harmony | 0.5–0.7 of the melody's width, lighter tint | Parallel motion → tight parallel; contrary motion → crossing; dissonance → denser hatch |
| Vocals | Glowing multi-strand "ribbon" in a separate additive light pass | 0.05 per strand | Breathiness → strands spread; level → glow; vibrato → ripple |
| Pads, ambience | Edge-less fog drifting on a slow curl-noise field | Huge, 0.02–0.06 | Brightness → lightness; level → density |

**[Design]** Five legibility rules make overlaps read as texture on a field rather than as mess: roles differ in scale by roughly a factor of three (fog ≫ washes > bass band > melody line > specks); a fixed depth order (fog, washes, bass, harmony, melody, vocal glaze added as light, percussion on top), following the earlier finding that light must be added in its own pass or a yellow glow over blue turns green ([ClaudeAnimationBase](https://github.com/JohnHeibel/ClaudeAnimationBase)); at most two roles share a hue family, and the rest are separated by light versus dark before hue; edges carry articulation (hard for sharp attacks and staccato, lost for legato and pads); and line roles never spatter while percussion never draws long lines, so the role reads from shape alone. Two songs that both have a busy hi-hat, a held pad and a stepwise melody will then show the same top stipple, the same fog and the same kind of line, whatever their key. For the app's own tracks the role is the track type, and the harmony layer is whatever the harmonizer wrote; for imported songs bass and vocals come straight from their stems, the drum stem is split into kick, snare and hats by band, and "other" is split by register and articulation into chords or pads versus a melody line. A harmony line inside an imported vocal stem cannot be told apart by any method the researchers found.

### Coverage follows musical density: 11% for one quiet part, 67% for six loud ones

**[Read]** One automatic oil painter stops adding where 0.65 of a stroke's path is already painted and rejects strokes that would destroy more than 0.4 of well-painted pixels ([ofxOilPaint](https://github.com/jagracar/ofxOilPaint)); generative-art rules "reserve 20–40% of the canvas as near-empty ground" ([algorithmic-art-skill](https://github.com/Pardesco/algorithmic-art-skill)); and a classic painterly-rendering loop paints coarse to fine only where the canvas still differs from a target by more than a threshold ([PyPainterly painterly.py](https://github.com/pschaldenbrand/PyPainterly/blob/master/painterly.py)). **[Design]** Musical density D is the sum over roles of "active in the last bar" (0 or 1) times the role's short-term loudness relative to the song's 95th-percentile level, and the target coverage is `C* = 0.04 + 0.74 × (1 − exp(−0.35 × D))`. **[Computed]** One quiet layer (D = 0.3) aims at 11%, two quiet layers (0.6) at 18%, two loud layers (1.8) at 39%, four medium ones (2.4) at 46% and six loud ones (5.4) at 67%; the ceiling of 78% always leaves at least 22% of ground bare. **[Computed]** Overlapping marks of opacity α reach 1 − (1 − α)^n, so a 0.04 wash needs 5.5, 17 and 29.5 layers to reach 20%, 50% and 70% coverage, while 0.15 impacts need 1.4, 4.3 and 7.4: washes fill slowly and percussion fills fast, which is why percussion needs the strictest ration. **[Design]** Keep a 64 × 36 coverage map weighted by opacity; allow a new mark in a cell only while that cell is below C* times the layout's importance weight there, otherwise shrink, thin or scumble it; ration each role's paint per second in proportion to its own density so a busy hi-hat cannot fill the canvas alone; fade old paint toward a floor of about 40% of its contrast with a half-life counted in bars or sections, not seconds, and count faded paint at reduced weight. A quiet passage after a loud one simply stops adding paint, and the bare ground stays visible.

### Seven compositions replace the single sweep

**[Read]** fieldplay (MIT) moves up to a million particles on the GPU through a user-defined velocity field with fourth-order Runge–Kutta steps, and spectro (MIT) is a WebGL spectrogram that scales and colours in the shader ([fieldplay README](https://github.com/anvaka/fieldplay/blob/master/README.md); [spectro LICENSE](https://github.com/calebj0seph/spectro/blob/master/LICENSE)). **[Read]** Curl noise is divergence-free, so particles "never clump into sinks" ([algorithmic-art-skill](https://github.com/Pardesco/algorithmic-art-skill)). **[Secondary]** No open WebGL painter was found that uses time as angle and frequency as radius. Every layout keeps one fixed spatial law for pitch and changes how time is spent:

| Layout **[Design]** | Time | Pitch | Strength | Weakness | Chosen for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sweep (current) | Across a section page | Height | Readable | Thin columns, little overlap | Default |
| Scrolling river | Canvas scrolls left at bar speed, a weak drift carries wet paint downstream | Height | Painterly flow | Only the recent part on screen; export a long strip | Steady groove, long songs |
| Palimpsest | Position within a bar or phrase; each loop glazes over the dried last one | Height | Repeats become pattern | Needs fade and budget | Repetitive, danceable |
| Spiral | One turn per 4 or 8 bars, or one Archimedean spiral for the song | Radius | Strong central form; downbeats line up on spokes | Less overlap | Metric music with clear phrases |
| Centre-out blooms | Each section blooms from its own golden-angle anchor | Local height or angle | Sections visible | Pitch less readable | Ambient, through-composed |
| Flow-field carriers | Marks born at their pitch, then carried by curl noise whose speed follows energy | Height at birth | Most painterly | Time order lost | Energetic, textural, electronic |
| Zones per role | Soft regions: bass low, chords middle, melody upper middle, percussion high, vocals across the centre | Height inside each zone | Legible with many parts | Less unified | Dense arrangements |
| Breathing zoom | The whole canvas shrinks slowly (to half over a song); new paint lands at full scale | Height | Old material becomes the core | Resampling blurs | Slow builds |

**[Design]** In the spiral, angle = 2π × beat ÷ beats per turn and radius runs from inner to outer limit with log2 of frequency, so each pitch keeps one radius and a repeated groove draws the same rosette every turn. In the flow-field layout a restoring force pulls each mark back toward its birth height so it drifts at most about one-twelfth of the canvas (about an octave). The coarse-to-fine painterly loop doubles as a placement rule: make the target an image the music implies (role fields at their heights and times) and paint only where the canvas differs from it, so empty music leaves empty ground. The layout family is chosen from the fingerprint (groove → palimpsest, spiral or river; low energy and long notes → blooms or flow field; many layers → zones), with anchors, spiral phase and field seed varied per section by the feature-space noise above.

### Timing and per-layer features

**[Secondary]** Broadcast guidance detects sound arriving ahead of picture from about 45 ms and picture ahead of sound only from about 125 ms (acceptable to about 90 and 185 ms), and the brain's audiovisual binding window is narrower when sound leads ([audio-video sync overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audio-to-video_synchronization); [NTIA report](https://its.ntia.gov/publications/download/11-474.pdf)). **[Design]** For files and the app's own playback, analyse ahead and land each mark 0–20 ms before its sound; for live input, onset detection (15–30 ms) plus one or two display frames gives a 40–65 ms lag at 60 Hz, at the edge of detectability, so draw a provisional mark on the first frame after an onset and refine it. Per role, normalised to that role's recent range: attack rise time from 10% to 90% of peak sets edge hardness (under 10 ms hard, over 50 ms feathered); the legato ratio, effective duration above 40% of peak divided by the time to the next onset ([essentia effectiveduration.h](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/blob/23ae7ba/src/algorithms/temporal/effectiveduration.h)), above 0.8 gives connected strokes and below 0.4 separate dabs; roughness and per-band flatness give angular, splayed marks and spatter spread; brightness gives lightness and saturation inside the palette; loudness against the song's 95th percentile gives size, pressure and paint load. Fast channels (size, edge, position) change per event, slow ones (palette, manner, layout) per section, and no single event may change a slow channel.

## Conclusion

The five features share one design lesson: the parts that make an app feel easy (presets, macros, named harmony choices, a canvas that stays sparse when the music is sparse) are thin layers of explicit, testable rules over engines that open source already does well, and the value lies in choosing the rules honestly and labelling taste as taste. Two connections are easy to miss. The 17-number reface preset format doubles as an exchange format with real hardware, so a beginner's saved sound can travel to a reface CS unchanged. And the vibe fingerprint can be computed exactly from the arrangement for songs written in M-Theory and from audio and stems for imported ones, which means a user's own sketch can be placed next to an imported song by distance and painted in a related style, the most direct demonstration that "similar elements look similar" works.

The constraints that matter are not the ones that first come to mind: piano quality is limited by memory, not download size; separation speed by the first 7.8 s segment and the user's graphics card, not the model's accuracy; and the synth's realism by unmeasured curves, not by processing power, since plain TypeScript has room to spare. The next step is therefore measurement rather than more reading: record a reface CS and tune the curves against it, run the synth and the separator on the weakest Windows laptop the app must serve, audition the trimmed piano pack against the original, play every harmony option over real progressions, and judge contact sheets of paintings ordered by fingerprint distance.
