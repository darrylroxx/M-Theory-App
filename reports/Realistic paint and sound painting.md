# Let any song pull a real brush

No open-source project already paints convincingly from music, so the best route is to join three bodies of free code that each solve one part of the job. For the marks, **libmypaint's dab engine and its public-domain brush files** cover touches, pulled strokes and spatter, with a bristle model from Krita for dry-brush streaks. For the paint, **a heavily damped two-dimensional fluid with a paint-height channel (the "Fluid Paint" demo) plus the pigment rules of two classic watercolour models** cover flow, dark rims, granulation, drying and paper, and **spectral pigment mixing (spectral.js)** makes blue and yellow go green. For the hearing, the answer is **an onset-and-note-ending listener built on your own FFT, not the browser's AnalyserNode**. All of this may be ported or re-implemented in a GPL-3.0-or-later TypeScript and WebGL2 app, with three exclusions that matter: the Mixbox pigment library (non-commercial licence), the trained model files of the madmom library (non-commercial), and MIRtoolbox code (GPL version 2 only). AGPL libraries such as essentia are formula references only. The hinge between sound and paint is one result from the audio work: telling a short sound from a held one takes roughly **40–100 ms after the sound starts**, so the painter must put a small touch down at once and only then decide whether to lift it or pull it into a stroke. The research on how people match sound to sight supports pitch by height, higher sounds lighter and smaller, louder sounds bigger and bolder, harsh sounds more angular and broken, and colour chosen by overall mood. It gives **no support to several attractive rules** (calm music is wet paint, staccato is dabs, a sharp attack is a hard edge, pitch or key is hue); those are taste and should be offered as options, not presented as truths. Nothing in open code ages a finished painting, so the smooth-fade recommendation is a design proposal: fade pigment density, not opacity, and fade to a floor. Two limits frame everything below: the researchers could reach only GitHub, so papers are known from abstracts and from other people's code, and nothing was rendered, listened to or timed in a browser.

## Four labels keep code facts apart from guesses

Every claim below carries one of four labels, because the difference between them is the difference between something you can build on and something you must test by eye and ear. **[Code]** means the researchers read it in a source file, licence file or specification at the linked commit. **[Computed]** means the researchers worked it out from those constants, by arithmetic or by running a small script. **[Unread]** means it comes from a research paper or a painting-teaching page that was not opened in full: the researchers saw an abstract, a search-result summary, or recalled it from memory. **[Design]** means a design choice or an inference with no evidence behind it, however reasonable it sounds. Where a single paragraph mixes them, each sentence is labelled.

## Marks: a dab stamper plus bristles makes touches, strokes and spatter

**In plain words.** A convincing brush mark does not need a physical brush simulation. The engine inside the MyPaint program builds every mark from many small soft-edged stamps ("dabs"), and its stock brush files are sets of numbers, free of copyright, that already describe dry brush, charcoal, oil, watercolour, spatter and fan brushes. A touch and a pulled stroke are the same machine with one dial turned: stamp by time and you get a touch that pools while the brush rests; stamp by distance and you get a stroke. Add Krita's bristle idea (one thin line per hair, each hair running out of ink on its own) and you get streaky, broken dry-brush strokes that thin as the paint runs out. What makes marks look hand-made is a short, consistent list of small irregularities, each with a number you can copy from working code. The one gap is drips and flicked paint: open code has good scatter recipes but no physical model, so those must be designed.

### The dab engine and how one dial separates a touch from a stroke

**[Code]** libmypaint turns each piece of brush movement into a number of dabs given by `dist/actual_radius × dabs_per_actual_radius + dist/base_radius × dabs_per_basic_radius + dt × dabs_per_second`, with defaults 2, 0 and 0, carries the fractional remainder forward, and interpolates position, pressure and time between input points for each dab ([mypaint-brush.c](https://github.com/mypaint/libmypaint/blob/d5a88fbe6649d5ec776bc42ec8c1f4bb29d7fd7f/mypaint-brush.c#L1253-L1287)). **[Code]** Every one of about 60 settings is computed as a base value plus a sum of piecewise-linear curves over 18 inputs (pressure, two smoothed speeds, a random number, distance along the stroke, direction, tilt and others); contributions always add, never multiply ([mypaint-mapping.c](https://github.com/mypaint/libmypaint/blob/d5a88fbe6649d5ec776bc42ec8c1f4bb29d7fd7f/mypaint-mapping.c#L146-L188)). **[Code]** Each dab is an ellipse whose opacity falls off in two straight segments of the squared distance from its centre, controlled by a `hardness` setting (default 0.8), and the per-dab opacity is corrected for overlap with `alpha_dab = 1 − (1 − opaque)^(1/dabs_per_pixel)` so that dense stamping does not saturate ([mypaint-tiled-surface.c](https://github.com/mypaint/libmypaint/blob/d5a88fbe6649d5ec776bc42ec8c1f4bb29d7fd7f/mypaint-tiled-surface.c#L226-L490), [mypaint-brush.c](https://github.com/mypaint/libmypaint/blob/d5a88fbe6649d5ec776bc42ec8c1f4bb29d7fd7f/mypaint-brush.c#L1042-L1250)). **[Computed]** The default spacing is therefore a quarter of the dab's diameter, while the painterly stock brushes stamp every 4–10% of the diameter.

**[Code]** The stock brushes show the touch-versus-stroke dial directly. A touch is made by `dabs_per_second` with little or no distance-based stamping, often with a large random radius and a soft edge; a pulled stroke uses five or six dabs per radius, long elliptical dabs locked to the direction of travel (ratio 6–7), and the "stroke" input as a measure of paint used up ([brush files](https://github.com/mypaint/mypaint-brushes/tree/08da4a48a4b63f2e0f3303942d7c21da8479ea9f/brushes)). The brush parameter files are **CC0-1.0**, so their numbers can be copied freely ([Licenses.dep5](https://github.com/mypaint/mypaint-brushes/blob/08da4a48a4b63f2e0f3303942d7c21da8479ea9f/Licenses.dep5)). **[Code]** The stock oil brush models paint running out without any reservoir: its dabs are ellipses of ratio 7.1 aligned with motion, and as the stroke input rises, "smudge" (painting with colour picked up from the canvas) climbs to 1 while the brush's memory of that colour shortens to nothing, so the brush ends up only dragging what is already there ([oil-01-paint.myb](https://github.com/mypaint/mypaint-brushes/blob/08da4a48a4b63f2e0f3303942d7c21da8479ea9f/brushes/tanda/oil-01-paint.myb)). **[Computed]** With that brush's setting of 6.0 the stroke input reaches its maximum after exp(6.0) ≈ 403 brush radii of travel; the engine default of 4.0 gives 54.6 radii. **[Code]** The dry-brush file gets its speckled, broken look from many tiny soft dabs (radius about 1.8 px, six plus six per radius) scattered sideways by up to 1.4 radii as pressure rises, so the width of the stroke is the scatter, not the dab ([dry_brush.myb](https://github.com/mypaint/mypaint-brushes/blob/08da4a48a4b63f2e0f3303942d7c21da8479ea9f/brushes/classic/dry_brush.myb)).

**[Code]** Colour pickup is a "smudge bucket": a remembered colour that is pulled toward the sampled canvas colour by a one-pole filter whose pole is the `smudge_length` setting, then mixed into the brush colour; up to 256 buckets let each virtual bristle of the stock fan brush carry its own colour ([mypaint-brush.c](https://github.com/mypaint/libmypaint/blob/d5a88fbe6649d5ec776bc42ec8c1f4bb29d7fd7f/mypaint-brush.c#L920-L1035)). **[Computed]** A faithful WebGL2 port is small: one settings evaluator, a spacing loop, and a six-line fragment shader for the dab mask on instanced quads. The one awkward part is the smudge blend, which pulls the canvas toward a partly transparent colour and cannot be done by a single fixed blend mode; it needs two passes per dab or a shader that reads the canvas through a ping-pong pair of textures, which spectral mixing needs anyway.

### Bristles: one line per hair, each with its own ink

**[Code]** Krita's bristle engine creates one bristle for every opaque pixel of the brush tip (times a density factor), draws each as a one-pixel line along the stroke with fresh random jitter per segment, and gives each bristle a counter that rises with every pixel it draws; the bristle's ink is read from a depletion curve at that counter (default: linear to empty over **1,024 pixels**), and its opacity is its length times its remaining ink. A "threshold" option skips any bristle whose length is below `1 − pressure`, so a light touch paints only the central hairs ([hairy_brush.cpp](https://github.com/KDE/krita/blob/afc124763282f5387735760cdda838c1c81545a1/plugins/paintops/hairy/hairy_brush.cpp), [KisHairyInkOptionData.h](https://github.com/KDE/krita/blob/afc124763282f5387735760cdda838c1c81545a1/plugins/paintops/hairy/KisHairyInkOptionData.h)). **[Computed]** A round tip 20 px across at full density has about 314 bristles; in WebGL2 they can be drawn as instanced thin quads with maximum-alpha blending inside the stroke buffer so overlapping hairs do not build up. These files are GPL-2.0-or-later and may be translated into a GPL-3.0-or-later app.

**[Code]** Krita's colour-smudge engine offers two cheap ways to push paint. "Smearing" copies the pixels under the previous dab onto the current one at the smudge rate, which drags image content along the stroke in streaks; "dulling" blends in one average colour sampled under the brush, which blends softly without streaks; brush colour then goes on top at the colour rate squared ([KisColorSmudgeStrategyBase.cpp](https://github.com/KDE/krita/blob/afc124763282f5387735760cdda838c1c81545a1/plugins/paintops/colorsmudge/KisColorSmudgeStrategyBase.cpp)). **[Code]** The fullest physical brush in open web code is in Fluid Paint: 10–100 bristles, each a chain of 10 points solved with 20 constraint passes per frame, each with a random stiffness of up to 0.3 so that limp hairs splay and split the tip. It has no paint-load variable at all: bristles deposit a constant amount while touching ([brush.js](https://github.com/dli/paint/blob/6b8adfa226ecda14546c90b14a3d2a0d4898ad89/brush.js)). **[Code]** A far cheaper trick comes from the Harmony drawing app (GPL-3.0-or-later): several "painters" each follow the target as a damped spring with its own ease of 0.6–0.8, giving parallel lagging lines that behave like bristles with individual inertia ([Harmony brushes](https://github.com/mrdoob/harmony/tree/master/js/brushes)). **[Unread]** The classic research papers on bristle brushes were not re-read, and no open implementation named after them was found; whether Krita's engine derives from them is not stated in its files.

**[Design]** For a browser painter the sensible ladder is: Krita-style bristle lines with ink counters first, Harmony-style per-bristle lag second, and Fluid Paint's full bristle physics only if wet-in-wet oil becomes a headline feature.

### Spatter is well served; flicks and drips have to be designed

**[Code]** The stock spatter brush stamps one dab per 20 radii of travel, displaces it by a random two radii on top of pointer noise of twelve radii (the maximum), and draws each droplet's radius from a log-normal distribution with the engine's maximum spread; droplets larger than nominal are made more transparent by `(r_nominal/r)²` ([splatter-02.myb](https://github.com/mypaint/mypaint-brushes/blob/08da4a48a4b63f2e0f3303942d7c21da8479ea9f/brushes/tanda/splatter-02.myb), [mypaint-brush.c](https://github.com/mypaint/libmypaint/blob/d5a88fbe6649d5ec776bc42ec8c1f4bb29d7fd7f/mypaint-brush.c#L1099-L1109)). **[Computed]** That gives a median droplet radius of 3.7 px with the middle two-thirds between 0.8 and 16 px. **[Code]** A second stock brush behaves like a flick: droplets are emitted by time only (10 per second), are large when the hand is slow and small when it is fast, and are thrown ahead of the pointer along its velocity ([splatter-04.myb](https://github.com/mypaint/mypaint-brushes/blob/08da4a48a4b63f2e0f3303942d7c21da8479ea9f/brushes/tanda/splatter-04.myb)). **[Code]** Krita's spray engine supplies the choice of scatter laws: a number of particles per dab (default 12) placed at a random angle and a radial distance that is uniform (clumps at the centre), square-root (even over the area), Gaussian, clustered, or drawn from a curve, each particle with random size, opacity and hue ([spray_brush.cpp](https://github.com/KDE/krita/blob/afc124763282f5387735760cdda838c1c81545a1/plugins/paintops/spray/spray_brush.cpp), [KisSprayRandomDistributions.cpp](https://github.com/KDE/krita/blob/afc124763282f5387735760cdda838c1c81545a1/plugins/paintops/spray/KisSprayRandomDistributions.cpp)).

**[Code]** The droplet-size laws in all of these are design choices by their authors, not measurements tied to fluid physics. No licensed open code models a flick as a parent drop with satellites, and the only drip code found is in a small GPL-3.0 watercolour palette, where drips are ordinary particles moved on the CPU: spawned only when wetness is at least 0.5, falling at 0.5–0.9 px per frame (before scaling by brush size) with a slow sideways sine, stopping at 65% of their life to form a bead ([drip.js](https://github.com/food211/Mixbox-Palette/blob/9915243c08207fc366a4059e4c548f9f5ddc1aa8/js/drip.js)). **[Design]** A flick can be assembled from the sourced parts (log-normal sizes with a spread of 0.3 for spray up to 1.5 for heavy spatter, opacity falling with size, throw distance proportional to gesture speed) plus two unsourced ones (droplets stretched along the throw, a few small satellites near each large drop). A drip can be treated as a stroke pulled by gravity whose load falls with distance, whose width follows load, and which ends in a bead; that too is a proposal, not a transcription.

## Wet paint: a damped fluid, a pinned edge and pigment that absorbs light

**In plain words.** Real-time wet paint in the browser does not need a full fluid simulation. The best open oil-paint demo uses a simple two-dimensional flow that is braked so hard it stops within a second, runs only where the brush has just been, and treats the thickness of paint as a height map that catches the light. Watercolour's characteristic looks (the dark rim round a wash, pigment gathering in the hollows of the paper, blooms where wet meets damp, feathered edges that stop at the grain) each reduce to a short rule that can ride on top of that flow, or be faked convincingly in one shader with no flow at all. Colour must be handled as pigment, which absorbs light, not as screen colour, which adds it; otherwise blue and yellow make grey. Drying is a wetness number that falls over time, with pigment locking in place as the water leaves. No open code fades an old painting on purpose, so the fade options here are proposals.

### The oil-paint core: two pressure passes, strong braking, and a clock that stops it

**[Code]** Fluid Paint (MIT) is a plain two-dimensional "stable fluids" solver, not a three-dimensional one. It keeps one floating-point texture whose red, green and blue are colour and whose alpha is paint height, plus velocity, divergence and pressure textures at the same resolution. Each frame it computes divergence, clears pressure, runs **two** Jacobi iterations, subtracts the pressure gradient, moves the paint along the flow with no loss, and moves the velocity along itself multiplied by a "fluidity" of **0.8** (slider range 0.6–0.9). There is no viscosity term, no swirl enhancement, no gravity and no drying variable; paint stops because velocity is cut by a fifth every frame and because each brushed rectangle is simulated for only **60 frames** after its last touch ([simulator.js](https://github.com/dli/paint/blob/6b8adfa226ecda14546c90b14a3d2a0d4898ad89/simulator.js#L347-L496), [advect.frag](https://github.com/dli/paint/blob/6b8adfa226ecda14546c90b14a3d2a0d4898ad89/shaders/advect.frag)). **[Computed]** After 60 frames the velocity is 0.8^60, about 1.5 millionths of where it started, so the cut-off is invisible; this is the cheapest drying model there is, time since last touch. **[Code]** Every pass is scissored to the union of recently brushed rectangles, and when none is live the simulation does nothing and the screen is not redrawn. Brush contact writes colour as an alpha blend toward the brush colour, height as a pure addition, and the brush's own velocity (scaled by 0.14 and clamped) into the flow ([splat.frag](https://github.com/dli/paint/blob/6b8adfa226ecda14546c90b14a3d2a0d4898ad89/shaders/splat.frag)). One line of `simulator.js` should not be copied: the end-of-simulation velocity clear is called with the wrong argument shape and does nothing, harmlessly.

**[Code]** The widely used WebGL fluid demo (MIT) is the better template for texture setup and fallbacks, but its defaults are tuned for smoke and ink: velocity on a grid 128 texels on the short side with dye at 1,024, 20 Jacobi iterations, a swirl-enhancing force of 30, and dye that fades ([script.js](https://github.com/PavelDoGreat/WebGL-Fluid-Simulation/blob/a2d292931f19d9b3b9f564e23e6c32729d2121c3/script.js#L59-L85)). **[Computed]** At those defaults the dye halves in about 0.7 s, which is a visualiser's behaviour, not a painting's. **[Design]** A paint solver should keep the low-resolution-flow, high-resolution-colour split, drop the swirl force, cut Jacobi to two to four with the demo's warm-started pressure (multiplied by 0.8 each frame), never let pigment dissipate, add a wetness texture, brake velocity per pixel according to wetness so dry paint cannot move, and add gravity times wetness times height to the downward velocity for runs. None of those adaptations exists in either code base. **[Computed]** Such a solver at 256 texels on the short side is about seven simulation passes and 0.8 million texel writes per frame, fewer than the smoke demo's 25 passes, so the display pass is likely the largest single cost.

### Watercolour: four short rules, with or without a flow underneath

Two independent open ports of the classic three-layer watercolour model agree on its equations, and both are slow CPU programs (up to 50 pressure-relaxation sweeps per step on grids of 128–200 cells), so the model as published is the wrong fit for 60 frames a second. Its pigment rules are cheap and separable.

**[Code]** Dark rims come from lowering pressure near the edge of the wet area: `p ← p − η·(1 − M′)·M`, where M is the wet mask, M′ is the same mask blurred over about 10 cells and η is 0.01–0.05, so water and the pigment it carries flow toward the boundary ([Watercolor2D.cpp](https://github.com/inchkev/watercolor/blob/0e4e3f77256f69b779c2a7db694ba0f4c43c67e4/src/Watercolor2D.cpp#L272-L297), [sim.rs](https://github.com/jfernand/curtis-watercolor/blob/31b66762bed4c6b379d9a9533cd1083ab928fbba/src/sim.rs#L407-L432)). **[Code]** Granulation comes from the settling rule: pigment sinks at `g·(1 − h·γ)·ρ` and lifts at `d·(1 + (h − 1)·γ)·ρ/ω`, where h is paper height, γ the pigment's granulation, ρ its density and ω its staining power, so granulating pigments collect in the paper's valleys and staining pigments do not lift again ([Watercolor2D.cpp](https://github.com/inchkev/watercolor/blob/0e4e3f77256f69b779c2a7db694ba0f4c43c67e4/src/Watercolor2D.cpp#L342-L366)). **[Code]** Blooms come from a capillary layer that lets the wet area creep into damp paper; the port's author notes that its constants "are not specified in the paper". The two ports disagree on the sign of the viscosity term and on velocity limits; **[Computed]** the diffusive (plus) form is the physically stable one.

**[Code]** The lattice-based ink model, available as an MIT Unity port and a new MIT-or-Apache Rust port, supplies the other two rules. Feathered edges that stop come from "pinning": a dry site stays unwettable until some neighbour holds more water than a threshold that depends on the paper's grain, so the front advances in fingers and halts. Drying fixes pigment in proportion to the fraction of water lost in that step, more strongly when little water remains, and fully dried sites drop all their ink into a fixed layer that never moves again ([moxi.wgsl](https://github.com/jfernand/moxi-ink/blob/ebb22fe512d97255f09ab3713812f681b29e034c/shaders/moxi.wgsl#L123-L242), [Moxi.compute](https://github.com/komietty/unity-moxi-ink/blob/a8711fc0bc8e5c921fab22f07c4e71bc670ff2a4/Assets/Moxi/Resources/Moxi.compute#L63-L77)). **[Computed]** One step of this model is about five passes with no iterative pressure solve, which suits WebGL2 well. Its caveat is trust: the Rust port has no reviewers, and the constants its author marks "tuned" are his fits, not published values ([params.rs](https://github.com/jfernand/moxi-ink/blob/ebb22fe512d97255f09ab3713812f681b29e034c/src/params.rs)).

**[Code]** The cheapest convincing watercolour found involves no simulation. p5.brush (MIT, WebGL2) draws each wash once, as seven steps of a twenty-step schedule of translucent, randomly grown polygons, into a half-float mask that stores `−ln(1 − alpha)` additively, so draw order does not matter and coverage is recovered as `1 − exp(−sum)`; it blurs that mask once at half resolution; and one composite shader then synthesises the rim (pigment rising over a band 1–5% of the shape's width), hard edges that follow the paper's hills, soft "lost" edges where the neighbouring wash is still wet, granulation strongest where paint is thin, back-run blooms with a dark ragged front 3% of their radius wide, pooling and two-pigment separation ([fill.js](https://github.com/acamposuribe/p5.brush/blob/ac2d829c760fcd677012caab95da002ad5aa692e/src/fill/fill.js#L485-L705), [gl_fill.js](https://github.com/acamposuribe/p5.brush/blob/ac2d829c760fcd677012caab95da002ad5aa692e/src/fill/gl_fill.js#L5-L14), [shader.frag](https://github.com/acamposuribe/p5.brush/blob/ac2d829c760fcd677012caab95da002ad5aa692e/src/core/gl/shader.frag#L350-L512)). **[Code]** The older generative recipe it descends from stacks 100 polygon layers at an opacity of 4 in 255 and is an offline technique ([watercolor.pde](https://github.com/sighack/watercolor-simulation/blob/ac6781b7487ad34191eaf0b1c1547cdf99ba24f9/watercolor.pde)); **[Computed]** as written it draws about 4.1 million vertices per wash.

**[Design]** For a live music painter the practical order is: the analytic route first, because its cost is per new mark and frames with no new paint cost nothing; then the four rules above (rim from the blurred wet mask, granulation in the settling rule, pinning against the paper, fixing as water leaves) riding on the damped flow; and the full lattice model only as an upgrade. No repository read implements the often-described "blur a wetness mask and move pigment along its gradient" as a time-stepped rule; a version consistent with the sources is three cheap half-resolution passes, and it is untested.

### Pigment mixing: computed proof that the method decides whether blue plus yellow is green

**[Code]** spectral.js (MIT, with its own GLSL file) converts a colour to a 38-band reflectance curve, mixes the bands in absorption-over-scattering form `K/S = (1 − R)²/(2R)` weighted by concentration, and converts back with `R = 1 + K/S − √((K/S)² + 2·K/S)` ([spectral.js](https://github.com/rvanwijnen/spectral.js/blob/bb2b05c9d1e65ae824d47e3b1cc17ea32c8ee68f/spectral.js#L373-L392), [spectral.glsl](https://github.com/rvanwijnen/spectral.js/blob/bb2b05c9d1e65ae824d47e3b1cc17ea32c8ee68f/shader/spectral.glsl)). **[Code]** libmypaint (ISC) uses a cheaper rule: a weighted geometric mean of two 10-band reflectance curves, with no scattering term, faded out at low opacity because, in its authors' words, spectral blending "does not handle low transparency well" ([helpers.c](https://github.com/mypaint/libmypaint/blob/d5a88fbe6649d5ec776bc42ec8c1f4bb29d7fd7f/helpers.c#L564-L604)). **[Code]** Mixbox, the best-known pigment-mixing library, is licensed **CC BY-NC 4.0** and cannot go into a GPL app ([Mixbox LICENSE](https://github.com/scrtwpns/mixbox/blob/a1bdb75a668f638ba066aa74bfd32809ed7fef45/LICENSE)).

**[Computed]** The researchers reproduced each method and mixed the same pairs half and half. This is a numerical check, not a judgement by eye.

| Mix (50:50) | Screen-colour average | Linear-light average | Geometric mean, 3 channels | K/S on 3 channels | libmypaint 10-band | spectral.js 38-band |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blue #0033cc + yellow #ffe600 | #808d66 (grey) | #bcac95 (beige) | #32702b (green) | #074707 (very dark green) | #33856a (teal-green) | #3f9e46 (green) |
| Red #d91e36 + yellow #ffe600 | #ec821b | #edaa25 | #eb5a12 | #e32c06 | #ee6e27 | #f75e2c |

| White + blue #0033cc | #8099e6 | #bcbee7 | #3276e4 | #0747da | #3e82e6 | #4ea1f5 |

Three conclusions follow. **[Computed]** The cheapest rule that turns blue and yellow green is a geometric mean in linear light, which is the same as storing optical density per channel, adding densities and showing `paper × exp(−density)`; it costs three exponentials per pixel but runs dark, and tints white too strongly because nothing scatters. **[Computed]** Applying K/S directly to the three screen channels is the darkest method in the table, and with real pigment constants its result depends on the pigment: using the classic model's 12-pigment table, one yellow with one blue gives a clear green (#8ace74) while the same yellow with another blue gives olive (#afa972). **[Computed]** spectral.js in plain JavaScript manages about 0.74 million mixes a second, far too slow for every pixel every frame on the CPU. **[Code]** Two open projects show how to afford it on the GPU: p5.brush precomputes the constant pigment's 38 values on the CPU and passes them as uniforms, so the shader converts only the canvas side; a GPL-3.0 palette project bakes the colour-to-spectrum step into a 32×32×320 lookup texture and needs no float textures at all ([shader.frag](https://github.com/acamposuribe/p5.brush/blob/ac2d829c760fcd677012caab95da002ad5aa692e/src/core/gl/shader.frag#L200-L223), [km-painter.js](https://github.com/food211/Mixbox-Palette/blob/9915243c08207fc366a4059e4c548f9f5ddc1aa8/js/km-painter.js#L1-L262)). **[Design]** The recommendation is spectral mixing in the deposit pass, which touches only pixels receiving new paint, with the 10-band geometric mean as the middle tier and density-per-channel as the floor.

### Thick paint, paper and canvas

**[Code]** Every implementation read stores thickness as one number beside colour, takes a 3×3 Sobel gradient of it for a surface normal, and lights it with one small specular highlight. Fluid Paint uses a normal scale of 7, roughness 0.075, a diffuse term of only 0.15 over 0.85 ambient and specular strength 0.5; bristle furrows are not modelled, they appear because each hair lays its own thin ridge of height ([painting.frag](https://github.com/dli/paint/blob/6b8adfa226ecda14546c90b14a3d2a0d4898ad89/shaders/painting.frag)). **[Code]** A newer MIT simulator adds a wetness-dependent gloss (roughness from 0.44 dry to 0.11 wet) and procedural bristle ridges across each stroke ([main.js](https://github.com/simonxxooxxoo/wet-paint-flow/blob/0b9ba9a5be665f3a2a8b2450945ec5006e61e2de/main.js#L1097-L1245)). **[Code]** Krita offers the no-lighting fallback: a separate grey height layer stamped from the brush tip, used only to brighten or darken the colour through a quadratic curve `f(x) = (1 − (4z − 1))·x² + (4z − 1)·x` that passes through the colour's own lightness z at mid-grey ([KoColorSpacePreserveLightnessUtils.h](https://github.com/KDE/krita/blob/afc124763282f5387735760cdda838c1c81545a1/libs/pigment/KoColorSpacePreserveLightnessUtils.h)). **[Code]** One TypeScript and WebGL2 oil simulator is the closest thing to a worked design for stamp-by-stamp impasto (eight upstream samples to drag paint along the motion, volume-weighted K/S mixing, a bristle imprint that moves height without creating it), but its licence file credits an AI model as author and it has no track record, so it should be read as a sketch, not a reference ([deposit.ts](https://github.com/Remotional/impasto-oil-painting-simulator/blob/85dbab1da1999a72dc7f19f0a0ea5937bd234721/src/shaders/deposit.ts), [LICENSE](https://github.com/Remotional/impasto-oil-painting-simulator/blob/85dbab1da1999a72dc7f19f0a0ea5937bd234721/LICENSE)).

**[Code]** No real-time code base needs a scanned paper. Paper height is synthesised from three to six octaves of smooth random noise plus a stretched "fibre" term (p5.brush stretches its third term 9:1), and canvas from a product of two sine waves for warp and weft ([paper_height.glsl](https://github.com/acamposuribe/p5.brush/blob/ac2d829c760fcd677012caab95da002ad5aa692e/src/fill/paper_height.glsl), [paper.rs](https://github.com/jfernand/curtis-watercolor/blob/31b66762bed4c6b379d9a9533cd1083ab928fbba/src/paper.rs#L24-L58)). The same height map does four jobs across the sources: it shifts where a wash edge halts, weights where pigment settles, breaks up thin or dry strokes so only the peaks take paint, and changes how fast water is absorbed. **[Code]** Krita's texture option keeps the grain fixed to the canvas while the dab moves, and its "height" mode makes harder pressure push paint deeper into the valleys ([kis_texture_option.cpp](https://github.com/KDE/krita/blob/afc124763282f5387735760cdda838c1c81545a1/plugins/paintops/libpaintop/kis_texture_option.cpp)). **[Design]** Bake one tileable paper texture at start-up and sample it in every pass; a single dry-brush rule (deposit only where load times pressure exceeds the depth of the valley) is derived from these sources but is not a verbatim formula from any of them.

### Drying, glazing and the fade options

**[Code]** Open code dries paint in three ways. The first is a timer (60 frames per touched region, above). The second is a wetness number that falls with time, more slowly where paint is thick: one oil simulator uses `wet −= rate·dt/(0.30 + 2.4·height)`, and a small MIT watercolour loses water at `0.45 × (0.35 + 0.75·paper)` per second while fixing pigment at `0.06·dt` plus the fraction of water just lost ([field.ts](https://github.com/Remotional/impasto-oil-painting-simulator/blob/85dbab1da1999a72dc7f19f0a0ea5937bd234721/src/shaders/field.ts), [watercolor.c](https://github.com/alderjhuynh/watercolor/blob/d33afcc5949a656982e37fdbf2281a4ca416da5d/watercolor.c)). The third is a counter per event: p5.brush multiplies a wash's wetness by 0.45 for each later wash ([gl_fill.js](https://github.com/acamposuribe/p5.brush/blob/ac2d829c760fcd677012caab95da002ad5aa692e/src/fill/gl_fill.js#L420-L436)). All of these constants are aesthetic; no repository contains drying times measured from real media.

**[Code]** Layering dry paint is done either physically, with the layer formula `R = R1 + T1²·R2/(1 − R1·R2)`, or cheaply as multiplied absorbance, `colour × exp(−2.1 × pigment mass)`, which does not depend on the order of layers ([pigment.rs](https://github.com/jfernand/curtis-watercolor/blob/31b66762bed4c6b379d9a9533cd1083ab928fbba/src/pigment.rs#L73-L82), [watercolor.c](https://github.com/alderjhuynh/watercolor/blob/d33afcc5949a656982e37fdbf2281a4ca416da5d/watercolor.c)). Ordinary alpha blending is what the polygon-stack sketches use and is what gives chalky overlaps. **[Design]** A two-layer scheme follows naturally: a dry density layer and a simulated wet layer, displayed as `paper × exp(−(dry + wet))`, so paint over a dry area glazes while paint into a wet area mixes before it sets.

**[Code]** Deliberate fading exists only in visualisers: a fluid's dye divided by `1 + dissipation × dt`, a watercolour audio visualiser multiplying dye by 0.992 per step, and a preset visualiser multiplying the previous frame by 0.98 ([script.js](https://github.com/PavelDoGreat/WebGL-Fluid-Simulation/blob/a2d292931f19d9b3b9f564e23e6c32729d2121c3/script.js#L746-L783), [butterchurn](https://github.com/jberg/butterchurn)). **[Computed]** At 60 frames a second those halve the paint in about 1.4 s and 0.6 s, so a song-length painting cannot simply slow them down. **[Design]** Four fade options are worth offering, none found in any source. Fading in density space, `D ← D·exp(−dt/τ)` with τ from tens of seconds to minutes, lightens old paint without the grey veil that fading opacity toward white leaves, because density is multiplied on display. Fading to a floor (down to 30–50% of original contrast and no further) lets old passages sink back into an underpainting instead of vanishing. Fading by section lowers the saturation and contrast of earlier sections one step each time a new one starts. "Dry, then glaze" involves no fade at all: paint is workable for a few seconds, then fixes, and later paint tints it optically; this is also the surest protection against mud.

### What WebGL2 asks for

**[Code]** The recurring recipe across the code bases is a WebGL2 context with no depth, stencil or antialiasing; the `EXT_color_buffer_float` extension (or its half-float sibling) to render into 16-bit float textures, verified by building a tiny framebuffer and checking it is complete; ping-pong texture pairs; scissored dirty rectangles; simulation at a fraction of display resolution; and no passes at all when nothing is wet ([script.js](https://github.com/PavelDoGreat/WebGL-Fluid-Simulation/blob/a2d292931f19d9b3b9f564e23e6c32729d2121c3/script.js#L118-L206), [PaintField.ts](https://github.com/Remotional/impasto-oil-painting-simulator/blob/85dbab1da1999a72dc7f19f0a0ea5937bd234721/src/field/PaintField.ts#L11-L25)). **[Code]** One project avoids float textures entirely, keeping wetness, deposit and masks as 8-bit "heat maps" and capping work per frame by device: at most 15 brush stamps per frame on touch devices and 120 on desktop, wet passes every second frame on touch ([device-profile.js](https://github.com/food211/Mixbox-Palette/blob/9915243c08207fc366a4059e4c548f9f5ddc1aa8/js/device-profile.js), [wetpaper.js](https://github.com/food211/Mixbox-Palette/blob/9915243c08207fc366a4059e4c548f9f5ddc1aa8/js/wetpaper.js)). **[Design]** That suggests a fallback ladder: float textures where available; 8-bit heat maps where rendering to float is refused; and, on plain Canvas 2D, a small grid of wet cells updated in typed arrays or washes pre-rendered once per event. A hard cap on stamps and particles per frame matters more for a music painter than for a hand-driven one, because a dense passage can otherwise demand unlimited paint in one frame; none of the code bases read coordinates rendering with audio.

## Hearing: your own FFT, off the audio thread, with a start and an end for every sound

**In plain words.** The browser's built-in analyser is a display tool. It smears every sound over a tenth of a second or more, reports only when the screen asks, and never says that a sound has ended, which is exactly what a painter needs to know to choose between a touch and a stroke. The workable design taps the audio in an AudioWorklet, hands the samples to a separate worker thread, and analyses them there with three window lengths at once: long for bass, where notes sit close together, short for treble and for timing. Starts of sounds are found by a well-tested method that looks for sudden rises of energy in each stretch of the frequency scale and ignores vibrato. Whether a sound is short or held cannot be known at its start; it takes about 40–100 ms of watching the level fall or hold. Tone versus noise is one cheap number per band. Mood needs several seconds and is trustworthy for energy, much less so for happy-versus-sad. Because sound reaches the ears later than it reaches the analyser, the painting can be scheduled to land exactly with the sound, and a file can be analysed ahead of time.

### Why the built-in analyser cannot do this

**[Code]** The Web Audio specification fixes the AnalyserNode's behaviour: a Blackman window over the most recent `fftSize` samples, magnitudes only, smoothing between calls by a constant that defaults to 0.8, and no new data if it is asked twice within one audio block ([Web Audio specification](https://github.com/WebAudio/web-audio-api/blob/42e974a/index.bs)). **[Computed]** The consequences: the time between analyses is the time between screen frames, about 16.7 ms and uneven; a smoothing constant of 0.8 at 60 calls a second is a 75 ms time constant; an 8,192-sample window is 171 ms long at 48 kHz with its centre half of that in the past; and there is no phase, so the more sensitive onset methods are impossible. **[Code]** A popular open spectrum visualiser built on the analyser uses exactly that 8,192 window and accepts the smear for the sake of bass resolution ([audioMotion-analyzer.js](https://github.com/hvianna/audioMotion-analyzer/blob/bd226d1/src/audioMotion-analyzer.js)). **[Computed]** Any chain with that much smoothing turns a 30 ms hit into a plateau of 200 ms or more, which is the most likely reason a painting draws long strokes for staccato sounds.

**[Code]** An AudioWorklet receives 128 samples at a time (2.67 ms at 48 kHz) and can stamp each block with the exact audio-clock time ([Web Audio specification](https://github.com/WebAudio/web-audio-api/blob/42e974a/index.bs)). **[Design]** The recommended structure is for the worklet only to copy each block into a ring buffer shared with a dedicated worker, where all FFT work runs so that the audio thread can never stall. **[Computed]** A three-resolution analysis at 48 kHz fits the physics: 4,096 samples hopping by 512 for everything below about 250 Hz (43 ms centre delay, 11.7 Hz per bin), 1,024 hopping by 256 for the middle (11 ms), and 256 hopping by 128 for the top (2.7 ms), all mapped onto a common grid of 24 bands to the octave; together they cost about 7.3 million FFT butterfly operations per second, a small load. **[Code]** The alternatives are heavier for timing work: a constant-Q sliding transform (MIT, no JavaScript version) updates every sample but **[Computed]** its windows are still 100 ms or longer below 100 Hz and it costs roughly four times as much. The trade between sharp timing and sharp pitch in the bass is physical, not a software limit. **[Design]** For bass timing, add a path with no window at all: a band-pass filter, a rectifier and a fast envelope.

### Starts of sounds: rises of energy per band, 15–30 ms late

**[Code]** Every open library uses the same three stages: a log-compressed spectrum on a log-frequency band grid; the positive part of the difference from a frame 5–10 ms earlier; and a peak picker that asks for a local maximum that also stands above a moving average. The SuperFlux variant compares each band with the maximum of three neighbouring bands in the earlier frame, which stops vibrato from firing false starts; its reference implementation (BSD-2-Clause source) uses 200 frames a second, 24 bands to the octave from 30 Hz to 17 kHz, and for live use sets all look-ahead to zero and suppresses any start within 30 ms of the last ([onsets.py](https://github.com/CPJKU/madmom/blob/27f032e/madmom/features/onsets.py#L196-L240), [SuperFlux program](https://github.com/CPJKU/madmom/blob/27f032e/bin/SuperFlux#L55-L75)). **[Code]** aubio (GPL-3.0-or-later) supplies the other useful parts: a complex-domain method that uses phase and is more sensitive to soft, pitched note changes; per-bin "adaptive whitening" that divides each bin by its own slowly decaying peak so thresholds behave alike on quiet and loud material; and a seven-value peak picker ([specdesc.c](https://github.com/aubio/aubio/blob/ad5cf97/src/spectral/specdesc.c#L90-L240), [awhitening.c](https://github.com/aubio/aubio/blob/ad5cf97/src/spectral/awhitening.c#L27-L88), [peakpicker.c](https://github.com/aubio/aubio/blob/ad5cf97/src/onset/peakpicker.c#L92-L187)). **[Code]** A causal picker with two rules, signal above average plus 0.05 or signal more than 16 times the average, handles quiet passages; it lives in an AGPL library and is cited for the formula only ([superfluxpeaks.h](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/blob/23ae7ba/src/algorithms/rhythm/superfluxpeaks.h#L65-L72)).

| Detector and settings | Look-ahead in the picker | Total delay at 44.1 kHz [Computed] |
|---|---|---|
| aubio, window 512, hop 256 | 2 hops | about 12–23 ms |
| SuperFlux live, window 1,024, hop 5 ms | none | about 17 ms |
| SuperFlux live, window 2,048, hop 5 ms | none | about 28 ms |
| SuperFlux offline | 50 ms | about 78 ms |
| librosa defaults (offline) | 100 ms | about 170 ms |

**[Code]** No library read ships a ready-made live detector that reports starts separately per frequency region; the one that offers per-band envelopes is offline and normalises over the whole clip ([onset.py](https://github.com/librosa/librosa/blob/76f10b8/librosa/onset.py#L451-L665)). **[Design]** Summing the SuperFlux differences into six to eight band groups, each with its own causal picker, is therefore a design, not a port, and its threshold should be a "sensitivity" control tuned by ear. One practical trap: the MIT feature library Meyda has no onset detector, and **[Computed]** its spectral-flux function indexes a typed array with negative numbers, which yields NaN, so it should be re-implemented, not copied ([spectralFlux.ts](https://github.com/meyda/meyda/blob/ecf2566/src/extractors/spectralFlux.ts)).

### Short or held: the decision that takes 40–100 ms

**[Code]** No library outputs "staccato" or "sustained". The building blocks are a level-drop rule (aubio ends a note when the level falls 10 dB below its level at the start), an "effective duration" (the time an envelope stays above 40% of its peak), and an envelope follower with separate attack and release times whose default release of 1.5 s is far too slow for this purpose ([notes.c](https://github.com/aubio/aubio/blob/ad5cf97/src/notes/notes.c#L27-L245), [effectiveduration.h](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/blob/23ae7ba/src/algorithms/temporal/effectiveduration.h), [envelope.cpp](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/blob/23ae7ba/src/algorithms/standard/envelope.cpp)). **[Code]** Two further tools separate what rings from what clicks. Median filtering splits a spectrum into a harmonic part (steady along time) and a percussive part (spread along frequency); an MIT real-time version uses a harmonic filter seven frames long, keeps only the last four frames (about 46 ms at 44.1 kHz), and applies hard masks with a ratio of 2 ([hpss_rt.py](https://github.com/sevagh/Real-Time-HPSS/blob/master/python/hpss_rt/hpss_rt.py)). Partial tracking follows each spectral peak from frame to frame, matching a peak to an existing track when their frequencies differ by less than **20 Hz plus 1% of the frequency**; a track that finds no match has ended ([sineModel.py](https://github.com/MTG/sms-tools/blob/8b685e6/smstools/models/sineModel.py)). That source is AGPL, so the rule is taken as a formula. **[Computed]** A track's end is known one hop after its last peak, and the tolerance is wide enough (about 93 cents per hop at 440 Hz) to follow bends and vibrato.

**[Computed]** The percussive side of the median split needs no history, so a broadband click scores high in the very frame it starts; the harmonic side only "believes" a tone after it has lasted about half the filter length, 45–100 ms. Together with the level-drop rules this is where the 40–100 ms figure comes from. It is derived from filter lengths and hop sizes, not from a published evaluation. **[Design]** The proposed listener is a small state machine per band. At a start it opens a provisional event, records the peak level of the first 15–20 ms and reports at once. It then watches for up to about 100 ms with a fast follower (release 20–30 ms): a fall of 6–10 dB within about 60 ms means short; a fall of less than 3 dB after 80–100 ms together with tonal evidence (a live partial track, or a low noisiness score) means held; anything between is classed by how fast it decays. A held event ends when its partial tracks die, when the level falls 10–20 dB below the start, or when a new start arrives in the same band. Only the 10 dB drop, the 40% rule, the 20 Hz plus 1% tolerance and the median lengths come from code; every other threshold is a proposal to tune by ear. No open JavaScript or TypeScript real-time harmonic-percussive splitter or partial tracker was found, so these are ports of MIT code or re-implementations from formulas.

### Tone or noise, how loud, and what mood

**[Code]** Noise versus tone is spectral flatness, the geometric mean of the spectrum divided by its arithmetic mean: near 0 for a pure tone, near 1 for noise; the related "crest" (maximum over average) is high when one partial dominates ([spectralFlatness.ts](https://github.com/meyda/meyda/blob/ecf2566/src/extractors/spectralFlatness.ts), [spectral.py](https://github.com/librosa/librosa/blob/76f10b8/librosa/feature/spectral.py#L720-L838)). Computed per band, with the percussive share from the median split, it tells a hi-hat from a held note in the same register.

**[Code]** Loudness that matches hearing uses the two-filter "K-weighting" (a high shelf of about +4 dB at 1,682 Hz and a high-pass at 38 Hz) followed by `−0.691 + 10·log10(mean square)`, over 400 ms for "momentary" and 3 s for "short-term" loudness; an MIT library computes the filter coefficients for any sample rate ([ebur128.c](https://github.com/jiixyj/libebur128/blob/master/ebur128/ebur128.c#L453-L495)). **[Computed]** A-weighting is the wrong tool for comparing bands on a display: it sits at −19 dB at 100 Hz and −30 dB at 50 Hz, which would make kick and bass nearly invisible. **[Design]** Use three time scales: a fast K-weighted level over 50–100 ms per band for the size of each gesture; the 400 ms level for overall intensity; and the 3 s level plus a slow running mean only to normalise the other two, so a quiet recording uses the full visual range. For comparing bass with treble, per-band whitening (each band against its own recent peak) is the adaptive answer. A fixed tilt of 3 or 4.5 dB per octave is a common display default, but no source read here backs either figure.

**[Code]** The only open code found with actual regression weights for mood is in MIRtoolbox, which is GPL-2.0-only, so the published model may be used but not the code. Its "activity" score adds loudness (weight 0.67), the strength of the dominant rhythmic pulse (0.61), spectral brightness (0.45) and spectral entropy (0.71) and subtracts spectral spread (0.46); its "valence" score leans on key clarity (0.88), major-versus-minor (0.46) and pulse strength (0.61) and falls with loudness variability (0.32). The file itself warns that the model is not correctly calibrated for current versions of the toolbox ([miremotion.m](https://github.com/olivierlar/mirtoolbox/blob/71d6265/MIRToolbox/%40miremotion/miremotion.m)). **[Unread]** One regression study reached R² of about 0.63 for arousal against 0.36 for valence on 194 clips, the usual pattern: energy is predictable from sound, pleasantness much less so ([review](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.13369), [regression paper](https://scitepress.org/Papers/2016/58265/pdf/index.html)). **[Code]** Key and mode come from correlating a twelve-note profile of the sound against major and minor templates rotated to each tonic ([key.cpp](https://github.com/MTG/essentia/blob/23ae7ba/src/algorithms/tonal/key.cpp#L85-L130)); **[Design]** that needs several seconds of sound and should be treated as slow state with hysteresis, never as an instant reaction. **[Code]** For beats, BTrack (GPL-3.0-or-later) is a causal tracker that predicts each beat half a beat ahead but folds every tempo into 80–160 beats per minute, and aubio's tracker works on a window of about 6 s, updated every 1.5 s or so ([BTrack.cpp](https://github.com/adamstark/BTrack/blob/9d61276/src/BTrack.cpp), [tempo.c](https://github.com/aubio/aubio/blob/ad5cf97/src/tempo/tempo.c#L166-L212)); the neural beat trackers of one popular library depend on model files under a non-commercial licence ([madmom LICENSE](https://github.com/CPJKU/madmom/blob/27f032e/LICENSE)).

### Landing the paint with the sound

**[Code]** An analysis node sees samples before they are heard. The specification defines `baseLatency` and `outputLatency` for that gap, warns that output latency "may change while the context is running", and provides `getOutputTimestamp()`, which pairs the audio-clock time of the sample now leaving the device with a `performance.now()` time ([Web Audio specification](https://github.com/WebAudio/web-audio-api/blob/42e974a/index.bs)). **[Design]** Stamp every event with its audio-clock time minus the known analysis delay, and each animation frame release the events whose time has become audible, with a lead of about one display frame; fall back to `currentTime − outputLatency` where the timestamp is missing. No open project was found that documents such a scheme end to end; it is derived from the specification. **[Design]** For a file, a delay of 50–100 ms on the audible path only, with the analysis tapped before it, lets the short-or-held decision arrive before the note is heard; for live playing that delay is unacceptable and the provisional-touch approach is the only one.

**[Code]** `decodeAudioData` returns the whole file as samples at the context's rate, in any format the browser's audio element supports; Chromium decodes FLAC, MP3, Opus, Vorbis and PCM in every build and AAC only in builds with proprietary codecs ([Web Audio specification](https://github.com/WebAudio/web-audio-api/blob/42e974a/index.bs), [supported_types.cc](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/media/base/supported_types.cc)). **[Computed]** Five minutes of stereo at 48 kHz is 115 MB in memory. **[Design]** With the whole file in hand, the most accurate option is to analyse it ahead in a worker with look-ahead (centred filters, look-ahead peak picking, whole-file key and tempo) and simply play the resulting event list against the audio clock, keeping the live path for live input and for the app's own sound; for the app's own notes, the app already knows the true start and end and should use them.

## Joining them: what listeners agree on, what is taste, and how one event becomes one mark

**In plain words.** Join the two halves through events, not through a spectrum. Each sound the listener finds arrives with a start, a place on the frequency scale, a loudness, a noisiness and, a little later, an end; each becomes one gesture of a brush whose physical settings (how much paint, how wet, how hard, how fast, how splayed) are set from those measurements. Research on how people pair sound with sight is firm on a few links and silent on others, and the app should be honest about which is which: several of its most natural-seeming rules are your taste as a painter, not findings. Variety that still looks like one hand comes from a seed, from slow drifts instead of independent dice, and from a direction the strokes tend to follow. A whole song on one canvas needs a paint budget, or it turns to mud.

### The evidence behind each link

The psychology here is **[Unread]** throughout: the researchers saw abstracts and search summaries, not full papers. Grades are the researchers' own: A means replicated or cross-cultural, B several studies with caveats, C one study or indirect, D no controlled evidence found.

Three consequences are worth stating plainly. First, the two rules at the heart of this app, short sounds as touches and held sounds as strokes, have no experimental support as visual claims; they are sound as analogy and should simply be owned as the app's idea. Second, calm-equals-wet rests on a chain of indirect links (soft timbres pair with rounded shapes, legato reads as calm, quick hard strokes read as energetic in painting-teaching sources) and is a hypothesis to test by eye, best exposed as a switchable "manner". Third, hue should come from mood and harmony, not from pitch: the canvas already spends height, and gently lightness and size, on pitch, and the research gives hue no acoustic partner. **[Code]** If a harmonic tint is wanted, two open projects show the gentlest forms: pitch classes ordered round the circle of fifths so related notes get neighbouring hues, and the key used only as a tint of at most ±0.06 of the hue wheel on top of a mood colour ([watercolor-audio](https://github.com/komei222/watercolor-audio), [music-reactive-lighting](https://github.com/CanYuzbey/music-reactive-lighting)); the first has no licence, so only the idea is usable.

### From a heard event to a brush gesture

**[Design]** Everything in this subsection is a proposal built from the sourced parts above. At the start of a sound, put down a small touch at the height of its frequency, sized by its first 15–20 ms of level: time-stamped dabs with no travel. If the listener then reports "short", lift: the touch stays a dab, a round dot for a tone, and for a noise a spatter whose droplet sizes follow the log-normal law with a spread growing from 0.3 to 1.5 as noisiness rises. If it reports "held", start travelling from the touch: switch to distance-based stamping at five or six dabs per radius with long direction-locked dabs or bristle lines, let pressure follow the band's fast level so a swell fattens the stroke and a fade thins it, let the brush's load fall with distance so the stroke breaks up on the paper grain as the note dies, and lift when the note ends. A sound that decays without being cut (a plucked string) becomes a head with a thinning tail. A partial track's frequency drives the stroke's height from moment to moment, so a bend is a slope and vibrato a ripple. A new start in the same band while the old note still sounds ends one stroke and begins the next, joined.

The physical variables each measurement sets are few, and they are the same ones painting-teaching sources and the brush engines converge on **[Unread]** ([technique guide](https://www1.udel.edu/artfoundations/beg_painting/techniques.html)): load, dilution, pressure over time, speed, direction, bristle stiffness, dab spacing, pickup of paint already on the canvas, and whether the surface beneath is wet. **[Design]** Loudness goes to pressure, width and load. Noisiness and roughness go to scatter, grain and bristle splay. Brightness of timbre goes to lightness and saturation within the palette. Mood goes, slowly, to dilution and drying time if the calm-is-wet manner is switched on. **[Code]** The p5.brush pressure bell, slow to rise and quicker to release with its peak placed differently on every stroke, is a ready-made shape for a note's swell and release ([stroke.js](https://github.com/acamposuribe/p5.brush/blob/ac2d829c760fcd677012caab95da002ad5aa692e/src/stroke/stroke.js)). Because the engines derive taper, speed-thinning, lag and tremor from pressure and timing, synthesising a pressure curve and a speed curve per event delivers those for free.

### Mood sets the palette slowly; build it in a perceptual colour space

**[Unread]** Musical cues combine mostly linearly and additively in listeners' emotion judgements (77–89% of variance), ranked mode, tempo, register, dynamics, articulation, timbre ([study](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/full)), and a classic colour study relates pleasure to brightness (0.69) and saturation (0.22) and arousal to saturation (0.60) and negatively to brightness (−0.31) ([study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7996122/)). **[Code]** One MIT prototype shows a complete working version: arousal as 0.5 × loudness + 0.4 × density + 0.1 × onset, saturation as 0.4 + 0.6 × arousal, hue blended between a warm arc and a cool arc by arousal, and, when arousal is high, the colour held 95% to a slow palette so it cannot flicker; its README says the values "are not validated measurements of a listener's emotion" ([music-reactive-lighting](https://github.com/CanYuzbey/music-reactive-lighting)). **[Design]** A weighted sum is therefore defensible: strong, fast mappings for energy (saturation, contrast, stroke energy) and weak, slow ones for valence (lightness, a yellow-to-blue lean), computed over several seconds, with the palette held at section level and single notes varying only inside it. **[Code]** Generative-art rule sets add the craft: never interpolate raw screen colour because "midpoints go gray and muddy"; build three to five colours around a ground at roughly 60/30/10 proportions with the accent under about 15% of pixels ([palette rules](https://github.com/Pardesco/algorithmic-art-skill)); the licence of that rule set was not recorded, so take the ideas only. **[Design]** The widths of hue sweep suggested for calm versus energetic moods (30–60° versus 120–180°) are the researchers' proposal.

### Unpredictable, yet one hand

**[Code]** Hand-made-looking output in open code follows a consistent recipe: one seeded random source with separate sub-streams, smooth variation at several scales in place of independent jitter, a direction field that strokes follow with inertia, and per-stroke redrawing of the pressure curve and opacity. One MIT painter steers each particle by adding a noise-field direction to its velocity and renormalising, so direction eases instead of snapping, and includes the background colour in its palette "so some strokes may seem to erase the other colours" ([color-wander](https://github.com/mattdesl/color-wander)). A bristle-level MIT oil painter varies each bristle's brightness by ±9% ([ofxOilPaint](https://github.com/jagracar/ofxOilPaint)). **[Unread]** Human timing errors are long-range correlated, and listeners preferred correlated "humanising" to uncorrelated jitter ([study](https://dx.doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0026457)); melodies made from 1/f noise were judged more pleasing than those from white noise ([study](https://www.nature.com/articles/258317a0)). **[Design]** Carrying that from timing to brushwork is an assumption, not a tested result, but it suggests three nested scales: constants drawn once per painting (brush family, palette, how wet the painter works), slow drifts over sections (dominant stroke angle, loading habit, hue bias), and narrow per-stroke deviations around the drift, with pure white noise kept for the finest grain only. Seed the painting from a hash of the audio plus a visible run number, so the same file and number reproduce the painting and a new number paints the same music afresh; derive each mark's randomness from its own identity (start time and frequency), not from the order of drawing, so changing one mapping does not reshuffle everything else. A tool-choice habit (a 0.7–0.85 chance of keeping the current brush) is an untested starting guess; no open painting code using such a chain was found.

### A whole song on one canvas

**[Code]** One automatic oil painter rejects strokes by coverage: it stops adding to a region once 0.65 of the stroke's path is already painted and rejects a stroke that would destroy more than 0.4 of well-painted pixels, working from large brushes to small ([ofxOilSimulator.cpp](https://github.com/jagracar/ofxOilPaint)). Generative rule sets ask to "reserve 20–40% of the canvas as near-empty ground" and to drive density, weight and contrast from one importance map ([composition rules](https://github.com/Pardesco/algorithmic-art-skill)). **[Unread]** Painting-teaching sources put mud down to over-mixing and to more than three or four pigments in one mixture ([guide](https://drawpaintacademy.com/muddy-colors/)). **[Design]** No source describes a paint budget for music paintings; the proposal assembled from these is to track coverage and thickness per pixel, thin or break up new marks as a region nears the cap, let at most three or four pigments mix in any wet region, keep the darkest and most saturated paint for the loudest events, and pace dense passages so they cannot unload all their paint in one place at once.

**[Design]** With height fixed to frequency, time has to go somewhere. Left to right across the width is the most readable but gives each moment a thin column and little painterly overlap. Repeated passes over the same canvas, each section painting over the last after it has dried and sunk back, gives the strongest layering and needs the fade and budget rules above. A freely drifting brush position is the most painterly and loses time order. **[Unread]** The direction of a time axis follows reading direction ([study](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/record/344/files/Pitt_uchicago_0330D_14349.pdf)), so left-to-right should be a default that can be mirrored. No study comparing such layouts was found.

### Where the working tree already stands

This paragraph is the report writer's own reading of file headers and four text searches in the project on 18 November 2026, not part of the research notes. The uncommitted code in `src/listen` and `src/canvas` already follows the architecture recommended here: an ear with three window lengths that follows each tone from start to stop, a wet-paint simulation with wet, water and dry layers on 16-bit float textures, a hand that gives each tone a brush for as long as it sounds, and fade choices measured in passes of the music. Four points in it meet the findings above directly. The whole analysis runs inside the AudioWorklet's `process()` call (`src/listen/ear.worklet.ts`), whereas the notes recommend only copying there and analysing in a worker. Pigment is held as K/S worked out from each of the three screen-colour channels (`ksOf` in `src/canvas/sim.ts`), the same form as the fourth column of the computed table, which gave the darkest mixes. The level tilt of 4.5 dB per octave (`src/listen/ear.ts`) is the figure no source backs. And the "Auto" paint style, which runs held, quiet music wet and loud, clipped music thick and dry (`src/canvas/painting.ts`), is exactly the calm-is-wet mapping that has no research behind it; it is a fair artistic choice and should be described to users as one. Timing uses `currentTime − outputLatency`, the fallback form; `getOutputTimestamp()` appears nowhere in `src`.

## Honest limits: GitHub only, no full papers, nothing rendered, heard or timed

**In plain words.** Treat every number here as a starting point taken from someone else's working code, and every mapping from sound to paint as a hypothesis, until you have seen and heard it yourself.

**Only GitHub was reachable.** The researchers could fetch github.com and raw file contents and nothing else; journal sites, preprint servers, encyclopaedias, web archives, documentation sites for Krita and MyPaint, the generative-art essay sites and all live demos were blocked. GitHub's code search was unavailable, so projects that do not carry the searched words in their name or description will have been missed.

**Papers were not read in full.** The watercolour, ink and bristle-brush papers, the onset-detection and source-separation papers and the loudness standards were not opened; their equations are reported as transcribed by open implementations, cross-checked between two independent ports where two exist (which is how the viscosity-sign disagreement surfaced). All the psychology and all the painting-teaching material come from abstracts and search-result summaries; effect sizes beyond the few quoted are missing, and one abstract on pitch and height was internally inconsistent. Some constants that look authoritative are not: the ink-model port's "tuned" values and the capillary constants of the watercolour port are their authors' own.

**Nothing was rendered or listened to.** No brush, wash or mix was drawn; statements that a parameter set resembles a real medium are judgements from the numbers. The colour-mixing table is arithmetic, not a viewing test. No detector was run on music: there are no accuracy figures for the live onset settings, and the short-versus-held thresholds have never met a real mix, let alone a dense one where many sounds overlap in a band.

**No browser frame times exist.** No repository publishes measurements on named hardware, and the researchers took none. Every cost statement is a count of passes or operations. Whether the full chain (three FFTs, tracking, simulation, display) holds 60 frames a second on an ordinary laptop is unmeasured, as is the share of devices that allow rendering to float textures.

**Platform behaviour is unverified.** The accuracy of reported output latency (especially over Bluetooth), codec support in the system webviews a Tauri app uses on each operating system, and whether a worklet with no outputs keeps running in every engine were not checked.

**Several sources are thin.** Some of the most relevant repositories have no reviewers or a handful of stars; one is AI-generated. Parts of larger code bases were located but not transcribed: Krita's default pixel-brush loop and its stock presets, parts of p5.brush's fill code, and MIRtoolbox's internals.

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the problem more than it supplies a library to install. The paint side turns out to be cheaper than expected: the convincing results in open code come from event-driven deposits, a flow braked almost to a standstill, a height channel and pigment-aware colour, with cost that follows the number of new marks and not the size of the canvas. The hearing side turns out to hinge on one thing the usual visualiser never computes, the end of a sound, and on accepting a wait of a few hundredths of a second before knowing what kind of mark a sound deserves. Joined, they suggest a painter that commits in two steps, touch first and stroke only if the sound earns it, which is also how a hand behaves.

The honest position on meaning is that science backs the scaffolding (height, lightness, size, roughness, mood colour) and leaves the poetry to you. That is an advantage: the unsupported mappings are exactly where a musician's taste should show, provided they are switchable and described as choices. The next work is therefore measurement, not more reading: a sheet of at least nine seeds of the same song judged by eye, the short-versus-held listener run against real mixes with its thresholds on sliders, the three mixing methods compared on screen with the app's own palettes, and frame times logged on the weakest machine the app must serve.
