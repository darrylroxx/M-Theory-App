# M-Theory 🎸

**Your own guitar chord, key, mode & progression lab. No subscriptions, no ads, works offline.**

M-Theory answers the questions that matter when you're writing songs on guitar:

- *"I'm in B. What chords can I use?"* All of them: diatonic, colour chords, borrowed, chromatic.
- *"How else can I play this chord?"* The shapes guitarists really use, starting from the common ones and branching out, not just the barre shape.
- *"What could come next?"* Suggestions from safe to wild, each with a plain-English reason.
- *"What mode is this, and how do I make it sound like that mode?"*
- *"What does it sound like over a beat?"* A drum machine with a library of about 115 synthesized sounds, 18 kits, 8 classic machines, 100+ rhythms, studio-style effects, and a separate rhythm for every section of the song.

---

## What's inside

**How the app is laid out.** One page: the **track view**. A bar across the top (play, loop, where the music is, the key, the tempo, the time signature, undo and redo, the idea's name and **Save**, **Export**, **Canvas**, **Ideas** and settings), a ruler in bars, and under it a row for every track: its panel on the left, its lane of clips on the right. There are no sections any more: a song is tracks and clips on one line of time. **Space** (or **▶**) plays from the cursor to the end of the song; pressed again it stops, and the play line is back on the cursor, so pressing twice hears the same spot again. Click the ruler, or an empty part of a lane, to put the cursor there (while the song plays, the music starts again from there). **Drag across the ruler to loop** those bars; Ctrl + drag adds another stretch, and the music then goes round the stretches one after the other. **↻** (or **L**) switches the loop off and on. With the loop on and the cursor inside it, the music starts at the cursor; a click outside the loop while it plays only moves the cursor. The wheel scrolls the tracks, Shift + wheel scrolls time, Ctrl + wheel zooms about the pointer, and **fit** shows the whole song. **Follow** turns the lanes a page when the play line leaves them. The names on the ruler are markers (**M** adds one at the cursor; double-click one to loop from it to the next): the names the sections of older ideas had are there, each where its section started.

**On a phone.** On a narrow screen, or any screen under a finger, the same track view is laid out for a finger: nothing in it needs a second mouse button, a held key, a pointer that hovers, or a keyboard. (The desktop layout is as it was.)
- **The top bar** is two short rows: ▶, ↻, ● (once a track records), where the music is, ↶ ↷ and **⋯**; under them the key, the tempo, the signature and the idea with **Save**. Held sideways it is one row. **⋯** holds Ideas, Canvas, Settings and the three exports, and what a mouse does by a right-click on the ruler or a key: Follow, show the whole song, select every clip (or the clips in the loop), paste at the cursor, a marker at the cursor (and rename or delete the selected one), a bar put in at the cursor, the looped time put in or taken out, loop the whole song, clear the loop; in the desktop app also how you hear yourself while recording.
- **A track's panel** is its name, the bass clef, **M** and **S**. A tap on the name opens everything else of the track: sound and effects, hear it without its effects, the volume (a slider, with a way back to 0), on a track that records the arm switch and the input, on a harmony track ⟳, rename, move up, move down, duplicate, copy and paste its sound, its colour, remove. An armed track has a red dot by its name.
- **Time.** **− fit +** over the panels zoom, and so do two fingers on the lanes. A swipe sideways over the lanes moves through the song (the bar under the lanes does too); up and down scrolls the tracks. A tap on the ruler or on an empty lane places the cursor. A finger dragged across the ruler loops that stretch; the stretch's ends and its ✕ are finger sized. A finger rested on the ruler opens the ruler's menu, where **Add this bar to the loop** adds a second stretch. Zoomed in to where the beat lines show, a finger moves clips, their edges, the loop and the cursor to the beat instead of the bar.
- **Clips.** A tap selects a clip: its six buttons stand by it (open, mute, split at the cursor, duplicate, copy, delete) and its two ends become handles to drag, which repeat or shorten it. **A finger rested on a clip lifts it**: dragged from there it moves, onto another track of its kind too; let go where it is, its menu opens, with the same six and **Repeat once more** and **Repeat once less** (the right edge as two lines). A quick swipe over a clip never moves it. *Several clips at once:* with one selected, rest a finger on another and its menu starts with **Select with the other one**; ⋯ has **Select every clip** (with a loop: the clips in the loop). The buttons, and a lifted drag, then act on all of them. A finger rested on an empty lane: **Paste here** and **New clip here**.
- **+ Add track** opens a list of the six kinds. Every menu is a list across the bottom of the screen, each line a finger high; a tap beside it only puts it away.
- **Windows are sheets.** A composer, a sound window and the record window each fill the screen under the top bar (so play, undo and redo stay in reach), one at a time, and scroll inside themselves. A composer's strip has **Place**: a tap lays the composed clip on its own track at the cursor's bar and moves the cursor to its end, so a second tap lays the next one after it. The clip can be dragged out as well: rest a finger on it, and the sheet turns see-through so it can be dropped on any lane of its kind. In the Melody and Bass composer a finger rested on a note takes hold of its length (dragged sideways from there the note is made longer or shorter); a tap still puts a note in or takes it out, and a swipe scrolls the octaves.
- Recording with the phone's microphone, the ideas list, backup files, settings and the Canvas are the same as in a desktop browser.

**Tracks and clips.** Six kinds of track, any number of each: Chords, Melody, Bass, Drums, Vocals and Blank (an empty audio track, named "Blank 1", "Blank 2" as they are added). **+ Add track** under the last row adds one. A clip lies on one track, from a beat, for a length, and holds what a composer writes: chords, a melody, a drum rhythm (or a stretch of a recording). A clip only ever lies on a track of its own kind. Drag a clip to move it (onto another track of its kind too), Ctrl + drag to copy it (a copy is separate: changing it never changes the clip it came from), and drag either edge to make it longer or shorter. A clip is a window onto what it holds: dragged out past the end, the music comes round again, and each restart is drawn as a notch. **S** splits at the pointer (or the selected clips at the cursor), Delete removes, **0** mutes, Ctrl+C, X, V and D copy, cut, paste and duplicate; each of these is also a button on the selected clip and in its menu (right-click, or a long press). A track's panel has its name (double-click to rename), its level, **M** and **S**, the bass-clef button that opens its composer, **FX** for its sound, and **⋯** (remove, duplicate, copy and paste its sound, colour); drag the grip to move the track up or down. How a track sounds (its instrument, its strings, keys or synth, its effects; for drums the kit, its mixer and its effects) belongs to the track, on every clip. How chords are played (style, feel, where on the neck) belongs to the clip. **↶ ↷** (Ctrl+Z, Ctrl+Shift+Z) undo and redo everything in one list for the whole idea: clips, tracks, the mixer, sounds, the key, and everything written in a composer. A muted track is not painted on the Canvas either.

**Composing: a plugin for every track.** The bass-clef button on a track opens its **composer**, a plugin window over the lower lanes. It opens on the track's **draft**: what is being composed and is not on the timeline yet. Write in it (on a Drums track: pick a rhythm and it plays at once), and along the bottom of the window a **strip** shows the clip being composed, drawn as it will look on its lane. **Drag that clip up onto the timeline** and let go: a separate copy lands on the bar under the pointer (hold Shift for the beat; a drum clip always lands on a bar line), trimming what it covers. Drag it again and a second, separate copy lands; changing one never changes another, or the draft. Only a lane of its own kind takes it: a drum composition lands on a Drums lane, chords on a Chords lane, a melody or a bass line on a Melody or a Bass lane. While it is dragged those lanes are outlined, every other lane is dimmed and refuses it, the window turns see-through so the lanes under it can be reached, time scrolls at the edges of the lanes, and it works while the song plays. Let go anywhere else, or press Escape, and it glides back with nothing changed.
- **What the window is on.** After a drop the window is on the clip just placed (its title says "bars 5–8"), so the next change is heard in the song. **Double-click a clip** to open that clip in its composer; whatever is open is changed in place. The bass-clef button goes back to the draft, as it was left. A window that is closed is only hidden: an edit in progress, and the grid's place, are there when it opens again.
- **Hearing a draft with the song.** While a draft's window is being worked in, Space and ▶ play the song with the draft in it, lying at the bar the cursor is in (with the loop on: where the loop starts), and it is drawn there on its lane as a dashed outline. So a bass line can be written against the chords before it is placed. A press on a lane or a track's panel takes the window's focus away, and the song is heard as it is.
- **The strip**, left to right: **New** (a fresh, empty draft; one undo step), **▶** (the open bars go round, with the rest of the song, or with **alone** on, that track by itself), the clip to drag, **− N bars +** (how long it is; a placed clip that showed all of it once grows and shrinks with it, one that was dragged out to repeat keeps its own length), copy and paste of what is open (in another key, chords and melodies are moved to the key), and **Fit to these bars** when it is written in another time signature than the bars it lies in.
- **The title bar**: which track and which bars are open, the track's **M** or **S** when it is not heard (a tap lets it be heard), the open clip's **On / Off** switch, *Bars on screen*, and the **Sound** button. The window is moved by its title bar and made larger or smaller by its bottom-right corner, and opens where and how it was left; a press in it brings it to the front; Escape closes what is open inside it first, then the window; Space always plays or stops, whatever has the focus. Two sound windows opened one after the other stand a step apart, so both title bars can be reached. On a phone a window is a sheet (see *On a phone*).
- **The Drums composer** is built as a plugin: the kit beside the rhythm, every rhythm of the library by style and your own under "Mine", **✎ Edit** with Undo, Cancel and Save, the brush (Soft, Normal, Loud), ✨, copy the bar before, Clear, a tab for every bar, the nine-drum grid (it grows and shrinks with the window), and **Swing**. A rhythm picked is written out over the bars that are open, so each bar can be changed on its own.
- **The Chords composer** is built as a plugin too. On the left the **board**: the chords of the key as cards, **Outside** and **Next**, the ten kinds of chord, the key and the capo; its lists scroll inside it, and the chord last tapped keeps its name, **Shapes** and **+** in sight at the board's lower edge. On the right the **tray**, always in view: the chords of what is open as slots with their shapes (tap to hear, drag to reorder, ✕, **‹ ›** and the grid button for the shape finder), the ruler with their lengths, **+ Rest** and **Clear**, **Arrange** for a guitar (the frets the shapes span stand under its name; a chosen neck area adds **Strings** and **Inversions** on a second row) or **Voicing** for the keys, and **≈ Harmonize**. The **shape finder** opens as a panel in the board's place, beside the tray, so a shape is dragged straight into the tray; so do the playing styles. Over the tray, **Playing style ‹ ›**: the name opens every style by kind, with **Feel** (speed, octave, swing, touch, spread) on a second tab. Style and feel belong to the clip that is open, so one clip can be strummed and the next one picked; **All clips** makes every clip of the track play this way. A style two bars long, and a style at half speed, start with their clip wherever it lies. On a synth track the playing style is not shown: the synth's own way of playing takes its place. What the chords sound like is the track's, in its sound window. The window as it first opens is wide and low; made taller, the tray's chords go on to rows so all of them are in view, and taller still the tray lies across the bottom under the board.
- **The Melody and Bass composer** is built as a plugin, and one composer serves both kinds of track: the note grid, with **every octave from C1 to C8** in a box that scrolls. The row names (each with its octave) stay at the left, the bar numbers and the song's chords at the top, and the home note's row is marked in every octave. A Bass track opens low (an empty one with E1 as its bottom row), a Melody track on its home note, and a clip with notes opens on its notes. **▲ ▼** beside the grid move the view a whole octave at one press (so do Page Up and Page Down), the wheel scrolls the rows, and the **overview** between the two buttons shows the whole range, with a mark for every note and a box round what is in view: press it, or drag along it, to go there. A dot on ▲ or ▼ says there are notes further up or down. **Move notes: Octave ↓ ↑** in the row of tools is something else: it moves every note of what is open an octave (the view goes with them), and is greyed out when a note would leave the grid. Tap a square for a note, drag along a row to paint (one drag is one undo), and **drag a note's right end** to give it a length (on the keys and the synth it is held that long; the strings ring on). With the grid in focus the arrow keys move a square about it and Enter puts a note there or takes it out. The rest of the grid is described further down (*Melody and Bass*): the grid's steps with triplets, a tab for every bar, copy the bar before, **Key only**, a tap on a row's name to hear it, the dots on the notes of the chord underneath, the moving line and Follow, ↶, Clear, and ≈ Harmonize (with ⟳ in the title bar of a clip Harmonize wrote, to write it again). On / Off is the clip's switch in the title bar; the sound is in the track's sound window.
- A Vocals or a Blank audio track has no composer: its bass-clef button and a double-click on one of its clips open the **record window**: Record and Rehearse, the passes and ★ (or K) to keep one, the count-in and the click, the take list, how you hear yourself (Direct or through the Effects) and how loud the song and the click are in the headphones. Its **FX** opens the track's own sound window, like any other track's: the vocal sound (a preset to start from, Colour, EQ, Dynamics, Width, Echo, Reverb), **Apply sound** when takes were made with an older sound, and Bypass. Closing a window never stops a recording, and hearing yourself through the effects goes on with every window closed.
- **Recording.** Any number of tracks record, each with its own name, input and sound; **●** on a track's panel arms it (one at a time: arming another moves the arm), and **●** in the top bar (Ctrl+R in the desktop app) records onto the armed track. *Which input:* the small chip beside a track's ● opens the list of **every input the interface has**, each on its own (In 1 … In N), and every two neighbours as a **stereo pair** (In 1+2, In 3+4 …), with a live level beside each line while the interface is open (it is opened for the list after a moment, when nothing is playing), so the right one is easy to find. The choice is kept on the track. A choice the connected interface does not have is listed as *not there* and stays as it is until you pick another. *Two ways to record:* with the **loop on**, the loop goes round and every pass is a take (★ the keepers, as before); with the **loop off**, the song plays **once from the bar the cursor is in** and the recording goes on, past the song's end too, until you stop it: one take, lying where it was played. Either way a clip grows on the lane while you record, and a second take over the same song starts at once (the song under it is only made again when something else in it changed). *A pair* is recorded as one stereo take: drawn as two waveforms (left over right), played back in stereo, moved, trimmed, split and repeated by its edge like any clip, and in the exported WAV. The vocal sound takes one voice, so a track on a pair is heard as it was recorded and its sound window has only its Output level; a Blank track starts with the vocal sound off, a Vocals track with it on. One track records at a time. *In a browser* (no desktop app) ● records the **microphone the browser offers** (it asks the first time) while the song plays: the take lands close to in time, not to the sample, and the record window has the **timing nudge** to put it right; there is no count-in, no click, no monitoring and no vocal sound there, and a take recorded in a browser is kept in that browser.

**Sound: a plugin for every track.** **FX** on a track's panel opens that track's own **sound window**, down the right side of the lanes; the **Sound** button of its composer opens the same window standing beside the composer. A Chords, Melody or Bass track: the instrument (Guitar, Piano or Synth), the ready-made sounds and your own, the strings or the keys or the synth, and the effects. A Drums track: the kit (the ready-made ones, the machines and your own), the **mixer** (a fader, mute and solo for each drum, and the whole kit's level), the chosen drum's own sound (tap a drum's name in the composer and that drum is shown), and the effects of the whole kit. All of it belongs to the track, on every clip. In its title bar: **Bypass** (the track is heard without its effects while the switch is on; nothing in the idea is changed; **Alt + click on FX** does the same), and copy and paste of the sound (onto a track of the same kind).

**Time signatures.** The signature in the top bar is that of the bars the cursor is in (2/4, 3/4, 4/4, 5/4, 6/4, 7/4, 6/8, 9/8, 12/8). Picking another changes those bars, up to the next change; with **From this bar on**, everything after the cursor's bar. Each change stands on the ruler as a small flag (click it to change it or take it away). An older idea whose sections had signatures of their own opens with a flag where each of them started. The signature says how long the bars are everywhere: the ruler, the chord ruler, the melody grid, the drum grid, and the MIDI file, which writes each signature where it starts. A clip keeps the signature it was written in, and a drum rhythm picked into a clip is fitted to that clip's bars (6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 turn 16ths into triplets, and back).

**Harmonize.** **≈ Harmonize** sits in the Arrange row on top of the chord tray (Voicing on the keys) and in the top row of the Melody and Bass composer. It works on the bars of the clip that is open, and it writes into the track that is open when its clip is empty: add a second Chords track, open it, tap Harmonize, and its clip is filled; no third track is made. The sheet says where it goes and which bars, and offers the tracks that have music under those bars. Only when the open clip holds music of its own does the harmony go to another empty track of the kind, or to a new track directly below (the sheet then says *a new track*), as a clip over the same bars. A harmony track's panel has **⟳**, which writes the selected clip's harmony again after what it was written from has changed (a dot on the clip says so); it asks nothing first, also over a harmony changed by hand since: Ctrl+Z takes it back. Harmonizing again over a harmony you have not changed writes over it, in the same track; one you changed by hand is never written over. The first tracks count too: an empty Melody harmonized with Chords is filled. Then it offers the harmonies for that track, best first as music theory ranks them, only those that give something here. A melody line with a tune: *Low harmony* and *High harmony* (a second voice a 3rd below or above, bending to a chord note where a 3rd would rub, so the two are never one line an octave apart; under a low tune the high one comes first), *Stacked* (one voice above and one below, in the tune's rhythm), a *Counter-melody* that moves where the tune holds, *Long notes* (one held note per chord) and an *Answer* where the tune holds or breathes (its last note held on, moving only where a chord or a tune note it would rub comes in); with chords: *Long notes*, a *Counter-melody* and *Chord notes*. Chords with chords: *High chords* (your chords again, higher up, in the gaps of the first), *Airy* (open 5ths and 9ths high above), *Guide tones* (the 3rd and 7th of each chord), *Top note* (one high note over the changes); then, played **Instead** of the chords: *Relative*, *Darker* (*Brighter* in a minor key) and *Lead-ins*. Chords for a tune: *Best fit* and *Another fit*. Every harmony is written against what really plays here (the chords as their instrument voices them, the bass, the tune), so it sits in the gaps instead of on top of them. Tap an option to hear those bars round and round with it; once one is picked, three plain controls shape it while it plays: **Simpler ↔ Busier**, **Closer ↔ Wider**, **Plain ↔ Colourful** (one that means nothing for that harmony is greyed out). **✓** keeps it. Moving the song to another key, or pasting chords into one, moves a harmony's notes with its chords. Nothing about sound is chosen for you: the track keeps its instrument, sound and level. The same bars always get the same harmony, so what you hear is what you keep. **↶** in the top bar takes a harmony back; removing one that played instead of chords lets them play again.

**Bars on screen.** Every composer window has the same control in its title bar: **Bars 1 · 2 · 4 · 8 · All**, and **Follow**.
- The melody and drum grids put that many bars side by side, using the whole width of the screen. Each bar has its number over it, its own steps, a line between it and the next, and its own moving line while it plays. A drag paints straight across from one bar into the next. The chord ruler fits that many bars into its width (**All**: everything the clip holds); the rest is a sideways scroll away.
- Until you choose, a wide screen shows 4 bars, a tablet 2 and a phone 1. Your choice is kept, and it is the same in every composer.
- **The music never moves what you are looking at unless Follow is on.** With it off, the bars on screen stay where you put them while the song plays: a line shows where the music is whenever its bar is on screen, a dot marks the tab of the bar that is sounding, and the play line on the lanes always marks it too. With Follow on, a composer turns a whole page when the music runs off the end of it (with 4 bars on screen: bars 1–4, then 5–8; with 1 bar, bar by bar). It waits a moment after you touch the grid, and the drums stay put while you are editing them.
- The bar tabs choose the bar that ⧉, ✨ and Clear work on (a tap on a square, or on a bar's number, chooses it too). A tab whose bar is off screen brings its page with it; **‹ ›** turn a page.
- More bars than fit (on a phone, or **All** on a long clip) scroll sideways inside the grid, with the note and drum names kept in view; the page itself never scrolls sideways. On a phone, melody squares stay at least 30 px wide.

**Drum mixer.** In a Drums track's sound window, **Mixer** (before Sound and Effects) has a fader, mute and solo for each drum and one fader for the whole kit.

**Key, tempo and export.** The key button in the top bar opens the twelve notes, the modes, and **Move song**: the key, every chord and every melody up or down a semitone together (one undo puts the notes and the key back together). The tempo is dragged on its number, or set with the slider under it. **Export** has **WAV** (the whole song as you hear it, every track and the recordings, with the mixer's levels, mute and solo; 24-bit, 48 kHz, never clipping, the last reverb left to ring out), **MIDI** (every track as notes on a MIDI track of its own, under its name and with its instrument; recordings are sound, so they are in the WAV) and **Chords + tab** (as text: the marker names in running order, then each stretch between markers once, so three verses with the same chords print one; chords on a guitar as tab, chords on the keys as the keys by name).

**Copy and paste.** Clips are copied and pasted on the timeline: Ctrl+C, then Ctrl+V at the cursor (Ctrl+Shift+V first pushes everything after the cursor later to make room). A copied clip carries its track's sound, and what was copied is kept when another idea is opened and when the app is closed: pasted into an idea that has no track for it, the track is made, with that sound; in another key, chords and melodies are moved to the key. **Copy sound** and **Paste sound** in a track's **⋯** menu, and in the title bar of its sound window, move a sound alone, onto a track of the same kind.

**A clip holds exactly its bars.** Each composer shows every bar of what its clip holds. **What is in a bar plays; an empty bar is silent.** (A clip drawn longer than what it holds plays it again from the start: the notches on the lane show where.)

- **Chords:** the chord ruler is drawn as long as the clip's music. Chords fill it from bar 1; bars with no chord are drawn empty and play nothing. **+ Rest** adds a bar of silence between chords (it can be dragged and resized like a chord). Chords that run past the last bar are struck out, with one tap to make room for them.
- **Melody:** the grid has a tab for every bar. Bars with no notes are drawn hollow.
- **Drums:** the grid has a tab for every bar of the rhythm. In Edit, **Clear** empties the chosen bar so it plays nothing, and **⧉** copies the bar before into it. A rhythm picked from the list is written out over the bars that are open; it never changes how long the clip is on the timeline.

**Every track sounds its own way.** A chords, melody or bass track has its instrument (Guitar, Piano or Synth, and how that is set), its strings and its effects; a drums track its kit (the machine, each drum's sound, the mixer, the effects). They belong to the track for the whole song, and changing one never changes another track. Each chords clip keeps how it is played: its style and feel, and on the guitar where on the neck its shapes are arranged. An older idea in which one track changed its sound from section to section opens with one track for each of those sounds, named after the section ("Chords · Chorus"), so the change still comes exactly where it did.

In the **Chords** window (the key is in the top bar now):

1. **Key**: tap one of the 12 notes.
2. **Mode**: Major, Minor, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Locrian, Harmonic minor, Melodic minor (27 more under "More…").
3. **Chords appear**: one card per note of the scale, each drawn as an easy shape with finger numbers. **Tap a card to hear it**, and everything else the key offers on that note is listed under it by kind: sus, sixths, sevenths, added notes, ninths and beyond (56 chord types are checked against the scale, so nothing that fits is left out and nothing foreign gets in). A row of ten kinds chooses what the cards show: **Triads, Power, Sus2, Sus4, 6ths, 7ths, Add9, 9ths, 11ths, 13ths**. Each kind is built on every note of the scale and shown only where it uses notes of the key alone (so there is no sus2 on the third note of a major key); a note with no chord of that kind is left hollow. Two choices follow standard practice: ninths leave out a flattened ninth over a minor chord, and elevenths use the raised eleventh on major chords and the suspended form on dominants, because a plain eleventh clashes with a major third. Card colours show each chord's job: 🟢 Home · 🔵 Near home · 🟠 Away · 🔴 Tension; ★ = holds the mode's signature note. **Outside** has borrowed and surprise chords; **Next** suggests what could follow your last chord. On a phone the cards are one row to swipe through.
4. **Shapes** (the button on every chord) opens the shape finder: the ways guitarists really play that chord, starting from the ones everybody knows.
   - **Common**: the textbook open chord, the two movable barre shapes (root on the 6th string, root on the 5th string), the small four-string shape, and for seventh chords the standard jazz grips.
   - **Small**: three- and four-string shapes with no big barre, grouped by string set and running up the neck. The way out of playing barre chords for everything.
   - **Inversions**: the same chord with another note in the bass (C/E, C/G), grouped by bass note.
   - **All**: everything playable, grouped by where it sits on the neck.
   - Tap a shape to hear it and use it. **Flavours** along the top swap the chord itself (C → Cmaj7, Cadd9, Csus2, C6…; "All…" has all 56 types).
   - Only comfortable grips are listed: no muted strings in the middle of a chord (except the classic thumb-muted ones like 3x0003 and 5x555x), no stretch wider than four frets, no open strings mixed with notes far up the neck.
5. **Build the progression in the tray.** It is always in view in the Chords window, beside the chords (the strip under it shows the same chords in one line, with ▶). Drag chords in (touch: press and hold, then drag; or tap **+**), drag to reorder, ✕ to remove. The finder opens in the board's place, beside the tray, so you can **drag one exact shape straight into the tray**. Tap a chord in the tray to hear it; the small grid button under it opens the finder for that spot (shape, flavour, length, position).
   - **How long each chord lasts**: the strip under the tray is the clip's music drawn to scale, every chord as wide as it is long, with a line at each bar. **Drag the edge between two chords** to move where the change falls inside the bar (the two share the time). **Tap a chord** in the strip to set its length exactly: − / + in half beats, or 1 beat, ½ bar, 1 bar, 2 bars. A bar that is not full yet is shown hatched.
6. **Arrange** (the bar on top of the tray): **≈ Harmonize** writes chords to go with a track you choose, into the track on screen when it is free (see *Harmonize*). **The synth** (choose **Synth** on any chords or melody track). A virtual-analogue synthesizer modelled on a small, much-loved analogue-style keyboard whose whole sound is one flat panel of sliders, with what that keyboard leaves out for someone starting out: ready-made sounds, big macro knobs and ways of playing.
- **The panel** is the original's, section by section. **Osc**: five ways of making sound, each with two sliders, **Texture** and **Mod**, that mean something different for each: *Multi Saw* (Texture adds a sub an octave down, Mod spreads up to seven detuned saws into a supersaw), *Pulse* (Texture detunes and then retunes a second square to a fifth, an octave and so on; Mod narrows the pulse), *Sync* (a second oscillator locked to the first: Texture sets its pitch, Mod sweeps it with the envelope), *Ring* (two oscillators multiplied: bells and metal) and *FM* (one sine bending another: Texture is how much, Mod the ratio, resting on whole ratios with the clangy ones between). Plus noise. **LFO**: to the loudness, the filter, the pitch or the oscillator (on Pulse that is pulse-width modulation), with depth, speed, a shape and a fade-in for delayed vibrato. **Filter**: a resonant four-pole low-pass that sings on its own at the top, with drive and **key tracking** (high notes stay bright). **EG**: one envelope, with a balance slider choosing whether it shapes the loudness, the filter or both. **Effect**: distortion, chorus/flanger, phaser or delay, plus a **reverb** of its own. **Voice**: poly, mono or legato, glide, **velocity** (harder is louder and brighter), drift (the slow wander of old circuits), width and volume.
- **61 ready-made sounds** in Bass, Lead, Pad, Keys, Pluck, Brass, Strings, Bells, Drone, FX and Arp (and a plain start for each oscillator), all brought to the same loudness. **‹ ›** steps through a kind, **⚄** picks another of the same kind, a dot after the name says the sound has been changed.
- **Six macros**, big knobs that reshape any sound from where it was made: **Bright**, **Motion**, **Space**, **Grit**, **Width** and **Shape** (tight and short ↔ soft and long). Each moves several sliders at once, and the sliders move with it, so you can see what it did. Double-click a knob (or a slider) to put it back.
- **Ways of playing** the chords and notes already written: **Hold** (as written), **Arp** (a style: **Up**, **Down**, **Up & down**, **Skip**, **Bounce**, **Shuffle**, **Triplets** or **Random**, with **Speed** ¼ · ⅛ · 1/16 and **Range** 1–3 octaves; every step is heard as its own note on every sound, the track's reverb kept small behind it; a sound from the **Arp** kind turns it on; in 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 the steps are the meter's own eighths and sixteenths), **Strum** (down, up or alternating, with a spread), **Pulse** (the chord struck again on the beat), **Drone** (the key's home note, its fifth and octave held under everything, with a softer voice moving to each chord's root, even when the keys play the chord as an inversion) and **Glide** (one line, sliding from note to note). **Loose** humanises any of them.
- **■ silences it** within about a third of a second, however long the sound's own release (a drone lets go over several seconds while the music plays; on ■ it fades out quickly, echoes and reverb with it), and ▶ straight after is heard in full.
- It sounds the same live and in an exported WAV: the same engine plays the same notes, sample for sample. It sounds the same at 44.1, 48 and 96 kHz too, and switching the oscillator keeps the loudness within about a decibel.
- No clicks when notes start, stop or are struck again harder, or when a whole new chord takes over every voice at once. Choosing **Drone** on a sound that dies away (a pluck, a bell) keeps it sounding under the passage.

## The Canvas: music paints itself

Open **Canvas** and press ▶, or open **any song from a file** (the folder button, or drop a file on the canvas: WAV, MP3, FLAC, OGG, and whatever else the browser can read) and press its ▶. While the music plays it is painted, live, in wet paint on a woven surface. The painting is a picture of the sound you are making: every part of the music has its own kind of paint, the canvas fills only as far as the music fills it, and music of one kind paints pictures of one kind. No two paintings come out the same: press **Clear** and play the same song again for new detail on the same picture. The painting stays while you look at other pages; another idea opened (or a new one started) begins on a bare canvas.

**The layers.** The music is heard as the parts that play together, and each part is painted in a paint you can tell from the others by its shape alone, even in grey. The row of little swatches under the canvas shows each one in the colours in use; it lights up while that part is playing, and a tap hides or shows that part.

- **Drums: impacts.** The kick is a soft round blot low down, a snare a burst of hard little drops, the hats a dry stipple of specks near the top, a cymbal a trail of specks as long as it rings.
- **Bass: broad, flat, heavy bands** along the bottom. A held note is one band; short notes are stacked slabs.
- **Chords: washes.** A held chord is a thin, wide wash from its lowest note to its highest, in the colours of its notes; a new chord is a new wash; a stabbed chord is a hard-edged block. The more a chord clashes, the more layers it has and the greyer it goes.
- **Melody: one continuous line,** as if drawn by one loaded brush. Notes that join are one curve; short notes are a dotted line along the same path; a leap starts a new stroke.
- **Harmony: a companion line** beside the melody, thinner and lighter, hatched to it where the two run close (more densely the rougher they sound together), a few links every so far along the line, so a harmony that shadows its tune note for note is not much busier than the tune. A track marked as a harmony is painted this way.
- **Vocals: a luminous ribbon** of fine strands. Breath splays the strands, loudness makes them glow, a vibrato ripples them. On a dark ground it is laid on as light.
- **Other: fog,** edge-less haze that drifts (pads, ambience, the room).

They lie in a fixed order, as a painter would lay them: fog and washes underneath, the bass and the lines in the middle, the voice's glaze and the drums on top. A wash that dries after a spatter of drops goes in under the drops.

**The marks answer the sound.** How loud is how big and how heavily loaded. How hard a sound starts is how hard its edge is and whether it throws paint; a sound that swells in has a feathered edge. How long it rings is how long its tail is. How noisy it is is how broken and splayed the mark is, and how far its drops fly. How bright it is is how light its colour. A bend is a slope and a vibrato a ripple. The app knows every note and hit it plays before it is heard, so the marks of your own song land with their sound.

**Layouts** (**Layout**: **Auto**, or one of six). Every layout keeps a sound's height (or, in the round ones, its distance from the middle) for its frequency, so a picture can always be read. What changes is how time is spent:

- **〰 River:** the canvas itself flows slowly to the left and the newest paint is laid in at the right-hand edge and carried off to the left, so the whole width is painted; nothing sweeps across it, and the lines of the music meander a little as a river does.
- **≋ Field:** each part is carried by a current of its own across the whole canvas, back and forth, bent by a slow swirl, so the parts cross and weave over each other.
- **◎ Rings:** time goes round, frequency goes out from the middle; one turn every few bars, so the downbeats line up on spokes and a repeated groove draws the same rosette.
- **✿ Bloom:** each section opens like a flower from its own place on the canvas, turning a little as it grows, so a held note curls out like a petal.
- **☰ Lanes:** each part in a band of its own (the bass low, the chords in the middle, the melody above them, the drums at the top), flowing left like the river.
- **→ Sweep:** left to right across a page of bars, page after page, with the bar numbers and the guide: the way to read a song bar by bar.

On **Auto** the music chooses, from its vibe (below): a steady groove tends to the river or the rings, calm held music to blooms, energetic textured music to the field, a dense arrangement of many parts to lanes. It changes its mind only when another layout suits clearly better for a bar or more, and keeps its choice once it knows the song. When the layout changes, what was painted before sinks back for a few seconds under the new one, so the first picture stays as a ghost rather than a muddle.

**As full as the music.** The canvas fills in proportion to how much is playing, how loud: two quiet parts leave most of it bare ground (about a fifth covered); six loud ones cover about two thirds; some ground is always left. No part can cover the canvas on its own: a busy hi-hat gets its share and no more. When the music gets quieter nothing new is added, and paint that is more than the music now calls for sinks slowly back into the ground, so a long song never turns to mud.

**The vibe.** About seventy numbers are measured from the music as a whole (the shape of its sound, its harmony and key, its rhythm and groove, its dynamics, how its notes are held or clipped, and which parts play and how much), the way music-similarity research measures songs, and read against fixed reference figures. They set how wet the paint runs, how loose the hand is, how fast the currents move, how many marks a sound leaves, how long the strokes are, which way round the palette the parts are cast and which layout Auto chooses. The same song always gets the same vibe; a song with similar parts gets a similar one, whatever its key, tempo or mix, and so a similar painting, while every mark, which follows its own notes, is new. Write a song inspired by a favourite, run both through the canvas, and you can see how close the atmosphere is.

**Songs from files are split into layers** on your own computer, as soon as they are opened, well ahead of where they play: what is sudden is the drums, what is steady and low the bass, what is steady, in the middle of the stereo picture and does not repeat the voice, and the rest is shared out into chords, melody and fog by how its notes behave. This is plain signal processing, not a trained model, so the layers are approximate (a held synth line can pass for a voice, and a voice singing alone is painted as the other parts are). A split leaves scraps in every layer, and they are cleared away before anything is painted: a drum's ring (a kick's thump goes to the bass, a snare's body to the rest), and tones that flicker for a few hundredths of a second where the split is unsure; an overtone the ear hears as a tone of its own is part of its note, not a mark of its own; and fog comes only from noise that goes on (air, breath, a room), not from a hi-hat. So a song of drums alone is painted as drums, and a song from a file paints much closer to the way the same music written in the app does. Until the split has caught up, the whole mix is heard and its notes shared out. The vibe of the whole song is known by the time it has been heard through once (a first idea of it within a few seconds of opening it). A trained model's stems (from the desktop app) can take over from the split at any point, stretch by stretch as they are made, at their own sample rate, with a six-stem model's guitar and piano painted by hands of their own; wherever they have been heard, the painting is made from them.

**Better layers (Windows app).** In the desktop app the Canvas can hear a song from a file through a neural stem split instead (see *Stems* below), which tells the voice, the drums, the bass and the rest apart far more cleanly. It runs on your own computer; nothing is sent anywhere. The first time, a small **⤓** button beside the song's name shows how much it needs to download, once (about 190 MB: the model and the runtime that runs it); press it and a little bar fills as it downloads (press again to stop). After that every song you open is split by itself, from the start, while it plays: a thin line along the bottom of the canvas shows how far the better layers reach, and the painting switches to them wherever they are ready and keeps the quick split everywhere else. A song split before is ready at once. With the six-stem model, **Guitar** and **Piano** get swatches of their own in the row under the canvas. Closing the song, opening another or leaving the page stops the split; recording a take pauses it. Where it cannot run (the web version, a PC missing what it needs) the Canvas simply keeps its quick split; in the desktop app a dim **◇** says why when you point at it.

**How to read it**

- **Up is frequency.** The scale runs from 32 Hz at the bottom to 16 kHz at the top and is marked down the left edge (50, 100, 200, 500, 1k, 2k, 5k, 10k) in the layouts where height is frequency; the four octaves the notes of a song live in get the biggest steps. In the sweep, the canvas is as wide as the section's bars (numbered along the bottom). For a song from a file, **Across** sets how much of it one crossing of the canvas holds (8, 15 or 30 seconds, or all of it).
- **Every tone holds a brush to the canvas for exactly as long as it sounds.** A staccato note is over before the brush has moved, so it leaves a **dab**; a held note leaves a **stroke** as long as itself; a plucked note that dies away leaves a head and a thinning tail.
- **Colour follows harmony.** The notes of the key share the palette out between them in the order of the circle of fifths, so a chord is a family of neighbouring colours; each part leans to a colour of its own within the palette. For the app's own song the key is the song's; for a song from a file it is heard.
- **Down the right edge in the sweep (the app's own song): what the mix covers,** band by band, coloured by how much of it is chords, melody and drums (every track of each kind). The **Hz** button puts the guide away and brings it back.

**How it is painted**

- **It listens to the sound itself:** the whole mix as it leaves the mixer (for the mood, the key and the vibe), and every track through its own fader, each with an ear and a hand of its own: every drums track on its own desk, every chords and melody track on whatever it is played on (the strings, the piano or the synth: a held pad is painted as a held wash, an arpeggio as quick notes, a drone as a long held line low down), and a track marked as a harmony in the harmony's paint (a swap written instead of the chords is the chords now heard, and is painted as them). A muted track (or one left out by a solo, or with its fader all the way down) gets no ear and is not painted, from the moment it is muted, held notes included. The ears take turns on the audio thread so they never hold up the sound; there are never more than six, and beyond that tracks of one kind share one, the drums first and two melodies last (so two lines never become one). For tracks whose notes the app plays, the ear only listens for the texture (how loud, how bright, how noisy), every other hundredth of a second, and the notes themselves come exactly, a little ahead: with seven such tracks the ears take about a tenth of the audio thread's time. A song from a file is heard layer by layer in the background, ahead of playing, so each mark lands with its sound. How much of the canvas is covered is read back from the graphics card without making it wait.
- **The paint is wet.** The painting is simulated on the graphics card, point by point, sixty times a second: paint flows from where it stands deep to where it is shallow, is carried the way the brush pushed it, bleeds into wet paint beside it, and dries from its edges in; as it dries its pigment settles, more in the hollows of the surface than on its ridges. The edge of a wash holds, as water does on dry paper, unless it is so full that it spills: a bloom, or (with **Drips** on) a run down the canvas.
- **Colours mix as pigments do, not as lights.** Blue into yellow goes green, not grey. Only the voice's glaze is laid on as light, which brightens what is under it.
- **A brush is a brush.** Its hairs leave ridges, deeper the drier the paint; it carries only so much and runs dry as it goes. Its tip follows where it is sent the way a real one does, a little late and with a little overshoot, and the hand is never quite steady. A line (melody, harmony, bass, voice) glides from one note to the next, as gently as the time between them allows, rather than stepping up and down like a stair.
- **The mood of the music sets the manner** (**Paint: Auto**): held, quiet music runs wet and blends; loud, clipped, noisy music goes on thick, dry and loose, with more spatter. **Wash** and **Thick** hold it to one or the other.
- **Old paint** can stay, or sink back into the canvas smoothly as the music goes on (Slow, Medium, Fast), so the newest is the clearest.
- **36 colour schemes** in five families (below), three **surfaces** (canvas, linen, rough paper), **Save picture** (a PNG with the surface in it) and **⤢** to fill the screen.

**What it does not show.** Where a sound is from left to right in the stereo field. Notes less than about a semitone apart low down (below 200 Hz the ear's long look cannot separate them). Which instrument a tone came from, beyond its part. In a song from a file, the parts are only as good as the split (see above). The recorded vocal tracks of the Windows app while they are being recorded (they play through the recording engine, which the canvas cannot hear); once recorded they are painted, and an exported WAV opened here paints everything.

### Stems: a song split into its layers (Windows app)

The Windows app can split a song you open from a file into its **stems**, on your own computer:
**vocals, drums, bass** and **other** (everything else), or, with the six-stem model, **guitar** and
**piano** as well (the piano stem is rough). Each layer can then be painted from its own sound
instead of from the whole mix.

- **What does it.** Demucs v4, a neural network made by Meta's research lab and released as open
  source (MIT licence), run by Microsoft's ONNX Runtime. It is among the best free separators there
  are, but stems are never studio-clean: expect a little bleed (a touch of cymbal in the vocals, a
  little voice in "other"). For seeing what each layer does, that is plenty.
- **Downloaded once, when you ask.** The model is not in the installer. The first time, the app
  fetches ONNX Runtime (about 25 MB, from Microsoft's own package) and the model (about 166 MB for
  four stems, 136 MB for six; builds of the same network range from about 80 to 320 MB), checks each
  against its fingerprint (SHA-256) and keeps them. It only ever downloads from its publishers'
  own addresses, written into the app, over secure connections. A download that is interrupted
  carries on where it stopped. If it fails, or you never ask, nothing else changes.
- **One thing Windows may need.** ONNX Runtime needs Microsoft's *Visual C++ Redistributable*
  (x64), from 2024 or later. Most PCs have it; if yours does not, the app says so before it
  downloads anything. Install the latest one from Microsoft and try again.
- **How long it takes.** On an ordinary 4-core laptop processor, roughly as long as the song itself
  (somewhere between half and the whole of its length); with a graphics card Windows can use for it
  (DirectML, which most recent PCs have), several times faster. The song is worked through from the
  start, 7.8 seconds at a time, and every stem can be used up to where the work has got, so the
  painting can start long before the whole song is done.
- **It keeps out of the music's way.** It uses at most half of the processor, at low priority, and
  stops completely while you record a take (it does not even start until the take is over). If
  the graphics card will not do it, or gives back nonsense, the processor takes over; if neither
  can, the app tells you why and the Canvas carries on with its quick splits.
- **Kept for next time.** Stems are kept in the app's cache folder (32-bit WAV, about 90 MB per stem
  for a four-minute song), so the same song is ready at once the next time, whatever its file is
  called. When they take more than about 4 GB, the songs split longest ago make room.
- **Files it reads:** WAV, MP3, FLAC, OGG (Vorbis) and M4A (AAC or Apple Lossless), up to about
  three hours long at 48 kHz. Not Opus.
- **Only in the desktop app.** The web version and the phone app cannot run it; there (and until
  the model is downloaded) the Canvas uses its quick splits, approximate layers worked out from the
  sound as it plays.
- The stems come from your own file and stay on your computer; use them to study and paint. Your
  rights in the recording are what they were before. Licences and credits: `NOTICE.md`.

- **The shape book** keeps about one in six of the generated voicings. A shape has to be holdable (stretch, finger order, muted strings) and it is then filed under the families guitar teaching uses: open chords, the two barre shapes, triads on neighbouring strings, four-string shapes and shell voicings. Default shapes, Arrange and the ‹ › steppers only ever use these.
- **Voicings** are found by scanning 4-fret windows up the neck (plus open strings), trying every combination of chord tones per string, then keeping only shapes with all required tones, at most one muted string in the middle, and a feasible fingering. Each is scored for difficulty (fingers, barres, stretch, mutes) and usefulness (root position, fullness, low-end mud).
- **Suggestions** score every candidate chord by root motion (falling 5ths, steps, 3rds, chromatic slides), shared notes, voice-leading distance and resolution of tension, then group them by character.
- **Progression voicings** use dynamic programming to minimise total difficulty plus hand movement and top-note jumps across the whole progression.

- **The harp** is not a recording. Each note is a sum of fading sine waves, one per overtone, shaped by what acoustics research says makes a harp sound like a harp: harpists pluck a third to two fifths of the way along the string, which removes whole families of overtones (pluck the exact middle and only the odd ones are left); the soft finger pad rounds the attack; gut and nylon lose their high overtones quickly (a quality factor near 1000 that falls above 2–3 kHz), so the tail is pure; nothing damps the string; each overtone swings two ways at slightly different pitches, which gives the slow shimmer of a real string; a concert harp's soundboard radiates most around 148, 166, 228 and 335 Hz and little below, and answers each pluck with a soft knock; and the other strings hum along. A harder pluck is brighter as well as louder. Notes are in tune to within a few cents and all equally loud.
- **Tracks and clips (stored version 5).** A song is a list of tracks, each with its sound, and a list of clips, each on one track at a beat, holding its own chords, melody or rhythm (`src/timeline/model.ts`). One pure function, `flatten`, turns them into the list the unchanged player, the WAV render and the MIDI writer take; the song is cut for the player wherever no clip crosses a bar line. Ideas saved by older versions are converted once (`src/timeline/migrate5.ts`), held to asking the engine for exactly what they asked before, and the old data is kept as the originals behind **Before track view**. The Chords composer and Harmonize work on one clip through a lens that shows it to them as a small song of its own (`src/timeline/lens.ts`). The old pages themselves are gone; the code that reads an old idea (`src/song/`, `loadV4` in `src/ui/state.tsx`) stays for good, so an idea last saved by a build from months ago, or an old backup file, still converts exactly as it did. What follows in this bullet is how older versions stored a song, which is what that conversion reads. A section stores everything about itself: its chords and its melody, how its first tracks sound there (style and feel, the chord harp, the melody harp, the drum kit and rhythm), its instruments by track id, and its added tracks, each as its part of the section (`layers`, keyed by track id): what it holds there and how it sounds there. A track is in a section exactly when the section has a part for it, so there is nothing to keep in step, and a track (its name, its place and its mixer strip) that is in no section any more leaves the song. The stored state holds a live copy of the section that was on screen, written back into its section when the state is read. Letters are never stored: they are worked out from the playing order each time. Arrange (where on the neck a guitar track's chords sit) is part of how a chords track sounds, so it is kept the same way: the first chords track's in the section, an added one's in its part. A harmony track's record of each section says which option it was written with there, and only where that option plays instead of the chords are they left out (`insteadIn`). Songs saved by older versions are moved over once when they are opened (`src/song/migrate.ts`): every section gets exactly what it played (sounds that followed the first section resolved, the one-sound-per-track version's sounds copied into every section, and with no version a section's kit over the page's, as it played), an added track stays in the sections where it has music, the song's one Arrange is copied to every chords track in every section, and a harmony whose chords had been unmuted by hand is marked as playing along with them, so both are still heard. An old section clipboard is moved over once and marked so.
- **How many tracks one audio thread carries.** Every track's voice, effects and drum desk run on the browser's one audio thread, and no limit on tracks is built in. Measured with songs in which every track plays all the time (strummed chords on the strings, a piano and the synth, melodies in eighth notes, synth basses, a drum desk for each drums track; a loop of 8 bars; the audio thread's processor time by the system's own count, production build, on one core of a 2.3 GHz virtual server processor with no sound card, on which a song costs about half as much again as on the desktop of the earlier figures; every figure from two runs): with the load song's effects on every track (reverb, echo, chorus; reverb and echo on every drum desk) **5 tracks 22 to 26 % of one core, 9 tracks 40 to 44 %, 16 tracks 66 to 68 %, 24 tracks 92 to 93 %**, and 32 tracks full (95 %: the thread can no longer keep up and the sound breaks up); with every track as a new track is, 5 tracks 21 %, 9 tracks 33 to 35 %, 16 tracks 56 %, 24 tracks 78 to 81 %, 32 full. And a long arrangement of 30 tracks of which 8 to 10 sound at any moment: **46 to 51 %** with those effects, 40 to 42 % as new tracks are. Before this work the same songs cost 34 to 38 % for 5 tracks, 62 to 69 % for 9, and the arrangement 80 %: about 7 % of a core for each sounding track, the thread full (95 %, the sound breaking up) from 16 tracks on. Three things changed. Where the browser lets a page choose, the live sound is worked out one sound-card buffer at a time (about a thousand frames) instead of 128 frames at a time: the browser called on every one of a track's seventy-odd small nodes eight times for each buffer, and being called on was most of what they cost. The buffer is the size it was, so nothing is later; an echo's loop, the one thing a block's length is heard in, is set right for it; an offline render (a WAV, the recording engine's backing) still works in blocks of 128 and is made exactly as before. A drum desk that makes no sound now rests as a track's voice does (with Echo on, a silent desk used to cost 4 % of a core for good), and a desk's room is only fed while its Reverb is up. A track that is silent for a stretch rests and costs nothing there, so what counts is how many tracks sound at once: on that machine about 17 with those effects on every one, about 20 as new tracks are (before: 10 and 12) stay under 70 % of a core. The larger blocks are the browser's newest way of working: on a computer where the app's own sound misbehaves with them they are turned off in Settings, Audio (*Worked out a whole buffer at a time*; heard from the next time the app is opened), which puts the audio thread back to blocks of 128 exactly as it was. A song that is still too much for the computer says so in one line while it plays (fewer tracks playing at the same time, or effects turned off on some, will help) instead of only crackling. Checked against the sound: 99 songs rendered before and after (two renders of the same build already differ by up to -67 dB on songs with strings, a piano or drums, because the browser adds simultaneous notes up in another order each time; no song moved further than that, and the ten that repeat exactly stayed bit for bit).
- **A voice for each way a track is played.** Every chords and melody track plays through a voice of its own (`trackvoice.ts`): its instrument (the strings, a piano or the synth), its effects chain, its fader. A section that plays a track another way (another instrument, another sound) has a voice of its own for it, and sections that play it the same way share one, so a section's notes are simply booked on its own voice and the sound changes exactly where the section starts; a voice whose notes are over rests until it plays again. Every drums track has a drum desk of its own. All of them dip under the kick when a drums track's Pump is up. The live player, the WAV and the recording backing all play the same notes on the same voices; for a WAV the pianos load exactly the recordings the song needs at its sample rate first.
- **Theory audit.** A test walks every scale in all twelve keys and checks three things: every chord the app offers on a note uses only notes of the scale; every one of the 56 chord types that does fit is offered; and each chord tone is spelled with the scale's own note name (in C major the ♭5 of F is B, not C♭: an error this audit found and fixed). The chords of major, natural, harmonic and melodic minor are compared with the textbook lists, and every degree of the seven modes has a named triad, seventh and ninth chord.
- **The instruments** share one engine. A plucked string is a sum of fading overtones shaped by where it is plucked, what plucks it, the string's material and the body it sits on; each of the 18 string instruments is a set of those numbers (a banjo's drum skin makes it short and twangy; a dulcimer and a mandolin have two strings to a note, a few cents apart; the bray harp's buzz is a slow thinning-out of overtones; harmonics sound an octave up and almost pure). The four bells are bars, whose overtones are not whole multiples of the note.
- **Playing styles** are written the way teachers write them: one symbol per step of the bar (D down, U up, X muted, p-i-m-a for the picking fingers, and so on). Down-strums cross the strings in 20 to 35 ms, low string loudest; up-strums catch only the top four and are softer; muted strums are cut after 45 ms; a rolled harp chord climbs more slowly and swells to the top. The same notes go to the MIDI file.
- **The harp's effects** are wired like a pedalboard: compressor, saturation, tone, phaser, tape wobble, chorus, tremolo, then echo and reverb. The chorus is two delayed copies drifting opposite ways, one in each speaker; the phaser is four all-pass stages swept between about 360 Hz and 2 kHz; the echo's repeats bounce from side to side and get duller each time; the reverb starts 18 ms late so the pluck stays clear. Shimmer is done without a pitch shifter: an octave-up copy of each string swells in and is sent only to the reverb. Every knob is loudness-compensated from measurements of the finished output.

- **Saturation** (drums and harp) is the bend analogue gear puts in a signal when it is pushed, plus the tone shaping the real machine does around that bend. Each colour was tuned by measuring its overtones on a test note:
  - *Tape* follows the magnetisation curve of tape (a Langevin function), which adds almost only the third overtone. Treble is boosted going in and cut coming out, so the highs saturate first and come out smoother, and the lows get a small lift like a tape head's.
  - *Tube* is a valve biased off-centre: one half of the wave rounds off sooner, which makes the second overtone, an octave above the note. It stays more than 10 dB above the others up to three quarters of the knob; at full the odd overtones overtake it and it gets an edge.
  - *Console* is a pure cubic bend with the bass boosted going in and cut coming out, as transformer iron saturates on low notes first: at half, a 60 Hz note has about three times the distortion of a 1 kHz one.
  - *Crunch* and *Fuzz* pass the bass round the bend and distort only what is above it, the way a bass-preserving overdrive does. *Clip* is a straight line with a knee near the top: ordinary hits pass untouched, peaks are shaved.
  - On every colour the overtones grow as they do on the real thing: about 1 to 2% at a quarter turn (colour you feel more than hear), 3 to 6% at half, well into distortion at full.
  - The bend runs four times oversampled so it does not alias, which in a browser delays it by about 4 ms. The clean path and the bass path go through the same stage unbent, so the three stay exactly in step and Blend never sounds hollow. The harp's chords always send their lowest notes round the bend, so they stay clear instead of turning to mud.
  - Each colour's loudness was measured on drum loops and on strummed chords at every quarter of the knob and is taken back out: turning it up changes the tone, not the volume.

- **Drum sounds** come from one general drum synthesizer, built from how the classic machines and the best drum synths work (published circuit studies of the classic machines, open-source drum-synth code, physically informed shaker models and synthesis tutorials; credited in `NOTICE.md`). Each sound is a short recipe of layers: oscillators with a fast pitch drop and a slow sag; a pulse ringing a resonant filter (the bridged-T way of the classic machines); several drum-head modes at the ratios of a real membrane; a bank of square waves through band-pass filters for hats, cymbals and cowbell; noise that is white, pink or clocked, with clap bursts, gated tails and snare wires that follow the head; beads and jingles colliding for shakers and tambourines; and dozens of decaying partials for cymbals and bells. Each sound is rendered in three slightly different takes that are played in turn, and every sound is brought to the same loudness (over 40 ms, 300 ms and, for kicks, a second), so swapping one never upsets the mix.
- **Playing without crackles.** The browser works out all of the app's sound on one thread, however many cores the computer has, so the app keeps that work small and gives it room: the sound runs with the browser's larger buffer (about 20 ms rather than 10, which absorbs a busy moment; songs are booked a quarter of a second ahead, so nothing waits); an effect at 0 is not fed at all (an echo once its repeats have died away), a track's saturation at Drive 0 runs only its clean path, a synth that has gone quiet is let go of until its next note, and a stopped track rests until it plays again; tracks with the same kind and size of reverb share one room (each sends to it through its own level, fade and fader, so it sounds the same), and a room that changes rings out instead of being cut; the whole mix passes a safety stage with no look-ahead (the same in a WAV and in the backing you sing over): everything under −1 dBFS passes bit for bit; the tip of a peak over it (a drum hit on a full mix) is rounded off smoothly toward −0.2 dBFS, leaving the level alone; a sound held over it is brought down to it over about 20 ms; anything more than 1 dB over full scale is held down at once. Everything stops with a short fade (takes, the noise bed, a piano's release noise). When the page is held up, a note or drum hit whose moment has passed starts as far into itself as it is late, faded in over 4 ms so it never starts with a click (only the latest hit of each drum; one whose sound is already over is left out), so nothing piles up; and around a page switch the song is booked a second ahead instead of a quarter, so a page that takes a moment to open leaves nothing late.
- **Drum effects** are a small mixing desk of Web Audio nodes: each drum has its own level, pan, and reverb and echo sends; the kit then passes through the saturation above, a tape stage (a short delay whose length drifts), tone, a compressor and a soft ceiling that adds no delay. The reverbs are generated stereo impulse responses (a spring is an impulse sent round a loop of 60 all-pass filters; the gated room is a flat burst that stops dead). The sampler modes hold samples at a lower rate without filtering and quantise them, as the old sampling drum machines did. The noise beds (vinyl crackle, tape hiss, room tone, hum) are rendered once as seamless loops. Saturation, compression and tone are loudness-compensated from measurements of the finished output. The drum grid re-locks to every chord change, so the beat and the chords cannot drift apart.

- **The piano** is a real grand, recorded: every third key (each one a minor third from the next) struck at five strengths, from a whisper to full force, so each note is played by the recording nearest it, moved at most a semitone or so and tuned to the piano's own slightly stretched scale (the bass a little flat, the treble a little sharp, as tuners leave a real one). A harder touch picks a harder-struck recording, which is brighter as well as louder; played a little softer than a recording was made, it is darkened toward the next softer one, so the tone brightens smoothly as you play harder rather than in five steps (mixing the two recordings instead makes some overtones cancel, which sounds hollow). Letting a key go drops the damper: the note stops in a fraction of a second (longer in the bass, where the strings are heavy), with the soft thud and ring of the felt landing; the top keys have no dampers and ring on, as on a real piano. The sustain pedal holds everything let go until it lifts. Striking a key again stops its previous sound, and when more than 64 notes ring at once the oldest give way with a short fade. The recordings download once (about 19 MB, in the background: the first 4 MB are enough to play every key, middle octaves first) and stay on the device, so the installed app plays them offline.
- **The electric piano** is a classic tine piano: 11 recorded keys at three strengths, each a short attack followed by a loop of its steady tone, faded as a struck tine fades and stopped quickly by the damper. It has a treble control and a tremolo or autopan, and is under 1 MB. Each recording is retuned from its own loop, so every key is within a cent of true pitch. Both keyboards play as loud as the guitar does the same notes, so a track can switch between them without a jump in level.

Everything runs locally in your browser. Your ideas are saved in the browser's local storage. When the app is opened as a claude.ai artifact, each saved idea is also written to your private area of the artifact's database, so the same library shows up on your other devices.

### Harmony for a new track, and chords on a piano

A melody or chords track can be written to fit one that is already there (`src/theory/harmonize.ts`, `lines.ts`, `layers.ts`). Where it goes: the track on screen when it is empty in this section, or holds a harmony exactly as it was written there; else another empty track of its kind here; else a new track (`writeTarget` in `src/song/tracks.ts`). A track remembers, section by section, which track and which controls it was written with, so a plain row can be hand-written in A and a harmony in E.

How it chooses. Every line and every chord layer is chosen for the whole phrase at once (a Viterbi search, `search.ts`), not note by note, against the notes that really sound in the section: the other chords tracks as their instrument voices them (the guitar's stock shapes in its tuning and capo, or the keys' layout), their lowest note as the bass, and every tune. Each candidate note is priced by how rough it sounds with all of that (the Sethares and Hutchinson–Knopoff roughness models, ported from open research code, `consonance.ts`), by its interval with the tune (3rds and 6ths best, a unison or a semitone worst), by whether it is a chord tone, an available tension or an "avoid" note, and by its register (out of the chords' band, never a step from one of their notes). Each move is priced the way voice-leading teaching does: steps first, the tune's direction followed, the same interval kept, no parallel 5ths or octaves with the tune or the bass, a leap answered by a step back, no melodic tritone.

- **A melody from a melody.** *Low* and *High harmony*: a second voice a 3rd or 6th away, in the tune's rhythm (quick runs share one note in the middle setting). *Stacked*: a voice below, then one above it, a close triad around the tune. *Counter-melody*: on the other side of the tune, moving where it holds, contrary where it can. *Long notes*: the 3rd and 7th of each chord (or 1-3-5, or 7-9-13 as colour rises), held, never coming in on the tune's own note. *Answer*: the tune's last notes echoed, in their rhythm and shape, where it holds or breathes. From chords: *Long notes*, *Counter-melody* and *Chord notes* (a walk through each chord, over the tune), all hearing the tune.
- **Chords with chords.** *High chords* (1-3-5, 3-5-7 or 5-7-9 of each chord), *Airy* (1-5, 1-5-9, 1-5-9-6/11), *Guide tones* (3-1, 3-7, 7-9), *Top note* (sol, mi, re): each written as the exact notes it plays (`ProgChord.notes`), above Chords 1 and in its gaps, never a semitone above one of its notes nor rubbing a strong note of the tune, moving as little as it can, and playable on the guitar (a fingering is found for exactly those notes; the keys play them as they are). The chord's name says what the two tracks make together (C with E G B on top: Cmaj7). The notes play only while every one belongs to the chord: change the chord or pick a shape by hand and the track is voiced as usual again.
- **Instead.** *Relative*, *Darker*/*Brighter* and *Lead-ins* play in place of the chords they change, in this section; each swap is checked against every tune note on a beat (2 and 4 too) and never puts a #9 under the tune. *Best fit* searches the best row of the key's chords for a tune; *Another fit* the best row that differs from it.
- **The controls.** *Simpler ↔ Busier* (how many notes or changes), *Closer ↔ Wider* (how far from the tune or Chords 1), *Plain ↔ Colourful* (chord tones only, or tensions too). They shape the notes, never the sound.
- **Measured.** A quality suite (`harmony.quality.test.ts`) runs every option on nine sections, with Chords 1 on the guitar and on the piano, against the first engine's numbers kept in `fixtures/baseline.json`: for example the high harmony's clash time went from 3.4× the tune's own to 0.65×, its rubs against the chords from 11.5 % to 1.9 %; chords played along now add a fifth of the roughness they did or less, double far fewer of Chords 1's notes (6–27 %, against 68 % at best before), and never add a second bass. Rendered to audio through the app, every harmony tested was less rough than the first engine's (median 15 % less for melody lines, 23 % for chords).
- The older options (*Richer*, *Sus*, *Pedal bass*, *Pad*, *Higher voicing*, *Shell*, *Smooth inversions*, *Passing dim*, *Tritone swap*, *Sixth below*, *Octave*, *Drone*) are kept only so a track written with one can still be written again with it. The same input always gives the same result.

**Chords on a piano.** `src/keys/voicing.ts` chooses which keys to press: close, open, drop 2, shell or spread shapes, inside a register (pad, middle or low), at most eight keys, with nothing muddy down low (no seconds under E3, nothing closer than a fifth under B2, only octaves under E2), no semitone between the two top or the two bottom keys, never a 9th, 11th or 13th in the bass, and each hand within reach. Across a progression it searches every candidate shape of every chord together and picks the row where the hands move least: held notes count in its favour, big leaps in the top voice and parallel fifths against it. The keyboard diagram (`KeyboardDiagram.tsx`) shows a voicing at the size of a guitar chord box: pressed keys in the same colours as the guitar dots (the root ringed), their part in the chord or their names underneath, and which hand plays what when a shape needs both.

Sources: open music-theory teaching (counterpoint, applied chords, modal mixture) and several open-source harmony, voicing and keyboard libraries, whose rules were re-implemented and whose numbers and small routines were ported; each is credited with its licence in `NOTICE.md`.

### Where the harp, the styles and the effects come from

The numbers and patterns were collected from published acoustics research (harp plucking, the concert harp's soundboard and sympathetic strings, string damping and plucked-string synthesis), teaching material (strumming, fingerpicking, clave and tresillo rhythms, banjo rolls, classical right-hand studies, harp accompaniment guides) and audio-engineering references (flanging and phasing, reverb types, shimmer and echo, circuit analyses of classic overdrive, chorus and phaser pedals, the Web Audio API documentation). The session that gathered them could read search-result excerpts of these pages, not always the full text, so treat the list in `NOTICE.md` as where to look rather than as a guarantee that every figure was checked against the original.

### Where the synth comes from

The panel's layout and the meaning of its sliders follow the parameter tables of a compact virtual-analogue keyboard synth, as transcribed in open-source editors for it; the curves (hertz, seconds), the effects' details and the preset recipes are this app's own, grounded in decoded factory sounds of open-source synths. The engine follows published algorithms and open-source code: band-limited steps after the PolyBLEP idea; a supersaw after published measurements of the original; a zero-delay-feedback ladder filter run at twice the sample rate with half-band filters; analogue-style envelopes, glide and voice stealing; slow drift; FM as phase modulation; antiderivative anti-aliased distortion; a small feedback-delay-network reverb. Every knob glides, nothing clicks, and every preset is checked for pitch, folded-back noise, loudness and clipping by the tests. Each source is credited with its licence in `NOTICE.md`.

### Where the piano comes from

The grand piano's recordings are a reduced set of freely licensed concert-grand recordings (CC BY 3.0); the electric piano is a port of an open-source electric-piano instrument (MIT), its recordings included. The sources, what was taken, what was changed and the licences are in `NOTICE.md` and `public/keys/*/LICENSE.md`; `scripts/piano-pack.py` rebuilds the piano's files from the originals.

### Where the canvas's way of turning sound into paint comes from

What it rests on, and how sure each part is:

- **Well supported by research:** higher pitch is seen as higher up, smaller and lighter; louder as bigger and heavier; harsh, rough or bright sounds as broken, angular marks; and the mood of a whole piece as colour.
- **Choices of this app, with no study behind them:** a staccato note as a dab and a held one as a stroke (it follows from holding the brush down exactly as long as the tone lasts), noise as spatter, and calm music as wet paint.
- **From open-source code** (see `NOTICE.md`): how a brush tip lags and overshoots, how touches overlap along a stroke, how spatter sizes are spread, how a brush runs dry, how wet paint is damped until it stops, and how pigments mix.
- **The layers, the layouts, the budget and the vibe** follow `research_notes/Synths harmony stems and vibe painting/vibe_painting.md` and `stems.md`. The measures of a song's vibe (timbre, harmony, rhythm, dynamics) are those music-similarity research uses; turning them into a painting smoothly, so that similar music looks similar, and the paint each part is given, are this app's own design, as are the coverage figures (a fifth for two quiet parts, two thirds for six loud ones). Splitting a file into layers uses published signal-processing methods (median-filter harmonic/percussive separation, and REPET-SIM for the voice), not a trained model; a model's stems (from the desktop app, later) can take their place. Marks are laid a hundredth of a second or so before their sound: broadcast practice finds pictures that lead the sound far easier to accept than pictures that lag it.

The full research, with every source read and its licence, is `reports/Realistic paint and sound painting.md`.
