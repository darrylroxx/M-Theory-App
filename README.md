# M-Theory 🎸

**Your own guitar chord, key, mode & progression lab. No subscriptions, no ads, works offline.**

M-Theory answers the questions that matter when you're writing songs on guitar:

- *"I'm in B. What chords can I use?"* All of them: diatonic, colour chords, borrowed, chromatic.
- *"How else can I play this chord?"* The shapes guitarists really use, starting from the common ones and branching out, not just the barre shape.
- *"What could come next?"* Suggestions from safe to wild, each with a plain-English reason.
- *"What mode is this, and how do I make it sound like that mode?"*
- *"What does it sound like over a beat?"* A drum machine with a library of about 115 synthesized sounds, 18 kits, 8 classic machines, 100+ rhythms, studio-style effects, and a separate rhythm for every section of the song.

---

## What's inside

**How the app is laid out.** Seven pages, chosen at the top: **Track**, **Frame**, **Chords**, **Melody**, **Drums**, **Vocals** and **Canvas** (where they do not fit on one line with the name and the buttons, on a phone either way up or a tablet held upright, the tabs get a line of their own). The frame directs: it sets the sections, how many bars each has, the key and the tempo, for every track. Chords, Melody, Drums and Vocals are what plays inside the section you are working on. Canvas is the song as a painting, made while it plays. Under the page tabs is the play bar, the same on every page. **▶** plays the song straight through, in order, from the section you are on to the end; with **Follow** on (see *Bars on screen*) the page goes along with it. Nothing loops unless you ask. Beside it is the **loop bar**: the whole song drawn bar by bar, section after section, which scrolls sideways. Tap a section's name to go to it. **Tap bars to loop them: every tap adds a bar** (tap 1, 2, 3 and three bars loop), and a bar further off brings everything in between with it, in the same section or across several. Tapping the bar at either end of the loop takes it off again; a bar in the middle becomes the whole loop. **↻** loops the whole section you are on, or switches looping off. **While something loops, a section's name puts that section in the loop or takes it out** (a name with **+** is not looping), so any sections can loop, next to each other or not, say the verse and the chorus without the bridge between them: they play in the song's order, round and round. **All** loops the whole song. Looped bars and names are drawn dark, and a line marks the bar that is sounding. A loop can be changed while the music plays: the change comes in where the section playing ends, in time (let go, the song plays on from there to its end). So can the song itself (a section removed, made shorter or longer, a chord added or taken out): the music keeps its place, what is sounding plays out with its own drums and its own mark, and the song goes on from just after it (a section removed while it plays ends with the bar sounding, and one made shorter goes on to the next section). Both stay put as you scroll.

**The Track page.** The whole song on one page, left to right in bars, the way a studio app shows it: one row per track, and a block in every section that has that track, drawn from what is in it (the chords by name, the melody's notes, the drum hits as they are heard: a drum muted in its kit's Mixer is left out). Chords that a harmony plays instead of in a section are drawn faint there, and pointing at the block says which track plays in their place. Where a section does not have a track, its slot is empty with a faint **+** that puts the track there, plain and empty. The sections and the Track page are the same song seen two ways: whatever is written on a section's pages shows up in its blocks, and a tap on a block opens that section's page for that instrument. Opening the page brings the section you were working on into view, and a track added on another page since is scrolled to and lit for a moment. Each row's head is the mixer strip for the **whole song**: its volume, **M** (mute) and **S** (solo) apply in every section at once and are saved with the idea, and the little instrument in front of its name is what plays it in the section you are on. **✕** on a row takes that track out of every section (it asks first). A muted track is not painted on the Canvas either. A tap on a track's own page (a note, a chord, a drum, a take) plays through the same strip, so while M (or another track's S) keeps a track quiet, its page shows that **M** (or an **S**) next to its tab: tap it to hear the track again (unmuted, or soloed along with the others). While the song plays a line runs across the page; looped bars are shaded. **Bars** sets how many bars fill the width (**All**: the whole song on one screen); the rest scrolls sideways, and with **Follow** on the lanes turn a page when the line runs off them.

**More tracks.** Every section has one Chords, one Melody and one Drums track (they can be empty, or off). Any section can have more, and only that section has them: two melodies in A, one in B. **+** next to a page's track tabs (Chords, Melody, Drums) adds a plain new track of that kind to the section you are on, at once: it is empty, it starts on the plain sound (Guitar and the default harp for chords and melody, the default kit for drums), its tab is chosen and you stay where you are. Nothing is asked and nothing is copied. The tabs only ever show the tracks of the section on screen, and each section remembers the tab you were on there: on Chords 2 in A, over to B (which has no Chords 2) and back, the page is on Chords 2 again. A track keeps its row on the Track page in every section that has it: **+** in B after Chords 2 was added in A gives B its own Chords 2 on the same row (the lowest-numbered one B does not have yet). **✕** on a track's tab takes it out of the section you are on only (it asks first only when the track has music there); a track that is then in no section leaves the song. **+** at the bottom of the Track page shows the four kinds; tap one and a new track is there, in the section you are on, its row lit for a moment, and its page opens on it next time. Vocal tracks run across the whole song. Every track has its own mixer strip (volume, M, S) for the whole song and plays alongside the others.

**The Vocals page.** Recording, in the Windows app. It talks to the audio interface through its ASIO driver, playing and recording on the interface's own clock.
- **Record (●)**: the song of the loop is rendered and handed to the engine; a count-in (none, 1 or 2 bars in the time signature of the loop's first bar, accented on the first beat, counted down on screen) plays **once**, and then the loop goes round and round, **every pass a new take**, for as long as you keep singing, until **■**. It is made for staying in the flow: idea after idea, pass after pass, until something comes out that you want to keep. The loop (set with the bar strip; the section on screen if none; sections looped apart are sung over from the first to the last, with all between, as a take is one piece of sound) is locked while recording, and the main **▶** cannot start a second song over it. So is the idea (Ideas cannot open another or start a new one), and the vocal track being recorded on cannot be removed, until the takes are on it. **▶ Rehearse** sings along without recording.
- **While you sing**: the pass you are on is shown big (**Pass 7**), a thin line fills as the pass goes by, and every pass already sung waits in a row beneath. **Tap a pass to keep it ★** (tap it again to let it go), or tap the big **★** or press **K** on the keyboard for the pass you are singing (in the first moments of a pass, they keep the one you have just finished: a pass is known to be the one only once it is over). K works whatever you last touched on the page, a slider or the click included. Nothing pops up and nothing on the page moves while you sing; small notes appear beside the pass number after half an hour, when the disk has less than 2 GB left, and when the recording is near the longest a file can hold.
- **The lane**: the bars being recorded over, numbered, as many at a time as *Bars on screen* says, across the whole width (the control is at the top of the page). More bars than that scroll sideways inside the lane, by hand or with **‹ ›**. Each recording is a row of clips, each drawn with its waveform where it plays, and a line moves with the music while it plays or records. With **Follow** off the lane never moves on its own, so a page of bars can be looked at while the loop goes round; with it on, the lane turns a whole page when the music runs off it. In the browser the lane is there too (without recording), and its line follows the song as it plays.
- **The click** (Click on, and the count-in) follows the time signature of the bar being played: a loop that runs from a 4/4 section into a 3/4 one clicks four and then three, with the accent on the first beat of every bar, on every pass.
- **Lined up**: the take is moved by the round trip the driver reports (when the first beat was heard against when the voice was captured), so it sits on the beat; a timing nudge (Vocals, *On the interface*: more if takes sound late, less if early) corrects anything the driver does not count, and moves the takes already in the song as well as every recording after. The file holds one sample for every frame of song played, however long the session: wherever voice is lost (the computer could not keep up, the disk was busy for more than 20 seconds), silence takes its place, so every later pass stays exactly where it was sung. How much was filled is said when you stop.
- **Takes**: one recording is one file in *Music › M-Theory › Recordings*, cut into one take per pass at **■**. The passes you kept are marked **★**, and the last of them is the one that plays (with none kept: the last full pass). Sung again over the same bars, the new recording plays there instead (what the earlier one had before and after it still plays).
  - **Sifting**: every take of every recording is listed (newest recording first): **▶** hears it alone (no song), **★** keeps it, **Use** makes it the one that plays there, **✕** deletes it from the song (the file stays; **↶** brings it back). **Remove** beside a recording's number takes the whole recording out of the song. **All / ★** filters the list. Step through with **‹ ›** or the arrow keys (one being heard, the next is heard), **Space** plays or stops, **Enter** uses, **K** ★s, **Delete** deletes. Deleting the take in use falls back to the last ★, else the last full pass. A pass stopped before half-way is kept but hidden unless **Short takes** is on.
  - **Editing** (on the lane): drag a clip's **edge** to trim it (a cough at the start cut off; the voice stays where it is), drag its **body** to move it (to whole 1/16 notes of the bar it lands in: in 6/8 that keeps eighth notes on the grid; **Snap** off, or Alt/Shift, moves freely; ↔ on a clip says it was moved), **✂** then a tap (or **S** at the pointer or the playing line) splits it, **Delete** or **✕** deletes the selected clip, and the take picker plays another take in a clip's place: the same stretch of that pass (comping). Two taps hear a clip alone. On a phone, a swipe across a clip scrolls the lane; tap a clip first to move it (its edges always trim). Every clip edge fades over 5 ms, so a cut never clicks; where a split's two pieces meet they cross over, so a held note goes on with no dip. Nothing is cut out of a file: a clip is a stretch of a take, saved with the idea, and **↶** or **Ctrl+Z** undoes each edit, each vocal track on its own. An edit made while the song plays is heard at once. The player, loops, the Track page (a clip running on into the next section is drawn there too), the Canvas and the WAV play exactly the clips.
  - Each clip is heard once, in its place, wherever the song starts: part-way through it, round a loop, after a loop is changed while playing, after a page switch, or when the page was too busy to keep up (it comes in that much further on, still on the beat). A take heard alone stops when the song plays, when the page is left, or when another is asked for.
- **Long sessions** (an hour or more) are safe: the voice is written to disk as it comes (a crash loses a second at most); the vocal sound is put on the recording a block at a time, never holding it all in memory, and the page shows how far it has got; a take is read back on its own, so playing one pass of an hour-long file costs no more than any other. If the driver resets in the middle (its buffer size or rate was changed), the recording is closed, handed back and cut into takes, and the interface opens again. A WAV file cannot be bigger than 4 GiB, so a recording stops cleanly a little before that (about 3 hours at 48 kHz) and says so; if the disk has no room left for the version with the vocal sound, the takes are kept dry and **↻ Apply sound** puts it on later.
- **Hearing yourself**: **Direct Monitor** (on the interface: dry, no delay) or **Through my effects**: the voice goes through the vocal chain to the headphones, a few milliseconds late. The **Buffer** choice on the page asks the interface's driver for a buffer size (no need for the driver's own panel): smaller is sooner, too small crackles; the page shows the round trip as measured from the driver and counts **dropouts**, and says when to go bigger. 64 at 48 kHz is a good start, 128 is safe for most computers. Through my effects stays on as you move between pages (a red dot on the Vocals tab says the mic is live).
- **Steady live sound**: the audio thread never waits, allocates or frees memory; reverb tails can't slow it down (denormals are flushed to zero); on Windows it joins the "Pro Audio" scheduler class, the app is never power-throttled, and the computer doesn't sleep while the interface is open. Extra monitoring delay left by a hiccup is taken out with a short crossfade. What the interface plays (the song, the click, your voice and, while you hear yourself through the effects, the app's own sound) never reaches full scale: a safety stage with no look-ahead rounds off the tip of a peak over −1 dBFS (smoothly, toward −0.2 dBFS, which it never reaches), brings a sound held over −1 dBFS down to it, and holds down at once anything more than 1 dB over full scale; under −1 dBFS everything passes untouched, so nothing is clipped, a normal mix keeps its level, and nothing moves in time.
- **Sharing the interface with the app**: the app plays through the computer's own sound; the recording side opens the interface directly only while you record or rehearse, while you hear yourself through the effects (then on every page), and on the Vocals page once the app has been quiet for a second. On the Vocals page ▶ (or a take heard alone) waits for it to close first, so the two never play through the interface at once, and ● opens it again at once. While you hear yourself through the effects, the app's own sound goes through the recording side too: the computer's own sound lets go of the interface (the mix fades out for a hundredth of a second, and in again from the recording side), so only one program ever plays through it. The song is then heard about a sixth of a second later (the Canvas allows for it); if the computer cannot keep it fed (it ran dry six times in a minute), it goes back to the computer's own sound for the rest of the session.
- **Vocal sound** (per vocal track): a studio vocal chain with no look-ahead, so the voice is never delayed. Pick a **preset** by the kind of record (Modern pop, Ballad, R&B / soul, Rap, Melodic rap, Rock, Slapback, Punk / metal, Indie, Dream pop, Ethereal, Surf / dub, Folk / acoustic, Dance, Lo-fi, Spoken …), then shape it:
  - **Colour**: Clean, Tube, Console, Tape, Silk or Exciter, with one Amount knob that changes the tone and not the volume. The five that bend the waveform run at twice the sample rate (about 0.05 ms), so the harmonics they add never fold back as harshness.
  - **EQ**: a graph. Frequency runs left to right (20 Hz to 40 kHz, each octave as wide as the next), decibels up and down, and the curve shows everything the EQ is doing. Six points to take hold of: low cut, Body (a low shelf), Low mid and Presence (bells), Air (a gentle shelf whose corner can sit above hearing: the shaded strip) and high cut. Drag a point sideways for its frequency and up or down for its gain; the mouse wheel on a bell makes it wider or narrower; a double-click puts a band back; arrow keys work too. What is drawn is what is heard: the bells and the air shelf are analog-matched, so at 48 kHz they keep their shape right up to 20 kHz, where ordinary digital EQ is about a decibel out. Settings glide, so a preset can be changed mid-note without a click.
  - **Dynamics**: Punch (a feedback FET-style compressor), Smooth (an optical one whose release is fast at first and then takes seconds), De-ess, Gate. The **de-esser** is split-band and judges how much of the voice is "s", not how loud it is, so it works the same sung softly or belted; it sits after the brightening EQ (so it tames what Presence and Air add, and keeps esses out of the reverb), takes off at most 9 dB and lets go in 15 ms. The **gate** is a gentle 2:1 expander: gaps are turned down by 6 to 18 dB, never to silence, it waits 150 ms before closing and cannot flutter. At the very end a **limiter** holds loud passages at −3 dBFS (turned down cleanly, not clipped) under a soft ceiling at −1 dBFS; a red **LIMIT** light shows when it is working, which means the level going in is too high.
  - **Width**: Double, Micro (a few cents flat on the left and sharp on the right) or Chorus.
  - **Echo**: Slap, Tape, Analog, Digital, Ping-pong or Ambient, at a slap or at 1/8, dotted 1/8 or 1/4 of the tempo. The time glides when changed.
  - **Reverb**: seven spaces, each a different design and not one engine re-tuned: **Plate** (bright, the classic), **Room** (one dense, dark, short burst behind six wall reflections), **Hall** (late and warm, the highs fading first), **Chamber** (eighteen parallel echoes: bright, thick within a moment), **Cloud** (twelve drifting loops a side: a wide wash that arrives after the voice and hangs for seconds), **Spring** (a guitar amp's tank) and **Shimmer** (a plate whose tail climbs an octave). Size, darkness and pre-delay on each; all come back about equally loud. Echo and reverb step back while you sing (Duck) and their lows are kept out of the mix.
  The research behind it, with every source and licence, is in `reports/Open source vocal plugin DSP.md` and its follow-up `reports/Open source vocal plugin DSP 2.md`; credits are in `NOTICE.md`.
- **Interface**: the level of Input 1 (mic) and Input 2 (instrument/line) in the interface's own colours, which input the track records, the song and click levels in the headphones, and a checklist of the interface's switches (no software can read them).

**A time signature for the song, and for any section that wants its own.** The Frame page sets the song's (2/4, 3/4, 4/4, 5/4, 6/4, 7/4, 6/8, 9/8, 12/8), and every section plays in it unless it has been given one of its own: the small signature on each section's row picks it (the song's own is marked with a dot, and picking it makes the section follow the song again). A section's signature alone says how long its bars are, on every page: the chord ruler, the melody grid, the drum grid, the Track view, the loop bar, the count-in and click for vocals, and the MIDI file, which writes each section's signature where it starts (and a new tempo where the beat changes between a quarter note and a dotted quarter, since the tempo counts beats as the app does). (It used to follow the drum rhythm, or the playing style when the drums were off, so two sections or two tracks could disagree.) Changing a signature takes what it covers along: chords that lasted whole bars still do, melody notes stay in their bars, every drum rhythm on every track is fitted to the new bars (6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 turn 16ths into triplets, and back). The song's signature takes along every section that follows it; a section's own takes along only that section, and every other section sounds as it did. **↶** takes either change back for a little while, as long as nothing else has changed since. A section in another signature that follows the first section's drums plays them fitted to its own bars, and still follows them. A rhythm picked from the library, or pasted from a section in another signature, is fitted the same way. This is enforced in one place (`song/unity.ts`), on every change. Recordings keep the signature of the section they were sung in.

**Tracks from any page.** The bar above the Chords, Melody and Drums pages (and the top of Vocals) lists the tracks of that kind in the section on screen, with a **+**: another chords layer, another melody, more drums, here; another vocal, for the whole song. What a section has shows on the Track view and on its row of the Frame page.

**Harmonize a new track.** **≈ Harmonize** sits in the Arrange row on top of the chord tray (Voicing on the keys) and in the Melody page's top row. It works in the section you are on. It asks one thing: **Harmonize with** which track (every chords and melody track of this section that has some music, the one on screen first). Then it offers the harmonies for that track, best first as music theory ranks them, and only those that give something here. The new track is the page's kind: on the Melody page a new melody line, on the Chords page new chords. A melody line with a tune: *High harmony* or *Low harmony* (a second voice a third above or below, bending to each chord), a *Counter-melody* that moves against the tune, *Long notes* (one held note per chord) and an *Answer* in the tune's gaps; with chords: *Long notes*, a *Counter-melody* and *Chord notes* (a walk through each chord). New chords with chords: *Richer* (7ths, 9ths and 6ths), *Sus*, *Darker* (some chords from the minor key; *Brighter* in a minor key, from the major one), *Relative* (each chord swapped for its relative), *Pedal bass* (one held bass note under them) and *Lead-ins* (a 7th chord pulling into the next); with a tune, *Best fit* comes first, chords chosen to carry the tune. Darker, Relative, Lead-ins and Best fit play instead of the chords they change, so in this section those chords are not heard while the new track is there (the hint says so, and so does the message; their page then shows *… plays instead* beside their tab, a tap there shows that track, and the Track page draws them faint there); every other section plays its chords as it did, and the mixer is left alone. Tapping an option plays the section you are on, round and round, with it added; tap another to hear that one instead, **✓** to keep it. Nothing about how it sounds is chosen for you: it starts as a plain track, and you set its instrument, sound and level yourself. It is written into this section only, in its time signature, length and chords, and added to this section only; the same song always gets the same harmony. **⟳** on its name (Track page, or its tab on its page) writes it again in the section you are on, with another option, or from its track after that changed (a dot on ⟳ says it has); notes you changed by hand are only written over after you say yes, and other sections keep what they have, the option they were written with included (Darker in A and Pad in a copy of A: A still plays Darker in place of its chords). **↶** takes either back for a while; removing one that played instead of chords lets them play again.

**Bars on screen.** The Chords, Melody and Drums pages (in the bar at the top, next to Copy and Paste), the Track page and the Vocals page share one control: **Bars 1 · 2 · 4 · 8 · All**, and **Follow**.
- The melody and drum grids put that many bars side by side, using the whole width of the screen. Each bar has its number over it, its own steps, a line between it and the next, and its own moving line while it plays. A drag paints straight across from one bar into the next. The chord ruler and the Track page fit that many bars into their width (**All**: the whole section, or on the Track page the whole song); the rest is a sideways scroll away.
- Until you choose, a wide screen shows 4 bars, a tablet 2 and a phone 1. Your choice is kept, and it is the same on every page.
- **The music never moves what you are looking at unless Follow is on.** With it off, the bars and the section on screen stay where you put them while the song plays: a line shows where the music is whenever its bar is on screen, a dot marks the tab of the bar that is sounding, and the play bar at the top always marks it too. With Follow on, the view turns a whole page when the music runs off the end of it (with 4 bars on screen: bars 1–4, then 5–8; with 1 bar, bar by bar as before) and goes on to the next section with the song. Switch Follow on while the song is in another section and the page goes straight there. It waits a moment after you touch the grid, and the drums stay put while you are editing them.
- The bar tabs choose the bar that ⧉, ✨ and Clear work on (a tap on a square, or on a bar's number, chooses it too). A tab whose bar is off screen brings its page with it; **‹ ›** turn a page.
- More bars than fit (on a phone, or **All** on a long section) scroll sideways inside the grid, with the note and drum names kept in view; the page itself never scrolls sideways. On a phone, melody squares stay at least 30 px wide.

**Drum mixer.** On the Drums page, **Mixer** (before Sound and Effects) has a fader, mute and solo for each drum and one fader for the whole kit.

**The Frame page.** A row for every section in playing order: the grip **⠿** to drag it anywhere in the song (with a mouse, or a finger on the grip; on a phone, holding the row a moment and dragging works too, and the page scrolls along near the top and bottom of the screen; the other rows make room where it would land; with the keyboard, ↑ ↓ on the grip, or Alt+↑ ↓ anywhere on the row), its name (tap another section's to work on it; tap the one you are on to rename it: Enter keeps the name, Esc leaves it as it was), its length (**−**, **+**, or tap the number for 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32 bars), its time signature, **Copy**, **Paste** and **✕** (removing a section with vocals recorded in it asks first), and a chip for **every track it has** (Chords, Melody, Drums, the tracks added to that section, and each vocal track) that shows what that track holds in the section (how many chords; how many recordings) and opens its page there with that track ready. **+ B** adds a new section after the one you are on: no chords, no melody and no added tracks yet, and its Chords, Melody and Drums start with a copy of how they sound in the section it was made from (style, harps, instruments, kit and drum rhythm), so it sounds the same until you change it. **↻ A** plays a section a second time (the same music; **Unlink** makes a repeat a section of its own, an exact copy, added tracks and all, so it can differ).

**Letters and names.** Sections are lettered in the order they first play: whatever plays first is A, the next new one B, and so on, so dragging C to the front makes it A. Any section can be given a name of its own instead ("Verse", "Hook", up to 24 characters; an empty name gives the letter back). A name belongs to the section, so it goes with it wherever it is moved, and both blocks of a section played twice share it. Each section also keeps its own colour wherever it goes. The name is used everywhere the letter was: the play bar, the Track view, the top of the Chords, Melody and Drums pages, the chord chart and the list of ideas.

**Copy and paste a whole section.** **Copy** on a row takes everything in that section: its tracks and what each holds there (chords, melodies, drums: rhythm and kit), how each of them sounds there (instrument, style, harp, effects), its length and its time signature, and the vocals recorded in it. Every other row then offers **Paste**, which puts all of that in place of what that section had, its tracks too (asking first if it has anything in it). A section played twice changes in both places, as an edit there would; the recordings go only into the block pasted into, as recordings of their own that play the same audio files (nothing is copied or deleted on disk). The pasted section keeps its own name and colour, and the other sections keep sounding as they did, even when the paste goes into the first section. A message with **↶** offers to take the paste back for about 20 seconds, as long as nothing else has changed since. The copy is kept when the app is closed, so a section can be pasted into another idea; its tracks are matched by name there (or added), and in another key the chords and melodies move to that key. Below: the **key** and mode of the whole song, **Move song** (key, chords and every melody, the added tracks' too, up or down a semitone together), the **tempo**, and **Export**: **Song as WAV** (the whole song as you hear it, every track and the recorded vocals, with the Track page's levels, mute and solo; 24-bit, 48 kHz, never clipping, the last reverb left to ring out), **MIDI file** (every track by name: chords, melodies, drums; recorded vocals are audio, so they are in the WAV), and chords and tab as text (the added chords tracks too). Recordings keep the tempo and key they were sung in: if either is changed afterwards, the page says so (and offers the tempo back).

**The frame is literal.** A section is exactly as many bars as it is set to, and Rhythm, Melody and Drums each show every one of those bars. **What is in a bar plays; an empty bar is silent.** Nothing comes round again on its own.

- **Rhythm:** the chord ruler is drawn as long as the section. Chords fill it from bar 1; bars with no chord are drawn empty and play nothing. **+ Rest** adds a bar of silence between chords (it can be dragged and resized like a chord). Chords that run past the last bar are struck out, with one tap to make the section long enough.
- **Melody:** the grid has a tab for every bar of the section. Bars with no notes are drawn hollow.
- **Drums:** the grid has a tab for every bar too. A rhythm you pick fills every bar; in Edit, **Clear** empties the chosen bar so it plays nothing, and **⧉** copies the bar before into it. Each bar can be changed on its own.

Making a section shorter never deletes anything: what lies past the end is kept and comes back if the section is made longer again (a vocal take sung from a bar past the end too: until then it is not heard, and not drawn on any page). A new section starts as long as the one it was made from. Songs made before sections had lengths get one when they are opened, and anything that used to come round again in them is written out in full, so they sound as they did.

**Every section sounds its own way.** Every track in every section has its own settings: its instrument (Guitar, Piano or Synth, and how that is set; on the guitar also where on the neck its chords are arranged), playing style and feel, harp and effects, the melody's harp, and the drums' kit (the machine, each drum's sound, the mix, the effects), rhythm and on/off. Changing one never changes another track or another section: B can play the chords on the piano and A on the strings, and the sound changes exactly where B starts (live, in the WAV and in the MIDI file). Chords and melody notes always belong to their own section too. When one track should sound the same everywhere, **All sections** next to its sound (on the Chords and Melody pages beside Strings and Effects, on the Drums page beside Mixer, Sound and Effects) copies how it sounds here to the same track in every other section that has it: its instrument (and Arrange), style, harp and effects, or its whole kit, never its notes or its drum rhythm (a message with **↶** takes it back). Songs saved by older versions open sounding exactly as they did, every section with what it played then.

**Copy and paste between sections, one page at a time.** The Chords, Melody and Drums pages each have **Copy** and **Paste** at the top, next to the name of the section on screen. Copy takes the track on screen in that section: its music and how it sounds there (its instrument and, on the guitar, its Arrange, style, harp and effects; on the Drums page the rhythm and the kit). Paste puts both into the track on screen in the section on screen, in place of what is there. (A copy made by an older version holds only the music: the track keeps its sound.) Each page has a clipboard of its own, so chords only paste as chords, a melody as a melody and drums as drums, and copying drums never wipes a copied rhythm. Paste shows the section it comes from, and turns into **↶ Undo** for a little while after a paste.
- **Chords** copies the chords (lengths, rests and chosen shapes).
- **Melody** copies the notes, the grid and on/off.
- **Drums** copies the drum rhythm and on/off (the kit stays the one the track has).

A paste changes only that page: the section keeps its length (the Frame decides it) and its other two pages. If what is pasted runs past the end of the section, the part past the end is kept but does not play, and you are told. Pasting drums into section A changes the rhythm the other sections follow, just as changing A by hand does. The clipboards are kept when the app is closed, so a part can be pasted into another idea too; if that song is in another key, the chords and the melody are moved to its key.

On the **Chords** page:

1. **Key**: tap one of the 12 notes.
2. **Mode**: Major, Minor, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Locrian, Harmonic minor, Melodic minor (27 more under "More…").
3. **Chords appear**: one card per note of the scale, each drawn as an easy shape with finger numbers. **Tap a card to hear it**, and everything else the key offers on that note is listed under it by kind: sus, sixths, sevenths, added notes, ninths and beyond (56 chord types are checked against the scale, so nothing that fits is left out and nothing foreign gets in). A row of ten kinds chooses what the cards show: **Triads, Power, Sus2, Sus4, 6ths, 7ths, Add9, 9ths, 11ths, 13ths**. Each kind is built on every note of the scale and shown only where it uses notes of the key alone (so there is no sus2 on the third note of a major key); a note with no chord of that kind is left hollow. Two choices follow standard practice: ninths leave out a flattened ninth over a minor chord, and elevenths use the raised eleventh on major chords and the suspended form on dominants, because a plain eleventh clashes with a major third. Card colours show each chord's job: 🟢 Home · 🔵 Near home · 🟠 Away · 🔴 Tension; ★ = holds the mode's signature note. **Outside** has borrowed and surprise chords; **Next** suggests what could follow your last chord. On a phone the cards are one row to swipe through.
4. **Shapes** (the button on every chord) opens the shape finder: the ways guitarists really play that chord, starting from the ones everybody knows.
   - **Common**: the textbook open chord, the two movable barre shapes (root on the 6th string, root on the 5th string), the small four-string shape, and for seventh chords the standard jazz grips.
   - **Small**: three- and four-string shapes with no big barre, grouped by string set and running up the neck. The way out of playing barre chords for everything.
   - **Inversions**: the same chord with another note in the bass (C/E, C/G), grouped by bass note.
   - **All**: everything playable, grouped by where it sits on the neck.
   - Tap a shape to hear it and use it. **Flavours** along the top swap the chord itself (C → Cmaj7, Cadd9, Csus2, C6…; "All…" has all 56 types).
   - Only comfortable grips are listed: no muted strings in the middle of a chord (except the classic thumb-muted ones like 3x0003 and 5x555x), no stretch wider than four frets, no open strings mixed with notes far up the neck.
5. **Build the progression in the tray.** It sits under the chords, on the Chords page. While it is off screen a slim bar at the bottom of the screen stands in for it: ▶, your chords by name (tap one to hear it, drop new ones on it), and an arrow back to the full tray. On a phone this leaves about four fifths of the screen free. Drag chords in (touch: press and hold, then drag; or tap **+**), drag to reorder, ✕ to remove. The finder stays open above the tray, so you can **drag one exact shape straight into the tray**. Tap a chord in the tray to hear it; the small grid button under it opens the finder for that spot (shape, flavour, length, position).
   - **How long each chord lasts**: the strip under the tray is the section drawn to scale, every chord as wide as it is long, with a line at each bar. **Drag the edge between two chords** to move where the change falls inside the bar (the two share the time). **Tap a chord** in the strip to set its length exactly: − / + in half beats, or 1 beat, ½ bar, 1 bar, 2 bars. A bar that is not full yet is shown hatched.
6. **Arrange** (the bar on top of the tray): **≈ Harmonize** writes a new track to go with a track you choose (see *Harmonize a new track*). Once you have two chords, tap a neck area (**Open · 1–3 · 4–6 · 7–9 · 10–12**) and every chord of the track on screen is re-voiced there together, picking the set of shapes that needs the least hand movement. The bar shows the frets they now span. Arrange belongs to that track in that section: Chords 2, or the chords of another section, keep their own.
   - **Strings** (Any / 3 / 4 / 5–6) and **Inversions** (let the bass note change for smoother moves) re-deal the set.
   - **‹ ›** under each chord in the tray sifts that one chord through its other shapes, best fit with its neighbours first, while everything else stays put. Do it while the loop is playing to hear each option in context.
   - Chords you add later land in the same area. **My shapes** switches back to each chord's own shape.
7. **Melody** (the second part of a section): a grid of notes. **Tap a square and that note plays at that moment**; tap it again to take it out.
   - Rows are notes, two octaves from the key's home note, named the way the key names them. **Rows that belong to the key are lit** and the home note is brightest; the others are grey. **Key only** hides the grey rows, so no tap can be out of key.
   - **A dot marks the notes of the chord playing underneath**, and each chord's name sits above the step where it comes in. A tune that keeps to lit rows and dots cannot sound wrong.
   - **Grid**: ♩ ♪ ♪3 ♬ sets how many steps there are to a beat. Notes never move when you change it. **Bar** tabs choose a bar (**‹ ›** turn a page of bars), ⧉ copies the bar before into the chosen one, ↶ Undo, Clear empties it. With the mouse, drag along a row to write (or rub out) several notes at once. A line shows where the music is (see *Bars on screen*).
   - **It has an instrument of its own**, under the grid: **Guitar**, **Piano** or **Synth**, with its own sound and effects on a channel separate from the chords. Echo on the melody is not echo on the chords. On the strings each note rings until the next one; on the keys and the synth it is held until the next one (never past the end of its section). On the synth the tune is played its way too: a lead glides from note to note, an arp or a pulse runs through each note, a drone holds under the tune.
   - **↶** undoes the last note on the melody track you are on; switching track starts its undo afresh, and so does a change made to the melody on another page (**Move song**, a new time signature), so ↶ never puts back notes from before it.
   - Every section of the song has its own melody. A melody shorter than its section comes round again; one that is longer makes the chords repeat under it. It plays without any chords too. MIDI export puts it on a track of its own.
8. **The chords' instrument** (on the Chords page, under the tray). **Guitar · Piano · Synth**: each chords track plays one of them, chosen there.
   - **Every track has its own tone.** Each chords, melody and drums track keeps its own instrument, sound and effects, separate from the others, so tracks layer: a grand piano under a synth pad under a plucked guitar, two drum tracks with different kits. Each section has its own copy of them, so a track can sound one way in A and another in B (see *Every section sounds its own way*).
   - **Guitar**: the plucked strings below (harps, guitars and bells), built for writing with: warm, clear and never tiring.
   - **Piano**: **Grand** or **Electric**. **Keys** has **Bright** (duller … brighter; you hear it as you turn it), **Volume** and **Pedal** (the sustain pedal, changed with every chord). The playing styles still apply; Octave, Shimmer and Wide are only for strings.
   - **Synth**: the synth below, on the track, with its ready-made sounds, macros and ways of playing. A new synth track starts on a pad (chords) or a lead (melody). Its way of playing takes the place of the playing style and runs over the whole section, so an arpeggio carries on across the chords and a drone holds through them. Switching back to Guitar keeps the synth's sound for later.
   - **On the keys the chord pictures are keyboards**: the cards, the tray and the chord's sheet show the keys the track plays, laid out together so the hands move least. **Voicing** (Close, Open, Spread, Drop 2, Shell, and **≈ Harmonize**) replaces Arrange; a piano starts on Spread (the root in the left hand), the synth on Open. **⋯** under a chord opens its sheet: its keys on a bigger keyboard (tap to hear), its flavours, moving it, its length, Remove and its info. Tapping a chord plays it on the track's own instrument.
   - Switching instrument while the music plays fades the old one out in a few hundredths of a second, so nothing clicks. The piano's recordings load the first time it is chosen (a dot on the switch while they do); ▶ waits a moment for them, so the first chord is heard.
   - **Style ‹ ›**: 136 ways to play the chords, in eleven groups. The arrows flip through a group, the name opens the list, and picking one plays it straight away.
     - **Simple** (let ring, plucked, on the beat), **Strumming** (the island strum and its relatives, sixteenth-note ballad and pop strums, strums with a muted "chuck" on two and four), **Bass & strum** (boom-chick, bluegrass, bolero), **Fingerpicking** (alternating thumb, pinches, banjo-style rolls, bass and chord), **Arpeggios** (rolling up and down, ripples, sixes, triplets), **Harp** (rolled chords, broken chords, roving thumb, cascades, glissando), **Rock** (muted eighths, gallops, stabs), **Funk & reggae** (scratched sixteenths, skank, ska, one drop), **Latin** (bossa nova, samba, rumba, tresillo, habanera, clave), **Shuffle & 6/8**, **Waltz**.
     - The pattern follows the bar, not the chord: two chords sharing a bar share one pass of the pattern, the way a player keeps the hand moving through a change. In a 3/4 or 6/8 drum rhythm every style changes to fit, and sixteenth-note styles follow the drums' swing.
     - **Feel** (under the list): the same style at **½×, 1× or 2×** speed, with more or less **Swing**, a lighter or heavier **Touch**, tighter or wider **Spread** of the strings, and an **Octave** lower or higher.
   - **Harp ‹ ›**: 22 instruments. *Harps*: Concert, Folk, Wire, Latin, Baroque, Bray (its strings buzz), Kora, Lyre, Electric, Harp harmonics. *Plucked*: Nylon guitar, Steel guitar, Koto, Dulcimer, Mandolin, Banjo, Ukulele, Pizzicato. *Bells*: Music box, Kalimba, Glass bells, Vibraphone.
   - **Sound ‹ ›**: 18 ready-made sounds that work on any of them (Natural, Warm, Bright, Dry and close, Dreamy, Ambient, Cathedral, Lo-fi tape, Underwater, Twelve-string, Halo, Dotted echo, Tremolo, Phaser, Valve glow, Big desk, Crunch, Fuzz). **+ Save this sound** keeps your own.
   - **Strings**: **Pluck** (the middle of the string, round and hollow … by the soundboard, thin and bright), **Soft** (nail … finger pad), **Ring** (how long the strings sing, and how far one chord runs into the next), **Body** (the soundboard's warmth and knock), **Octave** (every string doubled an octave up), **Wide**, Volume.
   - **Effects**, in the order a pedalboard is wired: **Saturation** (the same six colours as the drums: Tape, Tube, Console, Crunch, Fuzz, Clip, with a Blend knob), **Squeeze** (compressor), **Tone**, **Chorus**, **Phaser**, **Wobble** (tape wow and flutter), **Tremolo** (in time with the song), **Echo** (bouncing side to side, in time), **Reverb** (Hall, Room, Plate, Spring, Lo-fi) with Size, and **Shimmer** (a halo an octave above, heard in the reverb). The knobs change the character, not the volume. Double-click any knob to reset it.
9. **Drums** (the third part of a section). At first it shows only what you need: the kit, the rhythm and the grid.
   - **Kit ‹ ›**: the arrows flip through kits, the name opens the list.
     - 24 kits by sound and genre: Soft Box, Cassette, Dusty, Cold Wave, Dark Club, Neon, Arena, Studio, Brushes, Trap, Techno, Industrial, Glitch, Dub, Breaks, Analog, Mallets, Boom Bap, Garage, Funk, Disco, Jazz Club, Metal, Hand Drums. Each picks a sound for every drum, knob settings, effects and a colour of saturation.
     - 8 machines, each built the way the real one makes sound: **Vintage** (70s organ rhythm box), **808**, **909**, **707**, **Sampled** (80s machine playing real drums), **Pocket** (a small analog box), **Lo-fi** (8-bit), **Modular**.
     - **+ Save this kit** keeps your own setup for other songs.
   - Every section has its own rhythm and its own kit, and the drums can be off in one section and on in the next.
   - **Every drums track is a kit of its own**: its own effects, noise bed, pump and fader, so Drums 2 can be drenched in reverb while Drums 1 stays dry, and soloing or muting one never touches the other. Switching drums track closes an edit in progress and lets go of a soloed drum, and so does leaving the Drums page (a drum's solo is only for listening while you work there).
   - **Rhythm ‹ ›**: 286 rhythms in fifteen styles: Pop, Rock, Indie, Alternative, Dream pop, Dark wave, Electronic, Hip hop, 808 & trap, Funk & soul, Jazz, Punk & metal, 6/8 & shuffle, Latin & world, Organ presets. These are the standard beats of each style (four on the floor, boom bap, trap, drill, two-step, the classic break, motorik, the swing ride pattern, bossa, the skank beat, the blast beat and so on), named for what they are. Some are two bars long, with a fill or an answering bar; some are in 3/4, 5/4, 6/8, 12/8 or a swung jazz 3/4 and 5/4. In the jazz rhythms the ride cymbal is on the Hat row and the hi-hat foot on the Shake row (the Jazz Club kit puts the right sounds there).

Tap **✎ Edit**, then tap squares (or drag along a row to paint). **↶ Undo** steps back one change at a time, **Cancel** throws the changes away, **✓ Save** keeps them and locks the grid again. A new time signature for the section (on the Frame page) keeps them and locks the grid too: the beat is fitted to the new bars, changes and all. A rhythm you changed is also stored under "Mine". While editing: Soft / Normal / Loud for new hits, ✨ for a small random change, Clear.

While playing, a line runs through the bar that is sounding; with **Follow** on, the grid turns a page when the music runs off it (never while you are editing).
   - **Sound**: tap a drum's name. **‹ sound ›** flips through that drum's library (a snare has 23: 808, 909, Acoustic, Gated, Dusty, Brush, Piccolo, Crack, Modal… plus each machine's), then **Tune, Decay, Click / Snap / Bright, Punch / Body / Attack, Drive, Filter**, and its place in the mix: **Level, Pan, Reverb, Echo**. The picture shows the sound change as you move a knob. **M** mutes, **S** solos, ↺ puts it back. Double-click any knob to reset just that one.
   - **Effects** for the whole kit: one-tap vibes (**Clean, Warm, Fat, Punch, Dreamy, Cassette, Dusty, Gated 80s, Big Room, Crunch, Blown out, Dub, Pump**), or set them yourself:

A quarter turn is colour you feel more than hear; full is distortion. **Blend** sends only part of the kit through it, which keeps the clean punch underneath.

   - **Drag** a section by its grip to move it anywhere (on a phone: hold the row, then drag). Whatever ends up first is A. ✕ removes it.
   - **↻ A** / **→ Song** next to ▶ choose between going round the section you are on and playing on through the song, each section with its own settings (with **Follow** on, the page goes along).

Save lights up whenever there are changes the library does not have. In the claude.ai version the library is also kept in your Claude account, so it follows you between devices; **Backup** and **Restore** move it as a file.

The Frame page has the tempo, **Move song** (the melody moves with the key) and **MIDI export** (each chords track exactly as it plays, each melody on its own track, drums on channel 10; every track named and set to an instrument close to its own: grand piano, electric piano, a harp for the strings, the synth by its kind of sound, whose chords are written held, or as the notes its arpeggio plays when it is on **Arp**), copy chords + tab (keys by name for a track on the keys), and saved ideas.

A **Capo** menu sits at the top of the Chords page, next to the key, for a chords track on Guitar; ★ marks the capo that removes the most barre chords (B major: capo 4). A shape chosen for one spot in the tray belongs to the capo and tuning it was chosen with: with another one on, that chord plays a shape that still makes it (the one it would have had anyway), and the chosen shape comes back with its capo and tuning.

Settings (⚙): avoid barre chords (on by default), 11 tunings, capo, dot labels, left-handed diagrams, light/dark theme.

**The synth** (choose **Synth** on any chords or melody track). A virtual-analogue synthesizer modelled on a small, much-loved analogue-style keyboard whose whole sound is one flat panel of sliders, with what that keyboard leaves out for someone starting out: ready-made sounds, big macro knobs and ways of playing.
- **The panel** is the original's, section by section. **Osc**: five ways of making sound, each with two sliders, **Texture** and **Mod**, that mean something different for each: *Multi Saw* (Texture adds a sub an octave down, Mod spreads up to seven detuned saws into a supersaw), *Pulse* (Texture detunes and then retunes a second square to a fifth, an octave and so on; Mod narrows the pulse), *Sync* (a second oscillator locked to the first: Texture sets its pitch, Mod sweeps it with the envelope), *Ring* (two oscillators multiplied: bells and metal) and *FM* (one sine bending another: Texture is how much, Mod the ratio, resting on whole ratios with the clangy ones between). Plus noise. **LFO**: to the loudness, the filter, the pitch or the oscillator (on Pulse that is pulse-width modulation), with depth, speed, a shape and a fade-in for delayed vibrato. **Filter**: a resonant four-pole low-pass that sings on its own at the top, with drive and **key tracking** (high notes stay bright). **EG**: one envelope, with a balance slider choosing whether it shapes the loudness, the filter or both. **Effect**: distortion, chorus/flanger, phaser or delay, plus a **reverb** of its own. **Voice**: poly, mono or legato, glide, **velocity** (harder is louder and brighter), drift (the slow wander of old circuits), width and volume.
- **61 ready-made sounds** in Bass, Lead, Pad, Keys, Pluck, Brass, Strings, Bells, Drone, FX and Arp (and a plain start for each oscillator), all brought to the same loudness. **‹ ›** steps through a kind, **⚄** picks another of the same kind, a dot after the name says the sound has been changed.
- **Six macros**, big knobs that reshape any sound from where it was made: **Bright**, **Motion**, **Space**, **Grit**, **Width** and **Shape** (tight and short ↔ soft and long). Each moves several sliders at once, and the sliders move with it, so you can see what it did. Double-click a knob (or a slider) to put it back.
- **Ways of playing** the chords and notes already written: **Hold** (as written), **Arp** (a style: **Up**, **Down**, **Up & down**, **Skip**, **Bounce**, **Shuffle**, **Triplets** or **Random**, with **Speed** ¼ · ⅛ · 1/16 and **Range** 1–3 octaves; every step is heard as its own note on every sound, the track's reverb kept small behind it; a sound from the **Arp** kind turns it on; in 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8 the steps are the meter's own eighths and sixteenths), **Strum** (down, up or alternating, with a spread), **Pulse** (the chord struck again on the beat), **Drone** (the key's home note, its fifth and octave held under everything, with a softer voice moving to each chord's root, even when the keys play the chord as an inversion) and **Glide** (one line, sliding from note to note). **Loose** humanises any of them.
- **■ silences it** within about a third of a second, however long the sound's own release (a drone lets go over several seconds while the music plays; on ■ it fades out quickly, echoes and reverb with it), and ▶ straight after is heard in full.
- It sounds the same live and in an exported WAV: the same engine plays the same notes, sample for sample. It sounds the same at 44.1, 48 and 96 kHz too, and switching the oscillator keeps the loudness within about a decibel.
- No clicks when notes start, stop or are struck again harder, or when a whole new chord takes over every voice at once. Choosing **Drone** on a sound that dies away (a pluck, a bell) keeps it sounding under the passage.

## The Canvas: music paints itself

Open **Canvas** and press ▶, or open **any song from a file** (the folder button, or drop a file on the canvas: WAV, MP3, FLAC, OGG, and whatever else the browser can read) and press its ▶. While the music plays it is painted, live, in wet paint on a woven surface. The painting is a picture of the sound you are making: every part of the music has its own kind of paint, the canvas fills only as far as the music fills it, and music of one kind paints pictures of one kind. No two paintings come out the same: press **Clear** and play the same song again for new detail on the same picture. The painting stays while you look at other pages; another idea opened (or a new one started) begins on a bare canvas.

**The layers.** The music is heard as the parts that play together, and each part is painted in a paint you can tell from the others by its shape alone, even in grey. The row of little swatches under the canvas shows each one in the colours in use; it lights up while that part is playing, and a tap hides or shows that part.

- **Drums: impacts.** The kick is a soft round blot low down, a snare a burst of hard little drops, the hats a dry stipple of specks near the top, a cymbal a trail of specks as long as it rings.
- **Bass: broad, flat, heavy bands** along the bottom. A held note is one band; short notes are stacked slabs.
- **Chords: washes.** A held chord is a thin, wide wash from its lowest note to its highest, in the colours of its notes; a new chord is a new wash; a stabbed chord is a hard-edged block. The more a chord clashes, the more layers it has and the greyer it goes.
- **Melody: one continuous line,** as if drawn by one loaded brush. Notes that join are one curve; short notes are a dotted line along the same path; a leap starts a new stroke.
- **Harmony: a companion line** beside the melody, thinner and lighter, hatched to it where the two run close (more densely the rougher they sound together), a few links every so far along the line, so a harmony that shadows its tune note for note is not much busier than the tune. A track marked as a harmony is painted this way.
- **Vocals: a luminous ribbon** of fine strands. Breath splays the strands, loudness makes them glow, a vibrato ripples them. On a dark ground it is laid on as light.
- **Other: fog,** edge-less haze that drifts (pads, ambience, the room).

They lie in a fixed order, as a painter would lay them: fog and washes underneath, the bass and the lines in the middle, the voice's glaze and the drums on top. A wash that dries after a spatter of drops goes in under the drops.

**The marks answer the sound.** How loud is how big and how heavily loaded. How hard a sound starts is how hard its edge is and whether it throws paint; a sound that swells in has a feathered edge. How long it rings is how long its tail is. How noisy it is is how broken and splayed the mark is, and how far its drops fly. How bright it is is how light its colour. A bend is a slope and a vibrato a ripple. The app knows every note and hit it plays before it is heard, so the marks of your own song land with their sound.

**Layouts** (**Layout**: **Auto**, or one of six). Every layout keeps a sound's height (or, in the round ones, its distance from the middle) for its frequency, so a picture can always be read. What changes is how time is spent:

- **〰 River:** the canvas itself flows slowly to the left and the newest paint is laid in at the right-hand edge and carried off to the left, so the whole width is painted; nothing sweeps across it, and the lines of the music meander a little as a river does.
- **≋ Field:** each part is carried by a current of its own across the whole canvas, back and forth, bent by a slow swirl, so the parts cross and weave over each other.
- **◎ Rings:** time goes round, frequency goes out from the middle; one turn every few bars, so the downbeats line up on spokes and a repeated groove draws the same rosette.
- **✿ Bloom:** each section opens like a flower from its own place on the canvas, turning a little as it grows, so a held note curls out like a petal.
- **☰ Lanes:** each part in a band of its own (the bass low, the chords in the middle, the melody above them, the drums at the top), flowing left like the river.
- **→ Sweep:** left to right across a page of bars, page after page, with the bar numbers and the guide: the way to read a song bar by bar.

On **Auto** the music chooses, from its vibe (below): a steady groove tends to the river or the rings, calm held music to blooms, energetic textured music to the field, a dense arrangement of many parts to lanes. It changes its mind only when another layout suits clearly better for a bar or more, and keeps its choice once it knows the song. When the layout changes, what was painted before sinks back for a few seconds under the new one, so the first picture stays as a ghost rather than a muddle.

**As full as the music.** The canvas fills in proportion to how much is playing, how loud: two quiet parts leave most of it bare ground (about a fifth covered); six loud ones cover about two thirds; some ground is always left. No part can cover the canvas on its own: a busy hi-hat gets its share and no more. When the music gets quieter nothing new is added, and paint that is more than the music now calls for sinks slowly back into the ground, so a long song never turns to mud.

**The vibe.** About seventy numbers are measured from the music as a whole (the shape of its sound, its harmony and key, its rhythm and groove, its dynamics, how its notes are held or clipped, and which parts play and how much), the way music-similarity research measures songs, and read against fixed reference figures. They set how wet the paint runs, how loose the hand is, how fast the currents move, how many marks a sound leaves, how long the strokes are, which way round the palette the parts are cast and which layout Auto chooses. The same song always gets the same vibe; a song with similar parts gets a similar one, whatever its key, tempo or mix, and so a similar painting, while every mark, which follows its own notes, is new. Write a song inspired by a favourite, run both through the canvas, and you can see how close the atmosphere is.

**Songs from files are split into layers** on your own computer, as soon as they are opened, well ahead of where they play: what is sudden is the drums, what is steady and low the bass, what is steady, in the middle of the stereo picture and does not repeat the voice, and the rest is shared out into chords, melody and fog by how its notes behave. This is plain signal processing, not a trained model, so the layers are approximate (a held synth line can pass for a voice, and a voice singing alone is painted as the other parts are). A split leaves scraps in every layer, and they are cleared away before anything is painted: a drum's ring (a kick's thump goes to the bass, a snare's body to the rest), and tones that flicker for a few hundredths of a second where the split is unsure; an overtone the ear hears as a tone of its own is part of its note, not a mark of its own; and fog comes only from noise that goes on (air, breath, a room), not from a hi-hat. So a song of drums alone is painted as drums, and a song from a file paints much closer to the way the same music written in the app does. Until the split has caught up, the whole mix is heard and its notes shared out. The vibe of the whole song is known by the time it has been heard through once (a first idea of it within a few seconds of opening it). A trained model's stems (from the desktop app) can take over from the split at any point, stretch by stretch as they are made, at their own sample rate, with a six-stem model's guitar and piano painted by hands of their own; wherever they have been heard, the painting is made from them.

**Better layers (Windows app).** In the desktop app the Canvas can hear a song from a file through a neural stem split instead (see *Stems* below), which tells the voice, the drums, the bass and the rest apart far more cleanly. It runs on your own computer; nothing is sent anywhere. The first time, a small **⤓** button beside the song's name shows how much it needs to download, once (about 190 MB: the model and the runtime that runs it); press it and a little bar fills as it downloads (press again to stop). After that every song you open is split by itself, from the start, while it plays: a thin line along the bottom of the canvas shows how far the better layers reach, and the painting switches to them wherever they are ready and keeps the quick split everywhere else. A song split before is ready at once. With the six-stem model, **Guitar** and **Piano** get swatches of their own in the row under the canvas. Closing the song, opening another or leaving the page stops the split; recording a take pauses it. Where it cannot run (the web version, a PC missing what it needs) the Canvas simply keeps its quick split; in the desktop app a dim **◇** says why when you point at it.

**How to read it**

- **Up is frequency.** The scale runs from 32 Hz at the bottom to 16 kHz at the top and is marked down the left edge (50, 100, 200, 500, 1k, 2k, 5k, 10k) in the layouts where height is frequency; the four octaves the notes of a song live in get the biggest steps. In the sweep, the canvas is as wide as the section's bars (numbered along the bottom). For a song from a file, **Across** sets how much of it one crossing of the canvas holds (8, 15 or 30 seconds, or all of it).
- **Every tone holds a brush to the canvas for exactly as long as it sounds.** A staccato note is over before the brush has moved, so it leaves a **dab**; a held note leaves a **stroke** as long as itself; a plucked note that dies away leaves a head and a thinning tail.
- **Colour follows harmony.** The notes of the key share the palette out between them in the order of the circle of fifths, so a chord is a family of neighbouring colours; each part leans to a colour of its own within the palette. For the app's own song the key is the song's; for a song from a file it is heard.
- **Down the right edge in the sweep (the app's own song): what the mix covers,** band by band, coloured by how much of it is chords, melody and drums (every track of each kind). The **Hz** button puts the guide away and brings it back.

**How it is painted**

- **It listens to the sound itself:** the whole mix as it leaves the mixer (for the mood, the key and the vibe), and every track through its own fader, each with an ear and a hand of its own: every drums track on its own desk, every chords and melody track on whatever it is played on (the strings, the piano or the synth: a held pad is painted as a held wash, an arpeggio as quick notes, a drone as a long held line low down), and a track marked as a harmony in the harmony's paint (a swap written instead of the chords is the chords now heard, and is painted as them). A muted track (or one left out by a solo, or with its fader all the way down) gets no ear and is not painted, from the moment it is muted, held notes included. The ears take turns on the audio thread so they never hold up the sound; there are never more than six, and beyond that tracks of one kind share one, the drums first and two melodies last (so two lines never become one). For tracks whose notes the app plays, the ear only listens for the texture (how loud, how bright, how noisy), every other hundredth of a second, and the notes themselves come exactly, a little ahead: with seven such tracks the ears take about a tenth of the audio thread's time. A song from a file is heard layer by layer in the background, ahead of playing, so each mark lands with its sound. How much of the canvas is covered is read back from the graphics card without making it wait.
- **The paint is wet.** The painting is simulated on the graphics card, point by point, sixty times a second: paint flows from where it stands deep to where it is shallow, is carried the way the brush pushed it, bleeds into wet paint beside it, and dries from its edges in; as it dries its pigment settles, more in the hollows of the surface than on its ridges. The edge of a wash holds, as water does on dry paper, unless it is so full that it spills: a bloom, or (with **Drips** on) a run down the canvas.
- **Colours mix as pigments do, not as lights.** Blue into yellow goes green, not grey. Only the voice's glaze is laid on as light, which brightens what is under it.
- **A brush is a brush.** Its hairs leave ridges, deeper the drier the paint; it carries only so much and runs dry as it goes. Its tip follows where it is sent the way a real one does, a little late and with a little overshoot, and the hand is never quite steady. A line (melody, harmony, bass, voice) glides from one note to the next, as gently as the time between them allows, rather than stepping up and down like a stair.
- **The mood of the music sets the manner** (**Paint: Auto**): held, quiet music runs wet and blends; loud, clipped, noisy music goes on thick, dry and loose, with more spatter. **Wash** and **Thick** hold it to one or the other.
- **Old paint** can stay, or sink back into the canvas smoothly as the music goes on (Slow, Medium, Fast), so the newest is the clearest.
- **36 colour schemes** in five families (below), three **surfaces** (canvas, linen, rough paper), **Save picture** (a PNG with the surface in it) and **⤢** to fill the screen.

**What it does not show.** Where a sound is from left to right in the stereo field. Notes less than about a semitone apart low down (below 200 Hz the ear's long look cannot separate them). Which instrument a tone came from, beyond its part. In a song from a file, the parts are only as good as the split (see above). The recorded vocal tracks of the Windows app while they are being recorded (they play through the recording engine, which the canvas cannot hear); once recorded they are painted, and an exported WAV opened here paints everything.

### Stems: a song split into its layers (Windows app)

The Windows app can split a song you open from a file into its **stems**, on your own computer:
**vocals, drums, bass** and **other** (everything else), or, with the six-stem model, **guitar** and
**piano** as well (the piano stem is rough). Each layer can then be painted from its own sound
instead of from the whole mix.

- **What does it.** Demucs v4, a neural network made by Meta's research lab and released as open
  source (MIT licence), run by Microsoft's ONNX Runtime. It is among the best free separators there
  are, but stems are never studio-clean: expect a little bleed (a touch of cymbal in the vocals, a
  little voice in "other"). For seeing what each layer does, that is plenty.
- **Downloaded once, when you ask.** The model is not in the installer. The first time, the app
  fetches ONNX Runtime (about 25 MB, from Microsoft's own package) and the model (about 166 MB for
  four stems, 136 MB for six; builds of the same network range from about 80 to 320 MB), checks each
  against its fingerprint (SHA-256) and keeps them. It only ever downloads from its publishers'
  own addresses, written into the app, over secure connections. A download that is interrupted
  carries on where it stopped. If it fails, or you never ask, nothing else changes.
- **One thing Windows may need.** ONNX Runtime needs Microsoft's *Visual C++ Redistributable*
  (x64), from 2024 or later. Most PCs have it; if yours does not, the app says so before it
  downloads anything. Install the latest one from Microsoft and try again.
- **How long it takes.** On an ordinary 4-core laptop processor, roughly as long as the song itself
  (somewhere between half and the whole of its length); with a graphics card Windows can use for it
  (DirectML, which most recent PCs have), several times faster. The song is worked through from the
  start, 7.8 seconds at a time, and every stem can be used up to where the work has got, so the
  painting can start long before the whole song is done.
- **It keeps out of the music's way.** It uses at most half of the processor, at low priority, and
  stops completely while you record a take (it does not even start until the take is over). If
  the graphics card will not do it, or gives back nonsense, the processor takes over; if neither
  can, the app tells you why and the Canvas carries on with its quick splits.
- **Kept for next time.** Stems are kept in the app's cache folder (32-bit WAV, about 90 MB per stem
  for a four-minute song), so the same song is ready at once the next time, whatever its file is
  called. When they take more than about 4 GB, the songs split longest ago make room.
- **Files it reads:** WAV, MP3, FLAC, OGG (Vorbis) and M4A (AAC or Apple Lossless), up to about
  three hours long at 48 kHz. Not Opus.
- **Only in the desktop app.** The web version and the phone app cannot run it; there (and until
  the model is downloaded) the Canvas uses its quick splits, approximate layers worked out from the
  sound as it plays.
- The stems come from your own file and stay on your computer; use them to study and paint. Your
  rights in the recording are what they were before. Licences and credits: `NOTICE.md`.

- **The shape book** keeps about one in six of the generated voicings. A shape has to be holdable (stretch, finger order, muted strings) and it is then filed under the families guitar teaching uses: open chords, the two barre shapes, triads on neighbouring strings, four-string shapes and shell voicings. Default shapes, Arrange and the ‹ › steppers only ever use these.
- **Voicings** are found by scanning 4-fret windows up the neck (plus open strings), trying every combination of chord tones per string, then keeping only shapes with all required tones, at most one muted string in the middle, and a feasible fingering. Each is scored for difficulty (fingers, barres, stretch, mutes) and usefulness (root position, fullness, low-end mud).
- **Suggestions** score every candidate chord by root motion (falling 5ths, steps, 3rds, chromatic slides), shared notes, voice-leading distance and resolution of tension, then group them by character.
- **Progression voicings** use dynamic programming to minimise total difficulty plus hand movement and top-note jumps across the whole progression.

- **The harp** is not a recording. Each note is a sum of fading sine waves, one per overtone, shaped by what acoustics research says makes a harp sound like a harp: harpists pluck a third to two fifths of the way along the string, which removes whole families of overtones (pluck the exact middle and only the odd ones are left); the soft finger pad rounds the attack; gut and nylon lose their high overtones quickly (a quality factor near 1000 that falls above 2–3 kHz), so the tail is pure; nothing damps the string; each overtone swings two ways at slightly different pitches, which gives the slow shimmer of a real string; a concert harp's soundboard radiates most around 148, 166, 228 and 335 Hz and little below, and answers each pluck with a soft knock; and the other strings hum along. A harder pluck is brighter as well as louder. Notes are in tune to within a few cents and all equally loud.
- **Sections, each with its own tracks and settings.** A section stores everything about itself: its chords and its melody, how its first tracks sound there (style and feel, the chord harp, the melody harp, the drum kit and rhythm), its instruments by track id, and its added tracks, each as its part of the section (`layers`, keyed by track id): what it holds there and how it sounds there. A track is in a section exactly when the section has a part for it, so there is nothing to keep in step, and a track (a row of the Track page: its name, its place and its mixer strip) that is in no section any more leaves the song. The page holds a live copy of the section on screen, written back whenever another section is chosen, the song plays, is saved or exported. Letters are never stored: they are worked out from the playing order each time. Arrange (where on the neck a guitar track's chords sit) is part of how a chords track sounds, so it is kept the same way: the first chords track's in the section, an added one's in its part. A harmony track's record of each section says which option it was written with there, and only where that option plays instead of the chords are they left out (`insteadIn`). Songs saved by older versions are moved over once when they are opened (`src/song/migrate.ts`): every section gets exactly what it played (sounds that followed the first section resolved, the one-sound-per-track version's sounds copied into every section, and with no version a section's kit over the page's, as it played), an added track stays in the sections where it has music, the song's one Arrange is copied to every chords track in every section, and a harmony whose chords had been unmuted by hand is marked as playing along with them, so both are still heard. An old section clipboard is moved over once and marked so.
- **A voice for each way a track is played.** Every chords and melody track plays through a voice of its own (`trackvoice.ts`): its instrument (the strings, a piano or the synth), its effects chain, its fader. A section that plays a track another way (another instrument, another sound) has a voice of its own for it, and sections that play it the same way share one, so a section's notes are simply booked on its own voice and the sound changes exactly where the section starts; a voice whose notes are over rests until it plays again. Every drums track has a drum desk of its own. All of them dip under the kick when a drums track's Pump is up. The live player, the WAV and the recording backing all play the same notes on the same voices; for a WAV the pianos load exactly the recordings the song needs at its sample rate first.
- **Theory audit.** A test walks every scale in all twelve keys and checks three things: every chord the app offers on a note uses only notes of the scale; every one of the 56 chord types that does fit is offered; and each chord tone is spelled with the scale's own note name (in C major the ♭5 of F is B, not C♭: an error this audit found and fixed). The chords of major, natural, harmonic and melodic minor are compared with the textbook lists, and every degree of the seven modes has a named triad, seventh and ninth chord.
- **The instruments** share one engine. A plucked string is a sum of fading overtones shaped by where it is plucked, what plucks it, the string's material and the body it sits on; each of the 18 string instruments is a set of those numbers (a banjo's drum skin makes it short and twangy; a dulcimer and a mandolin have two strings to a note, a few cents apart; the bray harp's buzz is a slow thinning-out of overtones; harmonics sound an octave up and almost pure). The four bells are bars, whose overtones are not whole multiples of the note.
- **Playing styles** are written the way teachers write them: one symbol per step of the bar (D down, U up, X muted, p-i-m-a for the picking fingers, and so on). Down-strums cross the strings in 20 to 35 ms, low string loudest; up-strums catch only the top four and are softer; muted strums are cut after 45 ms; a rolled harp chord climbs more slowly and swells to the top. The same notes go to the MIDI file.
- **The harp's effects** are wired like a pedalboard: compressor, saturation, tone, phaser, tape wobble, chorus, tremolo, then echo and reverb. The chorus is two delayed copies drifting opposite ways, one in each speaker; the phaser is four all-pass stages swept between about 360 Hz and 2 kHz; the echo's repeats bounce from side to side and get duller each time; the reverb starts 18 ms late so the pluck stays clear. Shimmer is done without a pitch shifter: an octave-up copy of each string swells in and is sent only to the reverb. Every knob is loudness-compensated from measurements of the finished output.

- **Saturation** (drums and harp) is the bend analogue gear puts in a signal when it is pushed, plus the tone shaping the real machine does around that bend. Each colour was tuned by measuring its overtones on a test note:
  - *Tape* follows the magnetisation curve of tape (a Langevin function), which adds almost only the third overtone. Treble is boosted going in and cut coming out, so the highs saturate first and come out smoother, and the lows get a small lift like a tape head's.
  - *Tube* is a valve biased off-centre: one half of the wave rounds off sooner, which makes the second overtone, an octave above the note. It stays more than 10 dB above the others up to three quarters of the knob; at full the odd overtones overtake it and it gets an edge.
  - *Console* is a pure cubic bend with the bass boosted going in and cut coming out, as transformer iron saturates on low notes first: at half, a 60 Hz note has about three times the distortion of a 1 kHz one.
  - *Crunch* and *Fuzz* pass the bass round the bend and distort only what is above it, the way a bass-preserving overdrive does. *Clip* is a straight line with a knee near the top: ordinary hits pass untouched, peaks are shaved.
  - On every colour the overtones grow as they do on the real thing: about 1 to 2% at a quarter turn (colour you feel more than hear), 3 to 6% at half, well into distortion at full.
  - The bend runs four times oversampled so it does not alias, which in a browser delays it by about 4 ms. The clean path and the bass path go through the same stage unbent, so the three stay exactly in step and Blend never sounds hollow. The harp's chords always send their lowest notes round the bend, so they stay clear instead of turning to mud.
  - Each colour's loudness was measured on drum loops and on strummed chords at every quarter of the knob and is taken back out: turning it up changes the tone, not the volume.

- **Drum sounds** come from one general drum synthesizer, built from how the classic machines and the best drum synths work (published circuit studies of the classic machines, open-source drum-synth code, physically informed shaker models and synthesis tutorials; credited in `NOTICE.md`). Each sound is a short recipe of layers: oscillators with a fast pitch drop and a slow sag; a pulse ringing a resonant filter (the bridged-T way of the classic machines); several drum-head modes at the ratios of a real membrane; a bank of square waves through band-pass filters for hats, cymbals and cowbell; noise that is white, pink or clocked, with clap bursts, gated tails and snare wires that follow the head; beads and jingles colliding for shakers and tambourines; and dozens of decaying partials for cymbals and bells. Each sound is rendered in three slightly different takes that are played in turn, and every sound is brought to the same loudness (over 40 ms, 300 ms and, for kicks, a second), so swapping one never upsets the mix.
- **Playing without crackles.** The browser works out all of the app's sound on one thread, however many cores the computer has, so the app keeps that work small and gives it room: the sound runs with the browser's larger buffer (about 20 ms rather than 10, which absorbs a busy moment; songs are booked a quarter of a second ahead, so nothing waits); an effect at 0 is not fed at all (an echo once its repeats have died away), a track's saturation at Drive 0 runs only its clean path, a synth that has gone quiet is let go of until its next note, and a stopped track rests until it plays again; tracks with the same kind and size of reverb share one room (each sends to it through its own level, fade and fader, so it sounds the same), and a room that changes rings out instead of being cut; the whole mix passes a safety stage with no look-ahead (the same in a WAV and in the backing you sing over): everything under −1 dBFS passes bit for bit; the tip of a peak over it (a drum hit on a full mix) is rounded off smoothly toward −0.2 dBFS, leaving the level alone; a sound held over it is brought down to it over about 20 ms; anything more than 1 dB over full scale is held down at once. Everything stops with a short fade (takes, the noise bed, a piano's release noise). When the page is held up, a note or drum hit whose moment has passed starts as far into itself as it is late, faded in over 4 ms so it never starts with a click (only the latest hit of each drum; one whose sound is already over is left out), so nothing piles up; and around a page switch the song is booked a second ahead instead of a quarter, so a page that takes a moment to open leaves nothing late.
- **Drum effects** are a small mixing desk of Web Audio nodes: each drum has its own level, pan, and reverb and echo sends; the kit then passes through the saturation above, a tape stage (a short delay whose length drifts), tone, a compressor and a soft ceiling that adds no delay. The reverbs are generated stereo impulse responses (a spring is an impulse sent round a loop of 60 all-pass filters; the gated room is a flat burst that stops dead). The sampler modes hold samples at a lower rate without filtering and quantise them, as the old sampling drum machines did. The noise beds (vinyl crackle, tape hiss, room tone, hum) are rendered once as seamless loops. Saturation, compression and tone are loudness-compensated from measurements of the finished output. The drum grid re-locks to every chord change, so the beat and the chords cannot drift apart.

- **The piano** is a real grand, recorded: every third key (each one a minor third from the next) struck at five strengths, from a whisper to full force, so each note is played by the recording nearest it, moved at most a semitone or so and tuned to the piano's own slightly stretched scale (the bass a little flat, the treble a little sharp, as tuners leave a real one). A harder touch picks a harder-struck recording, which is brighter as well as louder; played a little softer than a recording was made, it is darkened toward the next softer one, so the tone brightens smoothly as you play harder rather than in five steps (mixing the two recordings instead makes some overtones cancel, which sounds hollow). Letting a key go drops the damper: the note stops in a fraction of a second (longer in the bass, where the strings are heavy), with the soft thud and ring of the felt landing; the top keys have no dampers and ring on, as on a real piano. The sustain pedal holds everything let go until it lifts. Striking a key again stops its previous sound, and when more than 64 notes ring at once the oldest give way with a short fade. The recordings download once (about 19 MB, in the background: the first 4 MB are enough to play every key, middle octaves first) and stay on the device, so the installed app plays them offline.
- **The electric piano** is a classic tine piano: 11 recorded keys at three strengths, each a short attack followed by a loop of its steady tone, faded as a struck tine fades and stopped quickly by the damper. It has a treble control and a tremolo or autopan, and is under 1 MB. Each recording is retuned from its own loop, so every key is within a cent of true pitch. Both keyboards play as loud as the guitar does the same notes, so a track can switch between them without a jump in level.

Everything runs locally in your browser. Your ideas are saved in the browser's local storage. When the app is opened as a claude.ai artifact, each saved idea is also written to your private area of the artifact's database, so the same library shows up on your other devices.

### Harmony for a new track, and chords on a piano

A new melody or chords track can be written to fit one that is already there (`src/theory/harmonize.ts`). The options are the ones arrangers and harmony teaching use, each a rule you could check by hand. Harmonize offers, best first: for a melody line from a tune, *High harmony*, *Low harmony*, *Counter-melody*, *Long notes*, *Answer*; from chords, *Long notes*, *Counter-melody*, *Chord notes*; for chords from chords, *Richer*, *Sus*, *Darker* (or *Brighter*), *Relative*, *Pedal bass*, *Lead-ins*; from a tune, *Best fit* first, then the same. An option that would write nothing in the song is not offered. The older options below (*Sixth below*, *Octave*, *Drone*, *Pad*, *Higher voicing*, *Shell*, *Smooth inversions*, *Passing dim*, *Tritone swap*) are kept only so a track written with one can still be written again with it:

- **A new melody from a melody.** *High harmony* (a third above), *Low harmony* (a third below) and *Sixth below* move every note the same number of steps of the key's scale, so a third is three or four semitones, whichever the key says. On a strong beat (the downbeat, beat 3 of 4/4, the second beat of 6/8, the start of each group in 5/4 or 7/4) or a held note, the harmony note must sit in the chord underneath, so it bends to the nearest chord note when the plain third would rub; quick notes between may pass through. Parallel fifths and octaves are never written. *Chord notes* sings the nearest chord note under the tune. *Counter-melody* is a line of its own: chord notes (3rds and 7ths first; a 9th or 6th when the tune itself sits on a colour note) on the strong beats and chord changes, consonant with the tune there, moving against it, a leap followed by a step back, passing notes filling the gaps where the tune holds (the rules of species counterpoint). *Answer* plays only where the tune breathes, echoing as many of its last notes as fit a step away (or a run of chord notes), in triplets in 6/8, 9/8 and 12/8. *Octave* doubles it. *Drone* holds home or its fifth. *Long notes* walks the 3rd or 7th of each chord, one held note each, moving as little as it can.
- **A new melody from chords.** *Chord notes* (a walk through each chord), *Counter-melody*, *Long notes* and *Drone* also work from a chords track alone.
- **A new chords track.** Layers that sound together with the first (written from a melody track, they sit on the song's chords in that section): *Pad* (the same chords held high), *Higher voicing*, *Shell* (root, 3rd and 7th), *Smooth inversions* (the bass moving by step), *Richer* (7ths, 9ths and 6ths that fit the key and the chord's job, never a note a semitone above a chord note), *Sus* and *Pedal bass*. Re-harmonisations to play instead (the chords they change are not heard in the sections they are written in; the mixer is left alone): *Darker* or *Brighter* (iv, ♭VI, ♭VII from the parallel minor; a major IV or V in minor), *Relative* (each chord's relative, sharing two notes: heard with the chords it would only add a 6th or 7th), *Lead-ins* (the dominant 7th of each next chord), *Passing dim* and *Tritone swap* (a chord slipped in always starts on a beat: 2+2 in 4/4, 2+1 in 3/4, 3+2 in 5/4). Every swap is checked against the melody's strong notes, so it never clashes with the tune. *Best fit* chooses chords for a melody: it weighs how long and how strongly each note sounds over each chord of the key and searches for the best row of chords (borrowed chords only where the tune asks for one).
- The same input always gives the same result, and the harmony keeps each section's chord lengths and time signature.

**Chords on a piano.** `src/keys/voicing.ts` chooses which keys to press: close, open, drop 2, shell or spread shapes, inside a register (pad, middle or low), at most eight keys, with nothing muddy down low (no seconds under E3, nothing closer than a fifth under B2, only octaves under E2), no semitone between the two top or the two bottom keys, never a 9th, 11th or 13th in the bass, and each hand within reach. Across a progression it searches every candidate shape of every chord together and picks the row where the hands move least: held notes count in its favour, big leaps in the top voice and parallel fifths against it. The keyboard diagram (`KeyboardDiagram.tsx`) shows a voicing at the size of a guitar chord box: pressed keys in the same colours as the guitar dots (the root ringed), their part in the chord or their names underneath, and which hand plays what when a shape needs both.

Sources: open music-theory teaching (counterpoint, applied chords, modal mixture) and several open-source harmony, voicing and keyboard libraries, whose rules were re-implemented and whose numbers and small routines were ported; each is credited with its licence in `NOTICE.md`.

### Where the harp, the styles and the effects come from

The numbers and patterns were collected from published acoustics research (harp plucking, the concert harp's soundboard and sympathetic strings, string damping and plucked-string synthesis), teaching material (strumming, fingerpicking, clave and tresillo rhythms, banjo rolls, classical right-hand studies, harp accompaniment guides) and audio-engineering references (flanging and phasing, reverb types, shimmer and echo, circuit analyses of classic overdrive, chorus and phaser pedals, the Web Audio API documentation). The session that gathered them could read search-result excerpts of these pages, not always the full text, so treat the list in `NOTICE.md` as where to look rather than as a guarantee that every figure was checked against the original.

### Where the synth comes from

The panel's layout and the meaning of its sliders follow the parameter tables of a compact virtual-analogue keyboard synth, as transcribed in open-source editors for it; the curves (hertz, seconds), the effects' details and the preset recipes are this app's own, grounded in decoded factory sounds of open-source synths. The engine follows published algorithms and open-source code: band-limited steps after the PolyBLEP idea; a supersaw after published measurements of the original; a zero-delay-feedback ladder filter run at twice the sample rate with half-band filters; analogue-style envelopes, glide and voice stealing; slow drift; FM as phase modulation; antiderivative anti-aliased distortion; a small feedback-delay-network reverb. Every knob glides, nothing clicks, and every preset is checked for pitch, folded-back noise, loudness and clipping by the tests. Each source is credited with its licence in `NOTICE.md`.

### Where the piano comes from

The grand piano's recordings are a reduced set of freely licensed concert-grand recordings (CC BY 3.0); the electric piano is a port of an open-source electric-piano instrument (MIT), its recordings included. ### Where the canvas's way of turning sound into paint comes from

What it rests on, and how sure each part is:

- **Well supported by research:** higher pitch is seen as higher up, smaller and lighter; louder as bigger and heavier; harsh, rough or bright sounds as broken, angular marks; and the mood of a whole piece as colour.
- **Choices of this app, with no study behind them:** a staccato note as a dab and a held one as a stroke (it follows from holding the brush down exactly as long as the tone lasts), noise as spatter, and calm music as wet paint.
- **From open-source code** (see `NOTICE.md`): how a brush tip lags and overshoots, how touches overlap along a stroke, how spatter sizes are spread, how a brush runs dry, how wet paint is damped until it stops, and how pigments mix.
- **The layers, the layouts, the budget and the vibe** follow `research_notes/Synths harmony stems and vibe painting/vibe_painting.md` and `stems.md`. The measures of a song's vibe (timbre, harmony, rhythm, dynamics) are those music-similarity research uses; turning them into a painting smoothly, so that similar music looks similar, and the paint each part is given, are this app's own design, as are the coverage figures (a fifth for two quiet parts, two thirds for six loud ones). Splitting a file into layers uses published signal-processing methods (median-filter harmonic/percussive separation, and REPET-SIM for the voice), not a trained model; a model's stems (from the desktop app, later) can take their place. Marks are laid a hundredth of a second or so before their sound: broadcast practice finds pictures that lead the sound far easier to accept than pictures that lag it.

The full research, with every source read and its licence, is `reports/Realistic paint and sound painting.md`.
